## Two numbers, one of them noisy

Every submission gives you two report cards that often disagree. This chapter is about deciding
which one to trust, and the answer comes from counting how many rows stand behind each grade.

You get a CV score computed on all your training rows and a public leaderboard score computed on a
slice of the test set you cannot see. The whole discipline of competition validation is deciding
**which one to believe** — and the answer is almost always the one with more rows behind it.

The organiser's hidden test set is usually cut in two. The **public leaderboard** (public LB) is
scored on a small slice and shown to you during the competition. The **private leaderboard** is
scored on the rest and revealed only at the end; it decides the final ranking. Your **CV score**
is your own estimate from cross-validation on the training data (ch. 1).

> **Picture it:** you want to know how good a basketball player is. One coach watched 20,000
> shots. Another watched 2,000 shots from the bleachers in fog. Both report a shooting
> percentage. You trust the first coach — not because the second is lying, but because 2,000
> foggy shots cannot tell a 86.0% shooter from an 86.5% one.

The simulation below gives twelve models a known **true skill** (the AUC each would get on
infinite data) and then shows each one through three noisy windows: CV (small noise), public LB on
2,000 rows (large noise), and private LB on 18,000 rows (medium noise).


## The only diagnostic that matters: does CV *rank* like the LB?

You do not need CV and LB to give the same number. You need them to put your models in the same
order, because "which file do I submit?" is an ordering question.

An offset between CV and LB is harmless. A scrambled ordering is fatal, because your submission
choice is a ranking decision.

Two ways to measure agreement: **Pearson correlation** checks whether the numbers line up on a
straight line; **rank correlation** (Spearman) checks only whether the order matches.

```python
def agreement(a, b):
    pearson = float(np.corrcoef(models[a], models[b])[0, 1])
    spearman = float(stats.spearmanr(models[a], models[b]).statistic)
    return pearson, spearman

for pair in (("cv", "private_lb"), ("public_lb", "private_lb"), ("cv", "public_lb")):
    p, s = agreement(*pair)
    print(f"{pair[0]:10} vs {pair[1]:11} | pearson {p:+.3f} | rank corr {s:+.3f}")

print(f"\nmean |CV - public LB| offset: {np.abs(models['cv'] - models['public_lb']).mean():.4f}")
print(f"CV picks     : {models['cv'].idxmax()} (true {models.loc[models['cv'].idxmax(), 'true']:.4f})")
print(f"public LB picks: {models['public_lb'].idxmax()} "
      f"(true {models.loc[models['public_lb'].idxmax(), 'true']:.4f})")
```

The first line is the one that matters: CV versus the private LB has a rank correlation of +0.790,
so CV orders models much like the final judge. The public LB versus the private LB manages only
+0.322. Follow each selection rule: CV picks m11, whose true skill (0.8750) is the best of all
twelve; the public LB picks m02, with true skill 0.8620 — tenth of twelve.

Selecting by the public leaderboard picks a worse model here, and this is the ordinary case rather
than bad luck: a 2,000-row public split cannot resolve differences of a few thousandths, so its
ranking is mostly noise.

> **Watch out:** "my CV is 0.01 above my LB" is not a problem by itself. Look at the mean offset
> line: 0.0101 on average, and the CV still picked the best model. Chasing the offset instead of
> the ranking is a classic waste of a week.

### How many rows does a leaderboard need?

The fewer rows a leaderboard is scored on, the more its number wobbles. A formula tells you how
much, before you submit anything.

For AUC the standard error can be approximated from the sample size and the positive rate. The
useful form is: **what difference can this leaderboard actually resolve?**

The **standard error** (SE) is the typical size of the wobble: if you re-drew the public rows many
times, the AUC would scatter around its true value by about one SE.

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\mathrm{AUC}) \approx
\sqrt{\frac{A(1-A) + (n_1-1)(Q_1 - A^2) + (n_0-1)(Q_2 - A^2)}{n_0 n_1}}
$$

with $Q_1 = A/(2-A)$ and $Q_2 = 2A^2/(1+A)$ (Hanley and McNeil).

> **In plain words:**
> - $A$ is the AUC you expect (here 0.86).
> - $n_1$ is the number of positive rows and $n_0$ the number of negative rows.
> - $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are helper terms that depend only on $A$.
> - The whole thing is "spread of the AUC estimate", and it shrinks as $n_0 n_1$ in the
>   denominator grows — more rows of both classes, less wobble.

```python
def auc_se(auc, n_pos, n_neg):
    q1 = auc / (2 - auc)
    q2 = 2 * auc ** 2 / (1 + auc)
    var = (auc * (1 - auc) + (n_pos - 1) * (q1 - auc ** 2)
           + (n_neg - 1) * (q2 - auc ** 2)) / (n_pos * n_neg)
    return float(np.sqrt(var))

print(f"{'LB rows':>9} {'positives':>10} {'SE(AUC)':>9} {'resolves a gap of':>19}")
for rows in (500, 2_000, 10_000, 50_000):
    pos = int(rows * 0.2)
    se = auc_se(0.86, pos, rows - pos)
    print(f"{rows:>9,} {pos:>10,} {se:>9.4f} {1.96 * se * np.sqrt(2):>19.4f}")
```

```output
  LB rows  positives   SE(AUC)   resolves a gap of
      500        100    0.0244              0.0676
    2,000        400    0.0122              0.0338
   10,000      2,000    0.0054              0.0151
   50,000     10,000    0.0024              0.0068
```

Read the 2,000-row line: the SE is 0.0122, and two models must differ by 0.0338 before the
leaderboard can tell them apart. Even at 50,000 rows the board only resolves gaps of 0.0068. The
"resolves" column is $1.96 \times \mathrm{SE} \times \sqrt{2}$: 1.96 for 95% confidence, and
$\sqrt{2}$ because comparing two models adds two wobbles together.

The last column is the gap two models must differ by before the leaderboard can tell them apart
with 95% confidence. On a 2,000-row public split that is roughly 0.03 AUC — larger than the entire
spread of the top hundred teams in most competitions. **The public leaderboard is a coin flip
dressed as feedback.**

## When CV and LB genuinely disagree

Sometimes the disagreement is not noise: your CV is grading a different exam from the one the
organiser set. The pattern of the disagreement tells you which case you are in.

A constant offset is fine. Diverging *ranks* mean your CV is answering a different question from
the leaderboard, and there are only a few reasons why.

The report below looks at two things: the **offset** (LB minus CV, per model) and how much that
offset varies, plus the rank correlation. Three simulated cases show the three verdicts.

```python
def cv_lb_report(cv_scores, lb_scores, names):
    d = np.asarray(lb_scores) - np.asarray(cv_scores)
    rank_corr = float(stats.spearmanr(cv_scores, lb_scores).statistic)
    print(f"mean offset {d.mean():+.4f} | offset std {d.std(ddof=1):.4f} | "
          f"rank corr {rank_corr:+.3f}")
    if abs(d.mean()) > 3 * d.std(ddof=1) and rank_corr > 0.8:
        print("  -> constant shift, ranks intact: TRUST CV, ignore the offset")
    elif rank_corr > 0.8:
        print("  -> ranks intact: TRUST CV")
    elif d.std(ddof=1) > 0.01:
        print("  -> ranks scrambled and offsets noisy: the LB is too small to read")
    else:
        print("  -> ranks scrambled with tight offsets: your CV scheme is WRONG")

print("case 1: healthy (constant shift)")
cv_lb_report(models["cv"], models["cv"] - 0.02, models.index)
print("\ncase 2: small public LB (what we simulated)")
cv_lb_report(models["cv"], models["public_lb"], models.index)
print("\ncase 3: broken CV scheme (leaky folds inflate CV inconsistently)")
leaky_cv = models["true"] + rng.normal(0, 0.001, len(models)) + rng.uniform(0.010, 0.030, len(models))
cv_lb_report(leaky_cv, models["private_lb"], models.index)
```

```output
case 1: healthy (constant shift)
mean offset -0.0200 | offset std 0.0000 | rank corr +1.000
  -> constant shift, ranks intact: TRUST CV, ignore the offset

case 2: small public LB (what we simulated)
mean offset -0.0010 | offset std 0.0126 | rank corr +0.126
  -> ranks scrambled and offsets noisy: the LB is too small to read

case 3: broken CV scheme (leaky folds inflate CV inconsistently)
mean offset -0.0168 | offset std 0.0074 | rank corr +0.203
  -> ranks scrambled with tight offsets: your CV scheme is WRONG
```

- **Case 1:** every LB score is CV minus 0.02, so the offset std is 0.0000 and the rank
  correlation is +1.000. Trust CV.
- **Case 2:** the offset std is 0.0126 — bigger than the true gaps between models — so the LB is
  too blurry to read.
- **Case 3:** the offsets are tighter (std 0.0074) but the rank correlation is only +0.203. The
  CV numbers are inflated by different amounts for different models.

Case 3 is the dangerous one. The offsets are tight enough to look like a systematic shift, but the
ranks are scrambled, which means the CV is measuring something the test set does not reward — a
leak inside the folds, a split that ignores groups, or preprocessing fitted on everything
(eval-tuning ch. 5).

> **Picture it:** a bathroom scale that always reads 2 kg heavy is still useful for tracking
> your weight. A scale that adds a random 1–3 kg depending on how you stand is not — even though
> its "average error" looks similar.

## Making CV less noisy is cheaper than making the LB bigger

The leaderboard size is fixed by the organiser. Your CV is yours to improve — and only one of the
two obvious knobs makes it steadier.

You cannot add rows to the public leaderboard. You can always reduce the variance of your own
estimate — but only one of the two obvious levers actually does it.

The two levers are **more folds** (k = 10 instead of 5) and **repeats** (run k-fold several times
with different random splits and average). The function models each validation row as adding a
little independent noise.

```python
def cv_noise(n_rows, k, repeats, per_row_sd=0.30):
    """Std of a repeated k-fold mean when each validation row contributes independent noise."""
    per_fold = per_row_sd / np.sqrt(n_rows / k)
    return float(per_fold / np.sqrt(k * repeats))

print(f"{'scheme':>22} {'CV noise (sd)':>15} {'resolves':>10}")
for k, reps in ((5, 1), (10, 1), (5, 3), (10, 5)):
    sd = cv_noise(20_000, k, reps)
    print(f"{f'{k}-fold x {reps}':>22} {sd:>15.5f} {1.96 * sd * np.sqrt(2):>10.5f}")
print(f"{'public LB (2k rows)':>22} {0.0100:>15.5f} {1.96 * 0.0100 * np.sqrt(2):>10.5f}")
```

```output
                scheme   CV noise (sd)   resolves
            5-fold x 1         0.00212    0.00588
           10-fold x 1         0.00212    0.00588
            5-fold x 3         0.00122    0.00339
           10-fold x 5         0.00095    0.00263
   public LB (2k rows)         0.01000    0.02772
```

Look at the first two rows: 5-fold and 10-fold have **identical** noise. That is not a bug in the
model — every scheme scores each of the 20,000 rows exactly once per repeat, so the number of
folds cancels out of the variance entirely. More folds buys you larger training sets (less
pessimistic bias), not a more stable estimate. **Repeats** are what shrink the noise, by a factor
of $\sqrt{R}$.

Check that against the table: 5-fold x 3 has noise 0.00122, which is 0.00212 divided by
$\sqrt{3} \approx 1.73$.

Repeated 10-fold on 20,000 rows resolves differences of 0.0026 against the public leaderboard's
0.0277 — an order of magnitude finer. The cost is compute, which is the cheapest input you have.

> **Watch out:** this toy model treats rows as independent. In real data, folds share most of
> their training rows, so fold scores are correlated and repeated CV is less of a free lunch than
> the table suggests (the solution below corrects for this).

The other lever is **matching the structure** of the test split, which costs nothing at all:

- Test rows are future rows: validate on a time cut, not a random shuffle.
- Test rows are unseen entities: GroupKFold on the entity.
- Test has a different class balance (the organiser says so): stratify to *their* rate, or
  reweight your metric.
- Train and test come from different sources: adversarial validation (ch. 10), then a
  distribution-matched validation subset.

## Using the leaderboard as one extra fold

The public LB is not worthless; it is one more measurement, just a blurry one. Blurry measurements
deserve a small say, not zero say and not the final word.

The public LB is not useless — it is one noisy fold, and it is the only fold drawn from the actual
test distribution. Treat it as evidence with a weight, not as an oracle.

The code mixes the two scores as `(1 - w_lb) * cv + w_lb * lb` for several weights and reports
which model each mix would pick.

```python
def blended_selection(cv, lb, w_lb):
    return (1 - w_lb) * np.asarray(cv) + w_lb * np.asarray(lb)

print(f"{'w_lb':>6} {'picked':>8} {'true skill of pick':>20}")
for w in (0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0):
    combo = blended_selection(models["cv"], models["public_lb"], w)
    pick = int(np.argmax(combo))
    print(f"{w:>6.1f} {models.index[pick]:>8} {models['true'].iloc[pick]:>20.4f}")
print(f"\nbest possible: {models['true'].max():.4f} ({models['true'].idxmax()})")
```

```output
  w_lb   picked   true skill of pick
   0.0      m11               0.8750
   0.2      m09               0.8700
   0.5      m09               0.8700
   0.8      m02               0.8620
   1.0      m02               0.8620

best possible: 0.8750 (m11)
```

With `w_lb` = 0.0 the pick is m11, the true best. At 0.2 and 0.5 it slides to m09 (0.8700), and
from 0.8 up it lands on m02 (0.8620). Every step toward the leaderboard made the pick worse.

Even a 20% leaderboard weight is enough to swap the pick from m11 (true 0.8750) to m09 (0.8700)
here. The right weight follows from the noise levels, not from taste. If CV has standard deviation
$\sigma_{cv}$ and the LB has $\sigma_{lb}$, the minimum-variance combination weights each by its
inverse variance:

$$
w_{lb} = \frac{1/\sigma_{lb}^2}{1/\sigma_{cv}^2 + 1/\sigma_{lb}^2}
$$

> **In plain words:** each source gets a vote proportional to $1/\sigma^2$, "how sharp it is". A
> source with five times the wobble gets $5^2 = 25$ times less vote. The weights then get scaled
> so they add up to 1.

```python
sd_cv, sd_lb = 0.0020, 0.0100
w = (1 / sd_lb ** 2) / (1 / sd_cv ** 2 + 1 / sd_lb ** 2)
print(f"optimal LB weight with sd_cv={sd_cv} and sd_lb={sd_lb}: {w:.3f}")
print(f"i.e. the public leaderboard deserves {w:.1%} of the decision, not 100%")
```

```output
optimal LB weight with sd_cv=0.002 and sd_lb=0.01: 0.038
i.e. the public leaderboard deserves 3.8% of the decision, not 100%
```

With CV noise 0.002 and LB noise 0.01, the leaderboard earns a weight of 0.038. That is the
formula's answer to "how much should the public LB change my mind?": about 4%.

## Recap in plain words

- The score with more rows behind it is the one to believe; usually that is your CV.
- What matters is whether CV and LB **order** your models the same way, not whether their numbers
  match.
- A 2,000-row public split at AUC 0.86 cannot separate models closer than about 0.03.
- Tight offsets with scrambled ranks mean your CV scheme is broken; noisy offsets mean the LB is
  too small.
- More folds do not reduce CV noise; repeats do, by $\sqrt{R}$.
- Weight the LB by inverse variance; here that gives it about 4% of the decision.

## Try it Yourself

`shakeup` replays the final-day decision 400 times with fresh noise and counts how often each rule
lands on one of the three truly best models, and how much true skill it gives up on average.

```python
def shakeup(models, n_trials=400, seed=0, w_lb=0.0):
    """How often does the selection rule pick a top-3 model? How much skill does it lose?"""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    hits, regret = 0, []
    top3 = set(models["true"].nlargest(3).index)
    for _ in range(n_trials):
        cv = models["true"] + r.normal(0, 0.0020, len(models))
        lb = models["true"] + r.normal(0, 0.0100, len(models))
        pick = models.index[int(np.argmax((1 - w_lb) * cv + w_lb * lb))]
        hits += pick in top3
        regret.append(models["true"].max() - models.loc[pick, "true"])
    return hits / n_trials, float(np.mean(regret))

print(f"{'rule':>26} {'picks a top-3 model':>21} {'mean skill lost':>17}")
for name, w in (("CV only", 0.0), ("80% CV / 20% LB", 0.2),
                ("50/50", 0.5), ("public LB only", 1.0)):
    hit, reg = shakeup(models, w_lb=w)
    print(f"{name:>26} {hit:>20.1%} {reg:>17.5f}")
```

```output
                      rule   picks a top-3 model   mean skill lost
                   CV only                99.8%           0.00052
           80% CV / 20% LB                97.5%           0.00100
                     50/50                81.0%           0.00291
            public LB only                59.5%           0.00513
```

Trusting CV alone picks a top-3 model 99.8% of the time and loses only 0.00052 of skill on
average. Trusting the public LB alone drops to 59.5% and loses ten times more skill (0.00513).
Every rule in between sits in between.

Change the noise levels inside `shakeup` to match a competition you know — a large public split,
or a CV you have reason to distrust — and the ranking of the rules moves accordingly. That is the
point: the correct weight is a property of the two noise levels, and both are measurable.

Variation to try: change the LB noise from `0.0100` to `0.0015` (a huge public split) and re-run.
The 50/50 rule should now rival or beat CV only.

```python
def estimate_lb_sd(auc, n_rows, pos_rate):
    return auc_se(auc, int(n_rows * pos_rate), int(n_rows * (1 - pos_rate)))

def estimate_cv_sd(fold_scores):
    """Standard error of the CV mean, with the Nadeau-Bengio inflation for fold overlap."""
    k = len(fold_scores)
    return float(np.std(fold_scores, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(1 / k + 0.25))

def choose_final_two(cands):
    fold_sd = np.mean([estimate_cv_sd(c["folds"]) for c in cands])
    lb_sd = np.mean([estimate_lb_sd(c["public_lb"], c["lb_rows"], c["pos_rate"])
                     for c in cands])
    w_lb = (1 / lb_sd ** 2) / (1 / fold_sd ** 2 + 1 / lb_sd ** 2)
    table = pd.DataFrame([{
        "name": c["name"],
        "cv": round(float(np.mean(c["folds"])), 5),
        "public_lb": round(c["public_lb"], 5),
        "blended": round((1 - w_lb) * float(np.mean(c["folds"])) + w_lb * c["public_lb"], 5),
    } for c in cands]).sort_values("blended", ascending=False)
    print(f"estimated CV sd {fold_sd:.5f} | estimated LB sd {lb_sd:.5f} "
          f"| optimal LB weight {w_lb:.3f}")
    print(table.to_string(index=False))
    picks = table.head(2)["name"].tolist()
    diverse = table.iloc[0]["name"], table.sort_values("cv", ascending=False).iloc[0]["name"]
    print(f"\nfinal two by blended score : {picks}")
    print(f"hedge (blend-best + CV-best): {list(dict.fromkeys(diverse))}")
    return w_lb

r = np.random.default_rng(7)
candidates = []
for i, skill in enumerate([0.8600, 0.8635, 0.8642, 0.8650, 0.8590]):
    folds = list(skill + r.normal(0, 0.004, 5))

w = choose_final_two(candidates)
```

The estimated LB standard deviation (0.01215) comes out roughly six times the CV standard error
(0.00205), so the inverse-variance rule gives the public leaderboard about 3% of the decision
(0.028) — which is another way of saying *ignore it, but not quite*. That number is derived, not
asserted: it falls out of 2,000 rows at a 20% positive rate through the Hanley-McNeil formula, and
it would change if the organiser had published a 50,000-row public split.

Two design choices worth defending. The CV standard error uses the **Nadeau-Bengio inflation**
(eval-tuning ch. 12) rather than the plain standard error of the fold mean, because folds share
training data and the naive estimate is optimistic — using it here would over-weight CV, which
happens to be the safe direction but for the wrong reason. And the second submission is a
**hedge**, not the second-best blend: picking the blend-best and the CV-best gives you two
genuinely different bets when they disagree, and costs nothing when they agree.
