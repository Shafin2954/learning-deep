## The first hour decides the month

Before you train anything, read the rules like a contract. Three answers in that contract decide
whether the next month of work moves your rank at all.

Before a single model, three questions have answers that constrain everything after them: **what
exactly is being scored**, **how could this data leak**, and **what validation scheme reproduces
the leaderboard**. Teams that get these wrong spend weeks improving a number that does not move
their rank.

A few words you will meet on every page of this course:

- The **metric** is the formula the organiser uses to turn your predictions into one score.
- A **leak** is a clue in the training file that gives the answer away but will not be there when
  the model is used for real.
- **CV** (cross-validation) is your home-made exam: you hide part of the training data, predict
  it, and score yourself. The **leaderboard** (LB) is the organiser's exam on their hidden test
  set.

> **Picture it:** you are training for a race whose course is secret. The metric tells you
> whether it is a sprint or a marathon. A leak is a shortcut on your practice track that will
> not exist on race day. Your CV is the practice course; if it is not shaped like the real one,
> faster practice times mean nothing.

The setup below builds one toy competition you will reuse for the whole chapter: 8,000 rows, a
hidden "true" probability for each row, and two models that put the rows in exactly the same
order but report very different probabilities.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 8_000
signal = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
noise_level = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, n)
p_true = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(0.9 * signal - 1.4)))
y = (rng.random(n) < p_true).astype(int)
score_a = np.clip(p_true + rng.normal(0, 0.05, n), 1e-6, 1 - 1e-6)   # calibrated
score_b = np.clip(score_a * 0.45 + 0.02, 1e-6, 1 - 1e-6)             # SAME ordering, squashed
print(f"rows {n} | positive rate {y.mean():.3f}")
print(f"model A mean prediction {score_a.mean():.3f} | model B {score_b.mean():.3f}")
```

```output
rows 8000 | positive rate 0.228
model A mean prediction 0.231 | model B 0.124
```

About 22.8% of rows are positive. Model A's average prediction (0.231) matches that rate, so A is
**calibrated** — when it says 30%, about 30% of those rows really are positive. Model B averages
only 0.124: it ranks rows the same way but systematically under-reports the probability.

Two models, one of them a monotone squashing of the other. They are identical to any rank-based
metric and very different to any probability-based one.

A **monotone** transform keeps order: if row 1 scores higher than row 2 under A, it still scores
higher under B.

## Metric families, and what each one rewards

Metrics come in three families, and the family tells you which kind of work pays. Score both
models with one metric from each family and watch which numbers move.

```python
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, log_loss, average_precision_score, accuracy_score

rows = []
for name, s in (("A calibrated", score_a), ("B same ranking, squashed", score_b)):
    s = np.clip(s, 1e-6, 1 - 1e-6)
    rows.append({
        "model": name,
        "ROC AUC": round(roc_auc_score(y, s), 5),
        "PR AUC": round(average_precision_score(y, s), 5),
        "log loss": round(log_loss(y, s), 5),
        "acc @0.5": round(accuracy_score(y, s > 0.5), 5),
    })
print(pd.DataFrame(rows).to_string(index=False))
```

```output
                   model  ROC AUC  PR AUC  log loss  acc @0.5
            A calibrated  0.71431  0.4322   0.49733   0.77975
B same ranking, squashed  0.71431  0.4322   0.52931   0.77238
```

Look at the columns one at a time. ROC AUC is 0.71431 for both models, and PR AUC is 0.4322 for
both: those metrics only look at the order. Log loss moves from 0.49733 (A) to 0.52931 (B): it
punishes B for wrong probability values even though B's order is perfect. Accuracy at a 0.5 cut
moves too (0.77975 versus 0.77238), because squashing pushes rows across the fixed threshold.

Read that table as a decision tree for your effort:

- **Rank metrics** (ROC AUC, MAP, NDCG) — only the ordering matters. Calibration work is wasted;
  blending by *rank* is safe (ch. 8).
- **Probability metrics** (log loss, Brier) — the values matter. A model that ranks perfectly can
  still lose badly, and calibration is free score (eval-tuning ch. 4).
- **Threshold metrics** (accuracy, F1, quadratic kappa) — the cut point is part of your
  submission. Optimising it on out-of-fold predictions is usually worth more than a better model.

> **Picture it:** a rank metric is a judge who only says who finished first, second, third. A
> probability metric is a judge who also checks your predicted finishing times. A threshold
> metric is a judge who only asks "did you finish under 4 hours?" — so where you draw that line
> matters as much as how fast you run.

The **threshold** is the cut that turns a probability into a yes/no. **F1** is a threshold metric
that balances catching positives (recall) against not crying wolf (precision). Here is what
choosing that cut is worth on model A:

```python
best_thr, best_f1 = None, -1
for thr in np.linspace(0.01, 0.99, 99):
    tp = int(((score_a > thr) & (y == 1)).sum())
    fp = int(((score_a > thr) & (y == 0)).sum())
    fn = int(((score_a <= thr) & (y == 1)).sum())
    f1 = 2 * tp / max(2 * tp + fp + fn, 1)
    if f1 > best_f1:
        best_thr, best_f1 = thr, f1
naive_tp = int(((score_a > 0.5) & (y == 1)).sum())
naive_fp = int(((score_a > 0.5) & (y == 0)).sum())
naive_fn = int(((score_a <= 0.5) & (y == 1)).sum())
naive_f1 = 2 * naive_tp / max(2 * naive_tp + naive_fp + naive_fn, 1)
print(f"F1 at the default 0.5 threshold : {naive_f1:.4f}")
print(f"F1 at the tuned threshold {best_thr:.2f}    : {best_f1:.4f}")
print(f"free gain from one line of code : {best_f1 - naive_f1:+.4f}")
```

```output
F1 at the default 0.5 threshold : 0.2515
F1 at the tuned threshold 0.24    : 0.4650
free gain from one line of code : +0.2135
```

At the default 0.5 cut, F1 is only 0.2515. Sweeping 99 thresholds finds 0.24, where F1 jumps to
0.4650 — a gain of +0.2135 from one loop. The best cut sits near the positive rate (0.228)
because only 22.8% of rows are positive, so a 0.5 cut throws away most of them.

That gain is larger than most feature-engineering ideas will give you, and it costs a loop.

> **Watch out:** tune the threshold on **out-of-fold** predictions (predictions each model made
> on rows it did not train on, ch. 2), never on the same rows you trained on. A threshold tuned
> on training predictions fits the noise and moves on the leaderboard.

## Leak hunting is a checklist, not an instinct

A leak is a clue that sneaked into the data by accident. You find leaks by running the same few
tests every time, not by waiting for a hunch.

A leak is any signal in the training data that will not exist at prediction time. Four patterns
cover most competition leaks, and all four are detectable in minutes.

The four families: **identifier** leaks (an ID or row number carries the answer), **order** leaks
(the position of a row in the file carries it), **duplicate** leaks (the same row appears in train
and test), and **group** leaks (many rows from one entity land on both sides of a split). The
first test is the cheapest: correlate every ID-like column with the target.

> **Picture it:** an exam paper where all the "true" answers happen to be printed on the left
> page and all the "false" ones on the right. You can score 100% without reading a question —
> until the real exam is shuffled.

```python
comp = pd.DataFrame({
    "id": np.arange(n),
    "feature_1": signal + rng.normal(0, 0.4, n),
    "feature_2": rng.normal(0, 1, n),
    "recorded_at": np.sort(rng.integers(0, 10_000, n)),   # rows arrive in time order
    "target": y,
})
# the organiser sorted the file by outcome before shuffling in the negatives (a real, common bug)
comp = comp.sort_values(["target", "id"], kind="stable").reset_index(drop=True)
comp["row_number"] = np.arange(len(comp))

print("correlation of the target with:")
for col in ("id", "row_number", "recorded_at", "feature_1", "feature_2"):
    r = float(np.corrcoef(comp[col], comp["target"])[0, 1])
    flag = "  <- LEAK" if abs(r) > 0.3 and col in ("id", "row_number", "recorded_at") else ""
    print(f"  {col:12} {r:+.4f}{flag}")
```

```output
correlation of the target with:
  id           +0.0193
  row_number   +0.7262  <- LEAK
  recorded_at  +0.0194
  feature_1    +0.3106
  feature_2    +0.0074
```

`row_number` correlates at +0.73 with the target purely because of how the file was assembled.
A model handed that column will top your local CV and score at chance on the leaderboard. The rule
is blunt: **any identifier, index, or file-order artefact is suspect until proven inert.**

Compare the honest feature: `feature_1` (a noisy copy of the real signal) only reaches +0.3106.
When a bookkeeping column beats your best real feature by more than double, that is not a
discovery; it is a bug in how the file was made. `id` (+0.0193) and `recorded_at` (+0.0194) are
inert here, because the sort was by target first and `id` second.

### Duplicates that cross the split

The second test: does the same row appear in both train and test? Turn each row's features into a
text **key** (rounded so tiny float differences do not hide a match) and intersect the two sets.

```python
dup_rows = comp.sample(300, random_state=1).copy()
comp_train = comp.iloc[:6_000]
comp_test = pd.concat([comp.iloc[6_000:], dup_rows]).reset_index(drop=True)

feat_cols = ["feature_1", "feature_2"]
key_train = comp_train[feat_cols].round(6).astype(str).agg("|".join, axis=1)
key_test = comp_test[feat_cols].round(6).astype(str).agg("|".join, axis=1)
overlap = set(key_train) & set(key_test)
print(f"train rows {len(comp_train)} | test rows {len(comp_test)}")
print(f"exact feature-duplicate rows shared across the split: {len(overlap)} "
      f"({len(overlap) / len(comp_test):.1%} of test)")
```

```output
train rows 6000 | test rows 2300
exact feature-duplicate rows shared across the split: 221 (9.6% of test)
```

221 test rows — 9.6% of the 2,300 — are exact feature copies of a training row. If your
validation had the same overlap, your CV would be flattered on about one row in ten: those rows
are answered by memory, not by skill.

Duplicates across a split are two different things at once: a validation problem (your CV is
optimistic by the duplicate share) and an opportunity (if the *real* test set has them, a
nearest-neighbour lookup is a legitimate part of a solution). Measure it before you decide which.

> **Watch out:** compare **rounded** keys. Floats written at full precision almost never match
> exactly, so an unrounded check reports zero duplicates when a large share of the test set is a
> copy.

### Group structure hiding as rows

The third test: do many rows belong to the same person, shop, or device? If so, a model can learn
"this customer usually churns" and look brilliant when that customer appears in both the training
and validation folds.

```python
comp["customer_id"] = rng.integers(0, 900, len(comp))     # many rows per customer
per_customer = comp.groupby("customer_id")["target"].agg(["count", "mean"])
same_label = float(((per_customer["mean"] == 0) | (per_customer["mean"] == 1)).mean())
print(f"customers: {len(per_customer)} | median rows each: "
      f"{per_customer['count'].median():.0f}")
print(f"share of customers whose rows all share one label: {same_label:.1%}")
print("=> random k-fold would put the same customer on both sides of the split")
```

```output
customers: 900 | median rows each: 9
share of customers whose rows all share one label: 12.2%
=> random k-fold would put the same customer on both sides of the split
```

900 customers own the 8,000 rows, about 9 rows each (median). For 12.2% of customers every row
has the same label, so seeing one of their rows in training gives away the rest. A **random
k-fold** split (cut the rows into k random piles) would scatter each customer across piles, and
the model would be graded partly on people it has already met.

## Designing the CV before the first model

Your CV is a guess about how the organiser split train from test. Write that guess down first,
then build folds that copy it.

The validation scheme is a *hypothesis about the test split*. State it explicitly, then check it
against whatever the organiser told you.

| What the data has | What the test split probably is | Your CV |
|---|---|---|
| plain i.i.d. rows | random | stratified k-fold |
| repeated entities | unseen entities | GroupKFold on the entity |
| a time column, test after train | future | rolling origin / a single time cut |
| both | future rows of unseen entities | grouped + time cut |
| heavy class imbalance | same imbalance | repeated stratified k-fold |

The words in that table, in plain terms:

- **i.i.d.** rows are independent and identically distributed: no row tells you anything about
  another, and all come from the same source.
- **Stratified k-fold** cuts the data into k folds that each keep the same positive rate.
- **GroupKFold** keeps every row of one entity inside a single fold, so validation is on
  entities the model has never seen.
- A **rolling origin** split trains on the past and validates on the next stretch of time, then
  slides forward.

> **Picture it:** if the real exam will ask about cities you have never visited, practice with
> quiz questions about cities you have never visited — not with questions about your home town.

The helper below turns the leak checks into a written plan:

```python
def suggest_cv(frame, target="target", id_like=(), time_col=None, group_col=None):
    notes = []
    n_rows = len(frame)
    pos = float(frame[target].mean())
    if group_col is not None:
        rows_per = frame.groupby(group_col).size()
        if rows_per.max() > 1:
            notes.append(f"GroupKFold on '{group_col}' "
                         f"(up to {rows_per.max()} rows per group)")
    if time_col is not None:
        notes.append(f"time-ordered split on '{time_col}' (train past, validate future)")
    if min(pos, 1 - pos) * n_rows < 500:
        notes.append(f"repeated stratified folds: only {int(min(pos, 1 - pos) * n_rows)} "
                     f"minority rows")
    for col in id_like:
        r = abs(float(np.corrcoef(frame[col], frame[target])[0, 1]))
        if r > 0.1:
            notes.append(f"DROP '{col}': |corr with target| = {r:.3f}")
    return notes or ["stratified k-fold; no structure detected"]

for note in suggest_cv(comp, id_like=("id", "row_number"), time_col="recorded_at",
                       group_col="customer_id"):
    print(f"- {note}")
```

```output
- GroupKFold on 'customer_id' (up to 21 rows per group)
- time-ordered split on 'recorded_at' (train past, validate future)
- DROP 'row_number': |corr with target| = 0.726
```

Each line is one decision with its evidence. The largest customer has 21 rows, so grouping is
needed. The time column means "train on the past, validate on the future". And `row_number` gets
dropped with its 0.726 correlation printed next to the verdict. There is no "repeated folds" line:
the minority class has far more than 500 rows (22.8% of 8,000 is about 1,800).

The output is a plan you can defend, produced before any modelling. When your CV and the
leaderboard later disagree (ch. 2), this is the document you re-read first.

> **Watch out:** a plan is a hypothesis, not a fact. If the organiser says "test is the next
> three months", that beats anything your helper guesses from the columns.

## Recap in plain words

- Read the metric first: rank metrics ignore calibration, probability metrics punish it, and
  threshold metrics make the cut part of your answer.
- Tuning the F1 threshold on this data was worth +0.21 — more than most features.
- A leak is a clue that will not exist at prediction time; IDs, row order, duplicates, and groups
  cover most of them.
- Any column that describes the file rather than the world is guilty until proven innocent.
- Your CV scheme is a written guess about how the test set was cut; choose it before any model.

## Try it Yourself

The function below folds this chapter into one printout: it names the metric family, states what
that family implies, and attaches the CV plan from `suggest_cv`.

```python
def competition_brief(frame, target, metric, id_like=(), time_col=None, group_col=None):
    metric_family = {"auc": "rank", "map": "rank", "ndcg": "rank",
                     "logloss": "probability", "brier": "probability",
                     "accuracy": "threshold", "f1": "threshold", "kappa": "threshold",
                     "rmse": "regression", "rmsle": "regression"}[metric]
    advice = {
        "rank": "calibration is worthless; rank-average when blending; tune ordering only",
        "probability": "calibrate (eval-tuning ch. 4); never rank-average raw probabilities",
        "threshold": "tune the cut on out-of-fold predictions; it is part of the model",
        "regression": "check whether the metric is on a log scale before transforming y",
    }[metric_family]
    print(f"metric        {metric} ({metric_family})")
    print(f"implication   {advice}")
    print(f"rows          {len(frame):,}   positive rate {frame[target].mean():.3f}")
    print("validation plan:")
    for note in suggest_cv(frame, target, id_like, time_col, group_col):
        print(f"  - {note}")

competition_brief(comp, "target", "auc", id_like=("id", "row_number"),
                  time_col="recorded_at", group_col="customer_id")
print()
competition_brief(comp, "target", "f1", id_like=("id",))
```

rows          8,000   positive rate 0.228

```python
def leak_audit(train, test, target, feature_cols, id_like=(), group_col=None):
    findings = []

    for col in id_like:                                    # 1. identifier leaks
        if col not in train:
            continue
        r = abs(float(np.corrcoef(train[col], train[target])[0, 1]))
        findings.append(("identifier", col, round(r, 4),
                         "HIGH" if r > 0.3 else "medium" if r > 0.1 else "low"))

    order_corr = abs(float(np.corrcoef(np.arange(len(train)), train[target])[0, 1]))
    findings.append(("row order", "(file order)", round(order_corr, 4),
                     "HIGH" if order_corr > 0.3 else "medium" if order_corr > 0.1 else "low"))

    ktr = train[feature_cols].round(6).astype(str).agg("|".join, axis=1)
    kte = test[feature_cols].round(6).astype(str).agg("|".join, axis=1)
    share = len(set(ktr) & set(kte)) / max(len(kte), 1)
    findings.append(("duplicates", "train∩test", round(share, 4),
                     "HIGH" if share > 0.05 else "medium" if share > 0.005 else "low"))

    if group_col:
        rows_per = train.groupby(group_col).size()
        multi = float((rows_per > 1).mean())
        findings.append(("group structure", group_col, round(multi, 4),
                         "HIGH" if multi > 0.5 else "medium" if multi > 0.1 else "low"))

    rep = pd.DataFrame(findings, columns=["family", "where", "evidence", "risk"])
    drop = [c for f, c, e, r in findings if f in ("identifier",) and r != "low"]
    if order_corr > 0.1:
        drop.append("(shuffle the file before splitting)")
    return rep, drop

feature_cols = ["feature_1", "feature_2"]
# the organiser shuffles rows into train/test, but the row_number column survives the shuffle
shuf = comp.sample(frac=1.0, random_state=3).reset_index(drop=True)
train_p = shuf.iloc[:6_000]
test_p = pd.concat([shuf.iloc[6_000:], shuf.sample(300, random_state=1)]).reset_index(drop=True)
print("POISONED dataset")
rep, drop = leak_audit(train_p, test_p, "target", feature_cols,
                       id_like=("id", "row_number"), group_col="customer_id")
print(rep.to_string(index=False))
print(f"action: drop {drop}\n")

clean = comp.sample(frac=1.0, random_state=7).reset_index(drop=True).copy()
clean["id"] = np.arange(len(clean))
clean["row_number"] = np.arange(len(clean))
clean["customer_id"] = np.arange(len(clean))              # one row per entity
c_train, c_test = clean.iloc[:6_000], clean.iloc[6_000:]
print("CLEAN dataset")
rep2, drop2 = leak_audit(c_train, c_test, "target", feature_cols,
                         id_like=("id", "row_number"), group_col="customer_id")
print(rep2.to_string(index=False))
print(f"action: drop {drop2 or 'nothing'}")
```

     identifier           id    0.0293  low

group structure  customer_id    0.9889 HIGH

The audit does the job on both inputs, which is the part people skip: a detector that only ever
fires is as useless as one that never does. On the poisoned frame it flags `row_number` at 0.7286,
a 9.6% duplicate overlap, and pervasive group structure, and it names the column to drop; on the
clean frame every one of those numbers collapses below 0.005 and it recommends nothing.

Read the poisoned report's *third* row too: the file-order check comes back low (0.0041) even
though the file was assembled sorted by target. That is correct and instructive — the organiser
shuffled the rows into train and test, so position no longer carries the signal, but the
`row_number` **column** they forgot to delete still does. Two checks that sound like the same
check are not, and dropping either one would have missed a leak.

Three things to note about the design. The evidence column carries the **number**, not just the
verdict, because a 0.11 correlation and a 0.98 correlation are both "medium-or-worse" and only one
of them is worth a morning. The duplicate check compares **rounded feature keys** rather than
whole rows, since a float printed at full precision almost never matches and you would conclude
there are no duplicates when 4% of the test set is a copy. And the group check reports the
*share* of multi-row groups rather than the maximum, because one customer with 500 rows in a
million-row file is a different problem from half the file being repeat customers.

What no audit can find is the leak that lives in the meaning of a column rather than its
statistics — a "days since last payment" field that was computed after the outcome, a status flag
that only exists once a case is closed. For those, the tool is a data dictionary and a
conversation, and the tell is a feature whose importance is implausibly high (eval-tuning ch. 8)
for what it claims to measure.
