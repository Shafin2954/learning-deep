## Law of Large Numbers

Flip a fair coin 10 times and you might get 7 heads. Flip it 10,000 times and you'll get
very close to 5,000. The **law of large numbers** is that promise: averages of many random
draws settle down to the true mean.

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mu$. The **Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)** states:

$$
\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \;\xrightarrow{p}\; \mu \quad \text{as } n\to\infty
$$

**i.i.d.** means *independent and identically distributed*: every draw comes from the same
distribution, and no draw influences another. $\bar{X}_n$ is the average of the first $n$
draws, and $\xrightarrow{p}$ reads "converges in probability to".

"Converges in probability" means: for any $\varepsilon > 0$, $P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \to 0$.

> **In plain words:** pick any tolerance you like — say 0.01. The chance that the average
> misses the true mean by more than 0.01 shrinks toward zero as you collect more data.

The **Strong LLN** is stronger: the convergence holds almost surely (the event $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ has probability 1).

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Bernoulli(p=0.3): true mean = 0.3
p = 0.3
ns = [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000]

print("n         x_bar     |x_bar - 0.3|")
print("-" * 40)
for n in ns:
    x = rng.binomial(1, p, n)
    xbar = x.mean()
    print(f"{n:>8}   {xbar:.5f}   {abs(xbar - p):.5f}")
```

```output
n         x_bar     |x_bar - 0.3|
----------------------------------------
      10   0.40000   0.10000
     100   0.37000   0.07000
    1000   0.31000   0.01000
   10000   0.29700   0.00300
  100000   0.29897   0.00103
```

At $n = 10$ the average is 0.40, off by 0.10. By $n = 100{,}000$ it's 0.29897, off by about
0.001. The error doesn't shrink steadily — at $n = 100$ it's still 0.07 — because each row is a
fresh random sample. But the trend is unmistakable.

The deviation shrinks roughly as $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ — the standard error.

> **In plain words:** the typical error is about $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$, the **standard error**.
> Because of the square root, 100 times more data buys only 10 times more accuracy. Here
> $\sigma = \sqrt{0.21} \approx 0.46$, so at $n = 100{,}000$ the typical error is about
> $0.46 / 316 \approx 0.0015$ — in line with the 0.001 observed.

> **Watch out:** "the law of averages" does *not* mean a run of heads makes tails more
> likely next. The LLN works by *swamping* early deviations with lots of new data, not by
> correcting them.

## Central Limit Theorem

The LLN says the average gets close to the mean. The **central limit theorem** says *how*
the leftover error is shaped: like a bell curve — whatever the original data looked like.
That's why the Normal shows up everywhere averages do.

The **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)** tells you the *shape* of that convergence. For i.i.d. $X_i$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$:

$$
\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N(0,1)
$$

> **In plain words:** take the sample mean's error, $\bar{X}_n - \mu$, and measure it in units
> of the standard error $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$. That standardized error has (approximately) a
> standard Normal distribution when $n$ is large. ($\xrightarrow{d}$ = "converges in
> distribution": its histogram approaches the bell curve.)

This holds regardless of the parent distribution — as long as $\sigma^2 < \infty$.
The **parent distribution** is the distribution of the individual $X_i$.

> **Picture it:** a single die roll is flat — every face equally likely. The average of two
> dice is a triangle (7 is common, 2 and 12 rare). The average of ten dice is already a
> bell. Adding many independent pieces sands the original shape away.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
N_sims = 10_000

# Three very different parent distributions
parents = {
    'Bernoulli(0.3)': lambda n: rng.binomial(1, 0.3, n),      # mu=0.3, sigma=sqrt(0.21)
    'Exponential(1)': lambda n: rng.exponential(1.0, n),        # mu=1,   sigma=1
    'Uniform(0,1)':   lambda n: rng.uniform(0, 1, n),           # mu=0.5, sigma=1/sqrt(12)
}

true_params = {
    'Bernoulli(0.3)': (0.3, np.sqrt(0.3*0.7)),
    'Exponential(1)': (1.0, 1.0),
    'Uniform(0,1)':   (0.5, 1/np.sqrt(12)),
}

for ns in [5, 30, 100]:
    print(f"\nn = {ns}")
    for name, sampler in parents.items():
        mu, sigma = true_params[name]
        xbars = np.array([sampler(ns).mean() for _ in range(N_sims)])
        z = (xbars - mu) / (sigma / np.sqrt(ns))
        # Test: should be ~N(0,1)
        from scipy import stats
        _, p_val = stats.normaltest(z)
        print(f"  {name:<22} normality p-val={p_val:.4f}  z_std={z.std():.4f}")
```

```output
n = 5
  Bernoulli(0.3)         normality p-val=0.0000  z_std=1.0103
  Exponential(1)         normality p-val=0.0000  z_std=0.9888
  Uniform(0,1)           normality p-val=0.0000  z_std=0.9989

n = 30
  Bernoulli(0.3)         normality p-val=0.0000  z_std=0.9993
  Exponential(1)         normality p-val=0.0000  z_std=0.9961
  Uniform(0,1)           normality p-val=0.8068  z_std=0.9970

n = 100
  Bernoulli(0.3)         normality p-val=0.0000  z_std=1.0018
  Exponential(1)         normality p-val=0.0000  z_std=0.9922
  Uniform(0,1)           normality p-val=0.0930  z_std=1.0093
```

Two different stories are in this table. The `z_std` column is about 1 everywhere, for every
parent and every $n$: the standard-error scaling is exactly right. But the **normality test**
(a hypothesis test that asks "could this be exactly Normal?", ch. 11) rejects Bernoulli and
Exponential means at every $n$, even 100, and accepts only the symmetric Uniform at $n = 30$
and above.

Uniform converges fastest (it's symmetric). The usual rule of thumb says a skewed parent like
the Exponential needs $n\approx 30$–100, yet here it still fails the normality test at $n = 100$: the leftover skewness of its mean shrinks like $2/\sqrt{n}$ (0.2 at
$n = 100$), and with 10,000 simulated means the test is sharp enough to detect it. The
Bernoulli's mean also only takes a few discrete values at these $n$, which an exact-Normal
test always notices.

> **Watch out:** the CLT is an approximation that gets better with $n$; it is never exactly
> true at finite $n$. With huge simulations a formal test will reject any approximation. The
> practical question is whether it's *good enough* — the next section measures that.

## Convergence rate: Berry-Esseen bound

How big must $n$ be? The **Berry–Esseen theorem** puts a number on the worst-case error of the
bell-curve approximation, and it depends on how skewed the parent distribution is.

The CLT is asymptotic. The **Berry-Esseen theorem** gives a finite-sample bound on the error:

$$
\sup_x \left|P\!\left(\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \le x\right) - \Phi(x)\right| \le \frac{C\,\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt{n}}
$$

where $\rho = E[|X-\mu|^3]$ and $C \approx 0.4748$. Heavier-tailed distributions converge slower.

> **In plain words:** the left side is the largest gap, anywhere, between the true CDF of the
> standardized mean and the Normal CDF $\Phi$ ("sup" = the largest value over all $x$). The
> right side says that gap is at most a constant times "how lopsided the parent is"
> ($\rho / \sigma^3$) divided by $\sqrt{n}$. Quadruple $n$, halve the worst-case error.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n, N_sims = 30, 50_000

# Compare CDF of standardized mean to N(0,1) CDF
for name, sampler, mu, sigma, rho_over_sigma3 in [
    ('Uniform(0,1)', lambda: rng.uniform(0,1,n), 0.5, 1/np.sqrt(12), 0.03125 / (1/np.sqrt(12))**3),  # E|X-0.5|^3 = 1/32
    ('Exp(1)',       lambda: rng.exponential(1,n), 1.0, 1.0,          12/np.e - 2),   # E|X-1|^3 = 12/e - 2 ≈ 2.415 for Exp(1)
]:
    z_samples = np.array([(sampler().mean() - mu) / (sigma/np.sqrt(n)) for _ in range(N_sims)])
    x_vals = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
    emp_cdf  = np.array([(z_samples <= x).mean() for x in x_vals])
    norm_cdf = stats.norm.cdf(x_vals)
    max_err  = np.abs(emp_cdf - norm_cdf).max()
    be_bound = 0.4748 * rho_over_sigma3 / np.sqrt(n)
    print(f"{name:<15}  max_err={max_err:.5f}  BE_bound={be_bound:.5f}")
```

```output
Uniform(0,1)     max_err=0.00385  BE_bound=0.11261
Exp(1)           max_err=0.02300  BE_bound=0.20931
```

At $n = 30$ the measured worst gap is tiny for the Uniform (0.004) and larger for the skewed
Exponential (0.023). Both sit well under their Berry–Esseen bounds (0.11 and 0.21): the bound
is a guarantee for the worst possible parent with that $\rho/\sigma^3$, so real distributions
usually do much better. A gap of 0.023 means probabilities computed with the Normal are off
by at most about 2 percentage points — good enough for most purposes.

## Recap in plain words

- LLN: the sample mean converges to the true mean; the typical error is $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$.
- CLT: the standardized sample mean is approximately $N(0, 1)$, whatever the parent
  distribution, as long as its variance is finite.
- Symmetric parents converge fast; skewed ones are slower, and formal normality tests on
  large simulations will still spot the leftover skew.
- Berry–Esseen bounds the worst-case CDF error by $C\rho / (\sigma^3 \sqrt{n})$.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(99)
N_sims, n = 20_000, 50

# Chi-squared(df=2) is very skewed: mean=2, var=4, skewness=sqrt(8/df)=2
df = 2
mu    = df
sigma = np.sqrt(2 * df)

xbars = np.array([rng.chisquare(df, n).mean() for _ in range(N_sims)])
z     = (xbars - mu) / (sigma / np.sqrt(n))

_, p_norm = stats.normaltest(z)
print(f"Chi-sq(2) parent, n={n}")
print(f"  z mean={z.mean():.4f}  z std={z.std():.4f}")
print(f"  normality p-value={p_norm:.4f}")
print(f"  CLT holds? {p_norm > 0.05}")
```

```output
Chi-sq(2) parent, n=50
  z mean=0.0065  z std=1.0025
  normality p-value=0.0000
  CLT holds? False
```

The code concludes "CLT holds? False" — but look at the first line: the standardized means
have mean 0.007 and standard deviation 1.003, exactly what the CLT predicts. What the test
detects is the remaining skew: a Chi-squared(2) parent has skewness 2, so means of 50 still
have skewness about $2/\sqrt{50} \approx 0.28$, and 20,000 simulations easily catch that. The
lesson: "fails a normality test" and "the Normal approximation is useless" are different
claims. Try `n = 500` and watch the p-value climb.

## Exercise

For a Poisson(3) parent: (1) What are $\mu$ and $\sigma$? (2) Simulate 10,000 sample means of size $n=20$. (3) Verify they're approximately Normal by computing the fraction falling within $\pm 1.96$ standard errors of $\mu$.

> **Hint:** for a Normal, 95% of the mass lies within $\pm 1.96$ standard deviations, so the
> fraction should be close to 0.95 if the CLT approximation is good.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
lam = 3.0
mu, sigma = lam, np.sqrt(lam)
n, N_sims = 20, 10_000

print(f"Poisson({lam}): mu={mu}, sigma={sigma:.4f}")
print(f"SE = sigma/sqrt(n) = {sigma/np.sqrt(n):.4f}")

xbars = rng.poisson(lam, (N_sims, n)).mean(axis=1)

# Fraction within ±1.96 SE of mu (should be ~95%)
se = sigma / np.sqrt(n)
within = np.abs(xbars - mu) <= 1.96 * se
print(f"Fraction within ±1.96*SE: {within.mean():.4f}  (expected: ~0.95)")

# Normality check
z = (xbars - mu) / se
_, p_val = stats.normaltest(z)
print(f"Normality p-value: {p_val:.4f}")
```

For Poisson($\lambda$): $\mu=\lambda=3$, $\sigma=\sqrt{\lambda}\approx1.732$. The coverage should be very close to 0.95.
The coverage check is the practical test of "good enough": if about 95% of the means land
inside $\mu \pm 1.96 \cdot SE$, intervals built on the Normal approximation (ch. 10) will work,
whatever a strict normality test says.
