## The Normal distribution

The **Normal** (or Gaussian) is the famous bell curve: most values near the middle, fewer
and fewer the further out you go, perfectly symmetric. Heights, measurement errors, and
averages of many small effects all look like this.

The Normal (Gaussian) distribution is parameterized by mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

> **In plain words:** the height of the bell at $x$ depends only on how many standard
> deviations $x$ is from the mean, squared: $\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^2$. The
> $\exp(-\ldots)$ makes it fall off very fast, and $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ is just the
> constant that makes the total area exactly 1.

The **Z-score** $Z = (X-\mu)/\sigma$ standardizes to $N(0,1)$. The 68-95-99.7 rule:
- 68% of mass within $\pm 1\sigma$
- 95% within $\pm 2\sigma$
- 99.7% within $\pm 3\sigma$

A Z-score is "how many standard deviations above (or below) the mean". $N(0, 1)$ — mean 0,
standard deviation 1 — is the **standard Normal**.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

mu, sigma = 5.0, 2.0
dist = stats.norm(loc=mu, scale=sigma)

print(f"mean={dist.mean():.1f}  var={dist.var():.1f}")
print(f"P(|X-mu| < 1*sigma) = {dist.cdf(mu+sigma) - dist.cdf(mu-sigma):.4f}")
print(f"P(|X-mu| < 2*sigma) = {dist.cdf(mu+2*sigma) - dist.cdf(mu-2*sigma):.4f}")
print(f"P(|X-mu| < 3*sigma) = {dist.cdf(mu+3*sigma) - dist.cdf(mu-3*sigma):.4f}")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sample = rng.normal(mu, sigma, 10_000)
print(f"\nSample mean={sample.mean():.3f}  std={sample.std():.3f}")
```

```output
mean=5.0  var=4.0
P(|X-mu| < 1*sigma) = 0.6827
P(|X-mu| < 2*sigma) = 0.9545
P(|X-mu| < 3*sigma) = 0.9973

Sample mean=5.013  std=1.996
```

The three probabilities are the exact areas under the bell: 0.6827, 0.9545, 0.9973 — the
68-95-99.7 rule to four decimals. They don't depend on $\mu = 5$ or $\sigma = 2$ at all; every
Normal has the same shape once you measure in standard deviations. The 10,000 random draws
recover mean $\approx 5$ and standard deviation $\approx 2$.

The Normal is closed under linear transformations: if $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ then $aX+b\sim N(a\mu+b,a^2\sigma^2)$. The sum of independent Normals is Normal.

> **Watch out:** the 68-95-99.7 rule is for Normals only. For heavy-tailed data (ch. 2's
> lognormal), "3 sigma" events happen far more often than 0.3% of the time — a frequent
> source of blown risk estimates.

## The Exponential distribution

The **Exponential** is the waiting time until the next random event — the next bus, the
next customer, the next radioactive click — when events happen at a steady average rate.

The Exponential distribution models the time until the first event in a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$:

$$
f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \quad x \ge 0
$$

Mean $= 1/\lambda$, variance $= 1/\lambda^2$.

A **Poisson process** is a stream of events that happen independently at an average rate
$\lambda$ per unit time; the count in any window is Poisson (ch. 3), and the gaps between
events are Exponential. The density is highest at 0: short waits are most common.

The **memoryless property** is its defining feature: $P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)$. If you've already waited $s$ minutes, the remaining wait has the same distribution as the original.

> **Picture it:** a light bulb that doesn't age. If it has already burned for 1000 hours,
> its chance of lasting another 100 is the same as a brand-new bulb's. (Real bulbs do age —
> which is why lifetimes are often modelled with the Gamma or Weibull instead.)

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

lam = 0.5  # average 2 minutes between events
dist = stats.expon(scale=1/lam)

print(f"mean={dist.mean():.1f}  std={dist.std():.1f}")

# Memoryless: P(X > 3+2 | X > 3) should equal P(X > 2)
s, t = 3.0, 2.0
p_gt_s = 1 - dist.cdf(s)
p_gt_st = 1 - dist.cdf(s + t)
conditional = p_gt_st / p_gt_s
p_gt_t = 1 - dist.cdf(t)

print(f"P(X > {s+t} | X > {s}) = {conditional:.4f}")
print(f"P(X > {t})              = {p_gt_t:.4f}")
print(f"Memoryless: {np.isclose(conditional, p_gt_t)}")
```

```output
mean=2.0  std=2.0
P(X > 5.0 | X > 3.0) = 0.3679
P(X > 2.0)              = 0.3679
Memoryless: True
```

With $\lambda = 0.5$ events per minute, the mean wait is 2 minutes and so is the standard
deviation. Having waited 3 minutes already, the chance of waiting more than 2 *more* is
0.3679 — exactly the fresh chance of waiting more than 2 ($e^{-1}$). The 3 minutes you
already waited bought you nothing.

## The Gamma distribution

The **Gamma** is the waiting time for *several* events instead of one: add up $\alpha$
independent Exponential waits and you get a Gamma.

The Gamma distribution generalizes the Exponential. With shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$:

$$
f(x) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x}, \quad x > 0
$$

Mean $= \alpha/\beta$, variance $= \alpha/\beta^2$.

$\Gamma(\alpha)$ is the **gamma function**, a smooth extension of the factorial:
$\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$ for whole numbers. Here it's only the constant that makes the area 1.

Special cases:
- $\alpha=1$: Exponential with rate $\beta$
- $\alpha=k/2, \beta=1/2$: Chi-squared with $k$ degrees of freedom
- Integer $\alpha$: Erlang distribution (sum of $\alpha$ independent exponentials)

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Verify: sum of alpha independent Exp(beta) ~ Gamma(alpha, beta)
alpha, beta = 4, 0.5
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 50_000

# Direct Gamma samples
gamma_samples = rng.gamma(shape=alpha, scale=1/beta, size=n)

# Sum of alpha Exponentials
exp_samples = rng.exponential(scale=1/beta, size=(n, alpha)).sum(axis=1)

print(f"Gamma direct:  mean={gamma_samples.mean():.3f}  std={gamma_samples.std():.3f}")
print(f"Sum of Exp:    mean={exp_samples.mean():.3f}  std={exp_samples.std():.3f}")
print(f"Theoretical:   mean={alpha/beta:.3f}  std={np.sqrt(alpha)/beta:.3f}")
```

```output
Gamma direct:  mean=7.976  std=3.991
Sum of Exp:    mean=7.956  std=3.973
Theoretical:   mean=8.000  std=4.000
```

Two very different ways of generating the data — NumPy's Gamma sampler, and adding 4
Exponential waits together — give the same mean ($\approx 8 = 4 / 0.5$) and standard deviation
($\approx 4 = \sqrt{4} / 0.5$). Note the parameter trap in the code: NumPy and SciPy take
`scale` $= 1/\beta$, not the rate $\beta$.

> **Watch out:** "Gamma($\alpha$, $\beta$)" can mean rate $\beta$ (as here) or scale $\beta$
> (as in some textbooks and NumPy's `scale=`). The mean is $\alpha/\beta$ in one convention
> and $\alpha\beta$ in the other. Always check which one a formula or library uses.

## The Beta distribution

The **Beta** is a distribution over *probabilities* — numbers between 0 and 1. It's how you
say "I think this coin lands heads about 70% of the time, give or take".

The Beta distribution has support $[0,1]$, making it a natural prior for probabilities:

$$
f(x) = \frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}, \quad 0 \le x \le 1
$$

Mean $= \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$. The shape parameters control skew:
- $\alpha=\beta=1$: Uniform
- $\alpha=\beta > 1$: Symmetric, bell-shaped around 0.5
- $\alpha < \beta$: Mass near 0, long tail to the right (right-skewed)
- $\alpha > \beta$: Mass near 1, long tail to the left (left-skewed)

> **In plain words:** think of $\alpha - 1$ as "heads seen" and $\beta - 1$ as "tails seen".
> More heads pushes the mass toward 1; more of both makes the curve narrower (more certain).
> Skew is named after the long *tail*, not the pile: mass near 0 with a tail to the right
> is right-skewed. $B(\alpha, \beta)$ is again just the area-1 constant.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

configs = [
    (1, 1, "Uniform"),
    (2, 5, "mass near 0, right-skewed"),
    (5, 2, "mass near 1, left-skewed"),
    (5, 5, "symmetric around 0.5"),
]

for a, b, desc in configs:
    d = stats.beta(a, b)
    print(f"Beta({a},{b}) [{desc}]: mean={d.mean():.3f}  var={d.var():.4f}")
```

```output
Beta(1,1) [Uniform]: mean=0.500  var=0.0833
Beta(2,5) [mass near 0, right-skewed]: mean=0.286  var=0.0255
Beta(5,2) [mass near 1, left-skewed]: mean=0.714  var=0.0255
Beta(5,5) [symmetric around 0.5]: mean=0.500  var=0.0227
```

Beta(1,1) is flat, with mean 0.5 and the Uniform's variance $1/12 = 0.0833$. Beta(2,5) and
Beta(5,2) are mirror images: means $2/7 = 0.286$ and $5/7 = 0.714$, identical variance.
Beta(5,5) is centered at 0.5 but narrower (variance 0.0227) than the flat Beta(1,1) — more
"pseudo-observations" means more certainty.

The Beta is the **conjugate prior** for the Bernoulli/Binomial likelihood. After observing $k$ successes in $n$ trials, a $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior becomes $\text{Beta}(\alpha+k, \beta+n-k)$.

**Conjugate** means the posterior is in the same family as the prior, so updating is just
adding counts — no integrals.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Bayesian coin-flipping: start with Beta(1,1) = Uniform prior
# Observe 7 heads in 10 flips
alpha_prior, beta_prior = 1, 1
k, n = 7, 10

alpha_post = alpha_prior + k
beta_post  = beta_prior + (n - k)

posterior = stats.beta(alpha_post, beta_post)
print(f"Posterior: Beta({alpha_post}, {beta_post})")
print(f"  mean = {posterior.mean():.4f}  (MLE = {k/n:.4f})")
print(f"  95% credible interval: [{posterior.ppf(0.025):.3f}, {posterior.ppf(0.975):.3f}]")
```

```output
Posterior: Beta(8, 4)
  mean = 0.6667  (MLE = 0.7000)
  95% credible interval: [0.390, 0.891]
```

Starting from a flat prior and seeing 7 heads in 10 flips, the posterior is Beta(8, 4). Its
mean $8/12 = 0.667$ sits a little below the raw frequency 0.7, and the 95% **credible
interval** — the range that holds 95% of the posterior's probability — runs from 0.39 to 0.89.
Ten flips leave a lot of uncertainty.

The posterior mean 0.667 pulls the MLE (0.7) toward the prior mean (0.5) — the Bayesian shrinkage effect.
(**MLE** = maximum likelihood estimate, here just $k/n$; ch. 9.)

## Recap in plain words

- Normal: the bell curve; 68/95/99.7% within 1/2/3 standard deviations; sums and linear
  transforms of Normals stay Normal.
- Exponential: waiting time for the next event at rate $\lambda$; mean $1/\lambda$;
  memoryless.
- Gamma: waiting time for $\alpha$ events; Exponential and Chi-squared are special cases.
  Check rate vs scale.
- Beta: a distribution over probabilities on $[0, 1]$; conjugate to the Binomial, so a
  Beta($\alpha$, $\beta$) prior plus $k$ of $n$ successes gives Beta($\alpha + k$,
  $\beta + n - k$).

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 10_000

# Generate data from a mixture: 60% Normal(0,1) + 40% Exponential(rate=2)
normal_part = rng.normal(0, 1, int(0.6 * n))
exp_part    = rng.exponential(scale=0.5, size=int(0.4 * n))
data = np.concatenate([normal_part, exp_part])

print(f"Sample mean:     {data.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Sample std:      {data.std():.4f}")
print(f"Sample skewness: {stats.skew(data):.4f}")
print(f"Sample kurtosis: {stats.kurtosis(data):.4f}")

# What fraction is negative? (only the Normal part can be negative)
print(f"Fraction < 0: {(data < 0).mean():.4f}")
```

```output
Sample mean:     0.1943
Sample std:      0.8730
Sample skewness: -0.2940
Sample kurtosis: 1.0778
Fraction < 0: 0.2976
```

A **mixture** draws each value from one of several distributions. Only the Normal part can be
negative, and half of it is, so about $0.6 \times 0.5 = 30\%$ of the data is below zero
(0.2976). The mean is the weighted average of the parts' means:
$0.6 \times 0 + 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.2$. The shape numbers are less intuitive — the skewness
comes out slightly *negative* even though the Exponential part is right-skewed — which is
exactly why you measure shape instead of guessing it. Try changing the mix to 20% Normal.

## Exercise

You observe 200 waiting times (in minutes) and suspect they follow $\text{Exponential}(\lambda)$. The sample mean is 4.2 minutes. (1) What is the MLE of $\lambda$? (2) Using `scipy.stats.kstest`, test whether a sample from this estimated exponential fits the data. (3) What is $P(X > 10)$ under this model?

> **Hint:** the Exponential's mean is $1/\lambda$, so a natural estimate of $\lambda$ is one
> over the sample mean. For (3), $P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
# Simulate the scenario
true_lam = 1/4.2
data = rng.exponential(scale=1/true_lam, size=200)

# 1) MLE: lambda_hat = 1 / x_bar
xbar = data.mean()
lam_hat = 1 / xbar
print(f"Sample mean: {xbar:.4f}  MLE lambda: {lam_hat:.4f}")

# 2) KS test
ks_stat, p_val = stats.kstest(data, 'expon', args=(0, 1/lam_hat))
print(f"KS statistic: {ks_stat:.4f}  p-value: {p_val:.4f}")
print(f"Reject H0 (not Exponential)? {p_val < 0.05}")

# 3) P(X > 10)
dist = stats.expon(scale=1/lam_hat)
print(f"P(X > 10) = {1 - dist.cdf(10):.4f}")
```

The MLE of $\lambda$ for the Exponential is $\hat\lambda = 1/\bar{x}$. A p-value above 0.05 on the KS test means we fail to reject the Exponential assumption.
The **KS test** (Kolmogorov–Smirnov) compares the data's empirical CDF with the model's CDF and
measures the largest gap. One caution: estimating $\lambda$ from the same data you test makes
the standard KS p-value too generous; the Lilliefors correction fixes that. For (3),
$P(X > 10) = e^{-10/4.2} \approx 0.09$.
</details>
