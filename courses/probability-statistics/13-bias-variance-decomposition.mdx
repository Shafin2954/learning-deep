## The decomposition

A model's mistakes come from three places: it can be **systematically off** (bias — too
simple to capture the pattern), it can be **jumpy** (variance — too sensitive to which
training data it happened to see), and the data itself is **noisy** (nobody can predict the
coin-flip part). Every model trades the first two against each other.

The expected test MSE of a predictor $\hat{f}$ at a point $x$ decomposes as:

$$
E\!\left[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(E[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)\right)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{E\!\left[\left(\hat{f}(x) - E[\hat{f}(x)]\right)^2\right]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2_\varepsilon}_{\text{Irreducible noise}}
$$

The expectation is over repeated draws of training datasets. The noise $\sigma^2_\varepsilon = \text{Var}(\varepsilon)$ is irreducible — no model can remove it.

> **Picture it:** four archers. Bias is how far the *centre* of an archer's arrows is from the
> bullseye; variance is how widely the arrows scatter around their own centre. A simple model
> is a steady archer aiming at the wrong spot; a complex model is an archer aiming at the
> bullseye with shaking hands. Wind (noise) pushes every arrow, whoever shoots.

> **In plain words:** imagine retraining the same kind of model on many different training
> sets. **Bias²** = how far the *average* prediction is from the truth, squared.
> **Variance** = how much individual predictions wobble around that average.
> **Noise** = the part of $y$ that was random to begin with.

### Algebraic derivation

Let $f = f(x)$, $\hat{f} = \hat{f}(x;\mathcal{D})$, $\bar{f} = E[\hat{f}]$. Then:

$$
E[(y-\hat{f})^2] = E[(f+\varepsilon-\hat{f})^2] = E[(f-\hat{f})^2] + \sigma^2
$$

$$
E[(f-\hat{f})^2] = E[(f-\bar{f}+\bar{f}-\hat{f})^2] = (f-\bar{f})^2 + E[(\hat{f}-\bar{f})^2]
$$

The cross term $2(f-\bar{f})E[\bar{f}-\hat{f}] = 0$ since $E[\hat{f}-\bar{f}]=0$.

> **In plain words:** the first line splits off the noise (it's independent of the model, so
> its cross term vanishes too). The second line is the trick from ch. 2's variance formula:
> add and subtract the average prediction $\bar{f}$. The middle term disappears because the
> wobble around an average always averages to zero. ($\mathcal{D}$ is the training dataset.)

## Simulating the decomposition

You can't compute bias and variance from one dataset, but in a simulation you can: generate
500 training sets from a known truth, fit a model to each, and look at how the 500 predictions
spread.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_train, n_test, n_datasets = 40, 200, 500
sigma_noise = 1.5

# True function: sin(x)
x_test = np.linspace(-3, 3, n_test)
f_true = np.sin(x_test)

def fit_poly(x_tr, y_tr, degree):
    coeffs = np.polyfit(x_tr, y_tr, degree)
    return np.polyval(coeffs, x_test)

# Collect predictions across many training datasets
degrees = [1, 3, 9, 15]
results = {}
for d in degrees:
    preds = np.zeros((n_datasets, n_test))
    for i in range(n_datasets):
        x_tr = rng.uniform(-3, 3, n_train)
        y_tr = np.sin(x_tr) + rng.normal(0, sigma_noise, n_train)
        preds[i] = fit_poly(x_tr, y_tr, d)
    results[d] = preds

# Compute bias^2, variance, total at x=0 (index n_test//2)
print(f"{'Degree':<8}  {'Bias²':<10}  {'Variance':<10}  {'Total':<10}  {'Noise'}")
print("-" * 55)
for d, preds in results.items():
    fbar = preds.mean(axis=0)
    bias2 = np.mean((fbar - f_true)**2)
    variance = np.mean(np.var(preds, axis=0))
    total = bias2 + variance + sigma_noise**2
    print(f"{d:<8}  {bias2:<10.4f}  {variance:<10.4f}  {total:<10.4f}  {sigma_noise**2:.4f}")
```

```output
Degree    Bias²       Variance    Total       Noise
-------------------------------------------------------
1         0.1682      0.1353      2.5535      2.2500
3         0.0035      0.2763      2.5298      2.2500
9         0.1130      59.0464     61.4094     2.2500
15        36290.1447  30820265.7802  30856558.1749  2.2500
```

Read across the rows. The straight line (degree 1) is too stiff for a sine wave: high bias
(0.17), low variance. Degree 3 nearly eliminates the bias (0.004) for a small increase in
variance and has the lowest total. Degree 9 is already very wobbly (variance 59), and degree 15
explodes to millions — high-degree polynomials fitted to 40 noisy points swing wildly,
especially near the edges of the range where there are few points to pin them down. Even
the bias of degree 15 is enormous: its average prediction is dragged around by rare, extreme
fits.

Degree 1 (linear): high bias, low variance. Degree 15: low bias in principle, enormous variance in practice. Degree 3 hits near-optimal.

> **Watch out:** the noise floor (2.25 here, $\sigma^2 = 1.5^2$) is the best any model could
> ever do. A test error below it means a bug or a leak, not a great model.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_train, n_datasets = 40, 1000
sigma_noise = 1.5
x_test = np.linspace(-3, 3, 200)
f_true = np.sin(x_test)

degrees = range(1, 16)
bias2_all, var_all, total_all = [], [], []

for d in degrees:
    preds = np.zeros((n_datasets, 200))
    for i in range(n_datasets):
        x_tr = rng.uniform(-3, 3, n_train)
        y_tr = np.sin(x_tr) + rng.normal(0, sigma_noise, n_train)
        preds[i] = np.polyval(np.polyfit(x_tr, y_tr, d), x_test)
    fbar = preds.mean(axis=0)
    bias2_all.append(np.mean((fbar - f_true)**2))
    var_all.append(np.mean(preds.var(axis=0)))
    total_all.append(bias2_all[-1] + var_all[-1] + sigma_noise**2)

best_d = degrees[np.argmin(total_all)]
print(f"Best degree (min total error): {best_d}")
print(f"At degree {best_d}: bias²={bias2_all[best_d-1]:.4f}  var={var_all[best_d-1]:.4f}")
```

```output
Best degree (min total error): 3
At degree 3: bias²=0.0034  var=0.2639
```

Sweeping every degree from 1 to 15 with 1000 training sets each confirms it: the total error
is smallest at degree 3, where bias is almost gone (0.003) and variance is still modest (0.26).
That sweet spot is what **model selection** (Classical ML, Eval & Tuning) tries to find without
being able to run this simulation.

## Bagging reduces variance

If a model is jumpy, train many copies and average them. Their wobbles partly cancel. That's
**bagging** — short for **b**ootstrap **agg**regat**ing** — and it's the idea behind random
forests.

Bootstrap aggregation averages $B$ independent models:

$$
\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}(x) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \hat{f}_b(x)
$$

If models are i.i.d. with variance $\sigma^2_f$: $\text{Var}[\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}] = \sigma^2_f/B$. In practice models are correlated, so variance reduction is partial but still substantial.

> **In plain words:** averaging $B$ independent noisy guesses divides their variance by $B$.
> But bootstrap models are trained on overlapping resamples of the *same* data, so they make
> correlated mistakes, and only part of the variance cancels.

The demo below uses a fully grown **decision tree** — an extremely jumpy model, because one
different training point can change a split and the whole prediction around it.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n_train, n_test = 30, 200
sigma_noise = 1.5
B = 50        # number of bootstrap models
n_sims = 300

x_test  = np.linspace(-3, 3, n_test)
f_true  = np.sin(x_test)

single_preds = np.zeros((n_sims, n_test))
bag_preds    = np.zeros((n_sims, n_test))

for sim in range(n_sims):
    x_tr = rng.uniform(-3, 3, n_train)
    y_tr = np.sin(x_tr) + rng.normal(0, sigma_noise, n_train)

    # Single model: a fully grown tree (low bias, high variance)
    tree = DecisionTreeRegressor().fit(x_tr[:, None], y_tr)
    single_preds[sim] = tree.predict(x_test[:, None])

    # Bagged models (bootstrap resample + fit)
    boot_preds = np.zeros((B, n_test))
    for b in range(B):
        idx = rng.integers(0, n_train, n_train)
        tree_b = DecisionTreeRegressor().fit(x_tr[idx, None], y_tr[idx])
        boot_preds[b] = tree_b.predict(x_test[:, None])
    bag_preds[sim] = boot_preds.mean(axis=0)

var_single = np.mean(single_preds.var(axis=0))
var_bag    = np.mean(bag_preds.var(axis=0))
print(f"Fully grown tree, n_train={n_train}, B={B} bootstrap models")
print(f"Variance (single): {var_single:.4f}")
print(f"Variance (bagged): {var_bag:.4f}")
print(f"Variance reduction: {var_single/var_bag:.2f}×")
```

```output
Fully grown tree, n_train=30, B=50 bootstrap models
Variance (single): 2.2682
Variance (bagged): 1.0897
Variance reduction: 2.08×
```

Averaging 50 bootstrap trees roughly halved the prediction variance (2.27 down to 1.09, a
2.08× reduction) — far from $50\times$, because the trees are correlated, but a large gain for
free. Random forests push further by also randomizing which features each split may use,
which de-correlates the trees.

> **Watch out:** bagging only helps **unstable** models — ones whose predictions change a lot
> when the training data changes slightly (deep trees, high-variance nets). Least-squares fits
> like polynomial regression are *linear* in the data: averaging their bootstrap refits gives
> roughly the same fit back, plus extra noise from the resampling. Try it with
> `np.polyfit` and you'll see bagging make the variance *worse*.

## Recap in plain words

- Expected test error = bias² + variance + irreducible noise.
- Bias: the average model is wrong (too simple). Variance: individual models wobble (too
  sensitive to the training set). Noise: unpredictable even in principle.
- More flexible models trade bias for variance; the best total error is in the middle.
- Bagging averages bootstrap models to cut variance; it works for unstable learners like deep
  trees, not for linear least squares.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_train, n_datasets = 50, 500
sigma_noise = 1.0

# True function: step function (high bias for smooth polynomials)
x_test = np.linspace(-2, 2, 200)
f_true = (x_test > 0).astype(float)  # step at 0

results = {}
for d in [1, 3, 5, 10, 20]:
    preds = np.zeros((n_datasets, 200))
    for i in range(n_datasets):
        x_tr = rng.uniform(-2, 2, n_train)
        y_tr = (x_tr > 0).astype(float) + rng.normal(0, sigma_noise, n_train)
        try:
            preds[i] = np.polyval(np.polyfit(x_tr, y_tr, d), x_test)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            preds[i] = np.nan
    fbar = np.nanmean(preds, axis=0)
    b2 = np.mean((fbar - f_true)**2)
    v  = np.nanmean(np.nanvar(preds, axis=0))
    results[d] = (b2, v, b2+v+sigma_noise**2)
    print(f"degree={d:<3}  bias²={b2:.4f}  var={v:.4f}  total={b2+v+sigma_noise**2:.4f}")
```

```output
degree=1    bias²=0.0626  var=0.0435  total=1.1061
degree=3    bias²=0.0353  var=0.0946  total=1.1299
degree=5    bias²=0.0256  var=0.2164  total=1.2420
degree=10   bias²=0.2406  var=56.5233  total=57.7639
degree=20   bias²=1090590.6202  var=759018593.7378  total=760109185.3580
```

A step function is hard for smooth polynomials: no degree can make a sharp jump, so bias never
reaches zero. Low degrees win here — degree 1 has the lowest total (1.106) because its small
variance outweighs its bias. Degree 10 already wobbles badly, and degree 20 explodes. Try a
decision tree on the same data: a single split at 0 has almost no bias for a step.

## Exercise

Implement the bias-variance decomposition for k-nearest-neighbors regression ($k=1,3,5,15,30$) on $f(x)=\cos(2x)$ with $n=80$ training points and $\sigma=0.5$. Which $k$ minimizes total error?

> **Hint:** in KNN, $k$ works backwards from polynomial degree: small $k$ = flexible (high
> variance), large $k$ = smooth (high bias). Average over many simulated training sets, as in
> the examples above.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
n_tr, n_test, n_sims = 80, 200, 500
sigma = 0.5
x_test = np.linspace(-3, 3, n_test)
f_true = np.cos(2 * x_test)

def knn_predict(x_tr, y_tr, x_te, k):
    preds = np.zeros(len(x_te))
    for i, xt in enumerate(x_te):
        dists = np.abs(x_tr - xt)
        idx   = np.argsort(dists)[:k]
        preds[i] = y_tr[idx].mean()
    return preds

for k in [1, 3, 5, 15, 30]:
    preds = np.zeros((n_sims, n_test))
    for s in range(n_sims):
        x_tr = rng.uniform(-3, 3, n_tr)
        y_tr = np.cos(2*x_tr) + rng.normal(0, sigma, n_tr)
        preds[s] = knn_predict(x_tr, y_tr, x_test, k)
    fbar = preds.mean(axis=0)
    b2 = np.mean((fbar - f_true)**2)
    v  = np.mean(preds.var(axis=0))
    print(f"k={k:<4}  bias²={b2:.4f}  var={v:.4f}  total={b2+v+sigma**2:.4f}")
```

Low $k$ (e.g., 1) gives low bias but very high variance. Optimal $k$ balances the two.
Running it, $k = 5$ wins (total about 0.315): $k = 1$ has almost no bias but variance 0.26;
$k = 30$ averages so many neighbours that it flattens the cosine's peaks, and its bias (0.33)
dominates. The noise floor is $\sigma^2 = 0.25$, so $k = 5$ is within about 0.06 of perfect.
</details>
