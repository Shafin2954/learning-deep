## What probability formalizes

A probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is: 0 means "never",
1 means "certainly", 0.5 means "half the time". If you could repeat the situation forever,
it's the fraction of times the thing would happen.

Probability is a number between 0 and 1 assigned to an event. The assignment must be
consistent: if something certainly happens, it gets 1; if it certainly doesn't, it gets 0;
and the probability of two mutually exclusive events happening is the sum of their
individual probabilities. These three requirements — Kolmogorov's axioms — are all of
probability theory. (**Mutually exclusive** = they can't both happen, like rolling a 2 and a
5 on the same throw.)

Formally: let $\Omega$ be the **sample space** (all possible outcomes). An **event** $A$
is a subset of $\Omega$. A **probability measure** $P$ satisfies:

$$
\text{1. } P(A) \ge 0 \quad \text{2. } P(\Omega) = 1 \quad \text{3. } P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \text{ if } A \cap B = \emptyset
$$

> **In plain words:** (1) no probability is negative; (2) *something* in the list of all
> outcomes must happen, so the whole sample space has probability 1; (3) for two events
> with no outcomes in common ($A \cap B = \emptyset$, "the overlap is empty"), the chance of
> "one or the other" ($A \cup B$) is the sum of their chances.

> **Picture it:** spread exactly one kilogram of sand over a map of all possible outcomes.
> An event is a region of the map, and its probability is how much sand lies in that region.
> Regions that don't overlap hold separate sand, so their amounts add.

Everything else — conditional probability, independence, Bayes — follows from these three.

## Conditional probability

"Given that…" changes the odds. If you know a die came up higher than 3, the chance it's
even is no longer 1/2 — you're now choosing only among 4, 5, and 6. **Conditional
probability** is probability inside that smaller world.

The conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is the probability of $A$ restricted to
outcomes where $B$ already occurred:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0
$$

> **In plain words:** throw away every outcome where $B$ didn't happen. Of the sand that's
> left, what fraction is also in $A$? Dividing by $P(B)$ rescales the leftover sand so it
> totals 1 again. ($P(A \mid B)$ reads "probability of $A$ given $B$".)

Rearranging: $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)$. This is the **multiplication rule**.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 100_000

# Roll a fair die. A = {even}, B = {> 3}
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, N)           # 1..6 uniform
A = rolls % 2 == 0                       # {2, 4, 6}
B = rolls > 3                            # {4, 5, 6}

P_A     = A.mean()
P_B     = B.mean()
P_A_and_B = (A & B).mean()              # {4, 6}
P_A_given_B = P_A_and_B / P_B          # among {4,5,6}, fraction that are even

print(f"P(A)           = {P_A:.4f}   (true 3/6 = 0.5000)")
print(f"P(B)           = {P_B:.4f}   (true 3/6 = 0.5000)")
print(f"P(A and B)     = {P_A_and_B:.4f}   (true 2/6 = 0.3333)")
print(f"P(A|B)         = {P_A_given_B:.4f}   (true 2/3 = 0.6667)")
```

```output
P(A)           = 0.4976   (true 3/6 = 0.5000)
P(B)           = 0.4996   (true 3/6 = 0.5000)
P(A and B)     = 0.3323   (true 2/6 = 0.3333)
P(A|B)         = 0.6651   (true 2/3 = 0.6667)
```

Each simulated estimate sits within a few thousandths of the exact value shown next to it,
which is what 100,000 random rolls buy you. The last line is the point: once you know the
roll is above 3, the chance of "even" jumps from about 1/2 to about 2/3, because two of the
three remaining faces (4 and 6) are even.

> **Watch out:** $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are different things. "The chance a
> professional basketball player is tall" is high; "the chance a tall person is a
> professional basketball player" is tiny. Swapping them is the most common probability
> mistake there is.

## Independence

Two events are **independent** when learning one tells you nothing about the other — like
two separate coin flips.

$A$ and $B$ are **independent** if knowing $B$ tells you nothing about $A$:

$$
P(A \mid B) = P(A) \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)
$$

The second form (factorization) is the operational definition. Use it to test independence:
check whether the joint probability equals the product of the marginals. (A **joint**
probability is "both happen"; a **marginal** is one event on its own.)

In the die example above, "even" and "greater than 3" are *not* independent:
$P(A \cap B) \approx 0.333$ but $P(A) \cdot P(B) \approx 0.25$.

## Law of total probability

To find the chance of something, split the world into cases, work out the chance in each
case, and add them up weighted by how common each case is.

If $B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_k$ partition $\Omega$ (mutually exclusive, exhaustive), then:

$$
P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^k P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i)
$$

> **In plain words:** a **partition** chops the sample space into pieces that don't overlap
> and together cover everything — like sorting people by age group. The overall rate of $A$
> is the average of the per-group rates, weighted by group size.

This is how you **marginalize** a conditional probability into an unconditional one. It
appears in every Bayesian computation.

## Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem flips a conditional around: if you know how likely the evidence is under each
explanation, it tells you how likely each explanation is given the evidence. It's the maths
of changing your mind.

Invert the conditional: given the likelihood $P(A \mid B)$ and the marginals, find
$P(B \mid A)$.

$$
P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{\sum_i P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}
$$

In the ML context: $B$ is a hypothesis (model, class, parameter value); $A$ is data.
$P(B)$ is the **prior**; $P(A \mid B)$ is the **likelihood**; $P(B \mid A)$ is the
**posterior**. The denominator $P(A)$ is the marginal likelihood — it normalizes the
posterior.

> **In plain words:** posterior = (how well this hypothesis explains the data) × (how
> believable it was before), divided by "how likely the data was overall". The denominator
> is the law of total probability, so the posteriors of all hypotheses add to 1.

```python
import numpy as np

# Classic medical test problem:
# Disease prevalence 1%, test sensitivity 99%, specificity 95%.
# Question: given a positive test, what is P(disease)?

P_disease   = 0.01                # prior P(D)
P_pos_given_disease   = 0.99      # sensitivity = P(+|D)
P_pos_given_no_disease = 0.05     # 1 - specificity = P(+|not D)

# Law of total probability for P(+)
P_pos = (P_pos_given_disease * P_disease
         + P_pos_given_no_disease * (1 - P_disease))

# Bayes
P_disease_given_pos = (P_pos_given_disease * P_disease) / P_pos

print(f"P(positive test)     = {P_pos:.4f}")
print(f"P(disease | positive) = {P_disease_given_pos:.4f}")
print(f"  → only {P_disease_given_pos*100:.1f}% chance of disease despite a 99% sensitive test")
```

```output
P(positive test)     = 0.0594
P(disease | positive) = 0.1667
  → only 16.7% chance of disease despite a 99% sensitive test
```

A positive result from a 99%-sensitive test means only a 16.7% chance of disease. Count it
out with 10,000 people: 100 have the disease and 99 of them test positive. Of the 9,900
healthy people, 5% — 495 — also test positive. So 594 people test positive, and only 99 of
them are sick: $99 / 594 = 16.7\%$. The 495 false alarms drown out the 99 true ones.

This counter-intuitive result — the **base-rate fallacy** — follows directly from Bayes.
The low prior (1% prevalence) overwhelms the strong likelihood. This is why ML models
trained on imbalanced classes with many false positives are hard to interpret without
calibration (ch. 15).

> **Picture it:** a smoke alarm that never misses a fire but also beeps at 5% of burnt toast.
> In a house where real fires are rare, most beeps are toast.

## Recap in plain words

- Probabilities follow three rules: never negative, everything together is 1, and
  non-overlapping events add.
- $P(A \mid B)$ is probability inside the world where $B$ happened: $P(A \cap B) / P(B)$.
- Independent means $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$ — knowing one says nothing about the
  other.
- Total probability: split into cases, weight each case's chance by how common it is, add.
- Bayes: posterior $\propto$ likelihood × prior. A rare condition stays fairly unlikely
  even after strong evidence.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np

# Simulate the classic Monty Hall problem and verify P(win|switch) = 2/3.
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 200_000

car   = rng.integers(0, 3, N)     # door hiding the car: 0, 1, or 2
first = rng.integers(0, 3, N)     # contestant's first pick

# Monty opens a door that is not the car and not the first pick.
# If you switch, you win if and only if your first pick was wrong.
win_if_switch = (first != car)
win_if_stay   = (first == car)

print(f"P(win | switch) = {win_if_switch.mean():.4f}  (true = 2/3 ≈ 0.6667)")
print(f"P(win | stay)   = {win_if_stay.mean():.4f}  (true = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333)")
```

```output
P(win | switch) = 0.6658  (true = 2/3 ≈ 0.6667)
P(win | stay)   = 0.3342  (true = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333)
```

In the **Monty Hall** game you pick one of three doors, the host (who knows where the car is)
opens a different door with a goat, and offers you a switch. Switching wins about 2/3 of the
time. The code skips simulating the host entirely, because switching wins exactly when your
first pick was wrong — and that happens 2 times in 3. Try changing to 10 doors with the host
opening 8 goats: what's the switching win rate now?

## Exercise

A factory has three machines: M1 produces 50% of output with 2% defect rate, M2 produces
30% with 3% defect rate, M3 produces 20% with 5% defect rate. A randomly chosen item is
defective. What is the probability it came from M3?

> **Hint:** the machines partition the factory's output. Use the law of total probability
> for the overall defect rate (the denominator), then Bayes for M3.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
# P(defect | Mi) * P(Mi)
p = [0.50, 0.30, 0.20]   # machine fractions
d = [0.02, 0.03, 0.05]   # defect rates

# P(defective) by law of total probability
P_def = sum(pi * di for pi, di in zip(p, d))

# P(M3 | defective)
P_M3_given_def = (p[2] * d[2]) / P_def

print(f"P(defective)       = {P_def:.4f}")
print(f"P(M3 | defective)  = {P_M3_given_def:.4f}")
```

M3 produces 20% of items but contributes disproportionately to defects (5% rate), so the
posterior is higher than the prior — a direct demonstration of Bayes' theorem in quality
control. The numbers: $P(\text{defective}) = 0.010 + 0.009 + 0.010 = 0.029$, and
$P(M3 \mid \text{defective}) = 0.010 / 0.029 \approx 0.345$ — up from a prior of 0.20.
</details>
