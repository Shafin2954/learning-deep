## Why these distributions

A **distribution** is a recipe that says how likely each value is. A few recipes come up
over and over because they describe simple, common situations: one yes/no trial, counting
yeses, waiting for the first yes, counting events per hour. Learn these five and you can
recognize most count data on sight.

A handful of discrete distributions appear everywhere in ML and statistics because they
arise naturally from simple processes: a coin flip, counting successes, counting events in
time. Knowing their shapes, parameters, and relationships lets you match a distribution to
a generative process instantly. (A **generative process** is the story of how the data
came about — "flip a coin 20 times and count heads".)

## Bernoulli$(p)$

One coin flip with a possibly unfair coin: it comes up 1 ("success") with probability $p$
and 0 otherwise. "Did this user click?" is a Bernoulli.

A single binary trial with success probability $p \in [0,1]$.

$$
P(X = 1) = p, \quad P(X = 0) = 1-p
$$
$$
E[X] = p, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = p(1-p)
$$

The building block of everything that follows.

The variance $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = 0.5$ (a fair coin is the least predictable) and
zero at $p = 0$ or $p = 1$ (a coin that always lands the same way has no spread).

## Binomial$(n, p)$

Flip that coin $n$ times and count the 1s. "How many of 20 shown ads get clicked?" is a
Binomial.

Count of successes in $n$ **independent** Bernoulli$(p)$ trials.

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n
$$
$$
E[X] = np, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = np(1-p)
$$

> **In plain words:** $p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$ is the chance of one particular sequence with $k$
> successes and $n - k$ failures; $\binom{n}{k}$ ("$n$ choose $k$") counts how many such
> sequences exist. The mean and variance are just $n$ copies of the Bernoulli's, added up
> (independent variances add, ch. 2).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, p = 20, 0.3

samples = rng.binomial(n, p, size=100_000)
print(f"Sample mean = {samples.mean():.4f}   true E[X] = {n*p:.4f}")
print(f"Sample var  = {samples.var():.4f}   true Var  = {n*p*(1-p):.4f}")

# PMF at k=6 (theoretical and empirical)
k = 6
pmf_true = stats.binom.pmf(k, n, p)
pmf_emp  = (samples == k).mean()
print(f"P(X=6)  true={pmf_true:.4f}  empirical={pmf_emp:.4f}")
```

```output
Sample mean = 5.9951   true E[X] = 6.0000
Sample var  = 4.2146   true Var  = 4.2000
P(X=6)  true=0.1916  empirical=0.1917
```

With $n = 20$ and $p = 0.3$ you expect $20 \times 0.3 = 6$ successes, and the simulation
averages 5.995. The variance $20 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 4.2$ matches too, and the exact
chance of exactly 6 successes (0.1916) agrees with the fraction of simulated runs that hit 6.

The Binomial approximates the Normal when $n$ is large and $p$ not too extreme (the CLT,
ch. 7). The **mode** (the single most likely value) is near $np$; the distribution is
right-skewed for small $p$.

## Geometric$(p)$

Keep flipping until the first success, and count the flips. "How many cold calls until the
first sale?" is Geometric.

Number of trials until the **first** success (inclusive).

$$
P(X = k) = (1-p)^{k-1} p, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
$$
$$
E[X] = \frac{1}{p}, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \frac{1-p}{p^2}
$$

> **In plain words:** to first succeed on trial $k$ you must fail $k - 1$ times, then
> succeed: $(1-p)^{k-1} p$. If each try succeeds with probability $p = 0.2$, you wait
> $1/0.2 = 5$ tries on average.

The Geometric is the **only discrete memoryless distribution**: $P(X > m+k \mid X > m) = P(X > k)$. Waiting times that "forget" their past are Geometric. This is why the number of requests until a server crash (if crashes are independent) is Geometric.

> **Picture it:** a slot machine doesn't remember your losing streak. After 5 losses, your
> chance of waiting 5 more pulls is exactly what it was at the start. "I'm due a win" is the
> gambler's fallacy, and memorylessness is the maths of why.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
p = 0.2
samples = rng.geometric(p, size=200_000)

print(f"E[X]   sample={samples.mean():.4f}   true={1/p:.4f}")
print(f"Var(X) sample={samples.var():.4f}   true={(1-p)/p**2:.4f}")

# Verify memoryless: P(X > 10 | X > 5) should equal P(X > 5)
P_gt5  = (samples > 5).mean()
P_gt10_given_gt5 = (samples > 10).mean() / (samples > 5).mean()
P_gt5_fresh = (samples > 5).mean()
print(f"P(X>5)           = {P_gt5:.4f}")
print(f"P(X>10 | X>5)    = {P_gt10_given_gt5:.4f}  ← should equal P(X>5)")
```

```output
E[X]   sample=5.0002   true=5.0000
Var(X) sample=19.9639   true=20.0000
P(X>5)           = 0.3280
P(X>10 | X>5)    = 0.3282  ← should equal P(X>5)
```

The mean (5.0) and variance (about 20, the formula's $(1 - 0.2)/0.2^2 = 20$) match. The last
two lines show memorylessness: having already waited 5 trials, the chance of needing more
than 5 *more* (0.3282) is the same as the fresh chance of needing more than 5 (0.3280).

## Negative Binomial$(r, p)$

The Geometric waits for one success; the **Negative Binomial** waits for $r$ of them.

Number of trials until the $r$-th success. The Geometric is the special case $r = 1$.

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{k-1}{r-1} p^r (1-p)^{k-r}, \qquad k = r, r+1, \ldots
$$
$$
E[X] = \frac{r}{p}, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \frac{r(1-p)}{p^2}
$$

> **In plain words:** the last trial must be the $r$-th success, and the other $r - 1$
> successes can be anywhere among the first $k - 1$ trials — that's the
> $\binom{k-1}{r-1}$. Waiting for $r$ successes is like waiting for one success $r$ times in
> a row, so the mean and variance are $r$ times the Geometric's.

In practice, the **NegBin distribution** is used for **overdispersed count data** — counts
where the variance exceeds the mean. Poisson requires Var = mean; NegBin allows Var > mean.
This is why NegBin appears in genomics (read counts) and text modeling (word counts).

> **Watch out:** libraries disagree on what NegBin counts. The formula above counts
> *trials*; NumPy's `rng.negative_binomial` and SciPy's `stats.nbinom` count *failures*
> before the $r$-th success, which is $r$ less. Always check the convention.

## Poisson$(\lambda)$

The **Poisson** counts how many times something happens in a fixed window when events are
independent and happen at a steady average rate — emails per hour, typos per page,
customers per minute.

Count of events in a fixed interval when events occur independently at rate $\lambda$.

$$
P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
$$
$$
E[X] = \lambda, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \lambda
$$

Mean equals variance — the defining constraint. If your count data has Var $\gg$ mean,
use NegBin; if Var $\approx$ mean, Poisson is appropriate.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
lam = 4.5

samples = rng.poisson(lam, size=200_000)
print(f"E[X]   sample={samples.mean():.4f}   true={lam}")
print(f"Var(X) sample={samples.var():.4f}   true={lam}  (mean ≈ var)")

# PMF shape: concentrated around lambda, right tail
for k in range(0, 11):
    bar = '#' * int(stats.poisson.pmf(k, lam) * 200)
    print(f"k={k:2d}  {bar}  {stats.poisson.pmf(k, lam):.4f}")
```

```output
E[X]   sample=4.4990   true=4.5
Var(X) sample=4.4763   true=4.5  (mean ≈ var)
k= 0  ##  0.0111
k= 1  #########  0.0500
k= 2  ######################  0.1125
k= 3  #################################  0.1687
k= 4  #####################################  0.1898
k= 5  ##################################  0.1708
k= 6  #########################  0.1281
k= 7  ################  0.0824
k= 8  #########  0.0463
k= 9  ####  0.0232
k=10  ##  0.0104
```

The sample mean and variance both sit near 4.5, as the formula demands. The text bar chart
shows the shape: the peak is at 4 (just below $\lambda = 4.5$), with a longer tail to the right
than the left — counts can't go below 0 but can run high.

### Poisson as limit of Binomial

When $n \to \infty$, $p \to 0$, and $np \to \lambda$, Binomial$(n,p)$ → Poisson$(\lambda)$.
The Poisson is the "rare events" distribution.

> **In plain words:** chop an hour into a million tiny slices, each with a tiny chance of
> holding an event. Counting events is then a Binomial with huge $n$ and tiny $p$ — and that
> Binomial is, to excellent accuracy, a Poisson with $\lambda = np$.

## Recap in plain words

- Bernoulli: one yes/no; mean $p$, variance $p(1-p)$.
- Binomial: count of yeses in $n$ independent tries; mean $np$, variance $np(1-p)$.
- Geometric: tries until the first yes; mean $1/p$; memoryless.
- Negative Binomial: tries until the $r$-th yes; used for counts whose variance exceeds
  their mean. Check whether a library counts trials or failures.
- Poisson: events per window at a steady rate; mean = variance = $\lambda$; the limit of a
  Binomial with many tries and a tiny chance each.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)

# 1) A factory has a 0.5% defect rate per item. In a batch of 1000, what is
#    P(X >= 10)? Use both Binomial and Poisson (lambda = n*p = 5).
n, p = 1000, 0.005
lam = n * p   # = 5.0

print("P(X >= 10):")
print(f"  Binomial: {1 - stats.binom.cdf(9, n, p):.5f}")
print(f"  Poisson : {1 - stats.poisson.cdf(9, lam):.5f}")
print(f"  Simulation: {(rng.binomial(n, p, 500_000) >= 10).mean():.5f}")

# 2) NegBin: number of trials until 3rd success, p=0.4
r_nb, p_nb = 3, 0.4
x_nb = rng.negative_binomial(r_nb, p_nb, 300_000) + r_nb   # NumPy counts failures; + r = trials
print(f"\nNegBin(r=3,p=0.4): E[X]={x_nb.mean():.3f} (true={r_nb/p_nb:.3f})")
```

```output
P(X >= 10):
  Binomial: 0.03147
  Poisson : 0.03183
  Simulation: 0.03169

NegBin(r=3,p=0.4): E[X]=7.491 (true=7.500)
```

The Binomial (exact) and the Poisson (approximation, $\lambda = 1000 \times 0.005 = 5$) differ
by only about 0.0004, and the simulation falls between them — the "rare events" limit
in action. The NegBin line adds $r$ to NumPy's failure count, so the mean lands on the
trials formula $r/p = 7.5$. Try $p = 0.05$ with $n = 100$ (same $\lambda$) and see the
Poisson approximation get worse.

## Exercise

Calls arrive at a call center at rate $\lambda = 8$ per hour. What is the probability that
in any given hour: (a) fewer than 5 calls arrive, (b) exactly 8 calls arrive, (c) more than
15 calls arrive? Use the Poisson PMF/CDF formula, then verify with simulation.

> **Hint:** "fewer than 5" is $P(X \le 4)$, which is `cdf(4)`. "More than 15" is
> $1 - P(X \le 15)$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

lam = 8.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sim = rng.poisson(lam, 500_000)

print(f"P(X < 5)  theory={stats.poisson.cdf(4, lam):.4f}  sim={(sim < 5).mean():.4f}")
print(f"P(X = 8)  theory={stats.poisson.pmf(8, lam):.4f}  sim={(sim == 8).mean():.4f}")
print(f"P(X > 15) theory={1-stats.poisson.cdf(15, lam):.4f}  sim={(sim > 15).mean():.4f}")
```

The Poisson CDF is $F(k) = \sum_{i=0}^k \lambda^i e^{-\lambda} / i!$; `scipy.stats.poisson.cdf`
computes it exactly. For $k \ge 4\lambda$, probabilities become very small — the tail
decays faster than exponential. The off-by-one translations (`< 5` means `cdf(4)`,
`> 15` means `1 - cdf(15)`) are where most mistakes happen with discrete distributions.
</details>
