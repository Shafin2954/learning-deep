## When variance is the signal

Sometimes you can't predict whether tomorrow goes up or down, but you *can* predict how wild
it will be. Stock returns are like that: the direction is close to a coin flip, but calm weeks
follow calm weeks and stormy weeks follow stormy weeks.

Most forecasting models target the conditional mean $E[y_t | \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]$. In
finance and risk management, the target is often the **conditional variance**
$\text{Var}(y_t | \mathcal{F}_{t-1})$ — the volatility. High volatility means wide
prediction intervals and high risk.

$\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ stands for "everything known up to yesterday". **Conditional** means "given
that information". So the conditional variance is "how spread out tomorrow's value is likely
to be, given what you know today". **Volatility** is its square root, the conditional standard
deviation.

**Volatility clustering**: large swings tend to cluster together. After a turbulent day,
the next day is likely to be turbulent too. This violates the i.i.d. residual assumption
of ARIMA — the variance is itself time-varying.

**i.i.d.** means "independent and identically distributed": every shock drawn from the same
distribution, unaffected by the others. ARIMA (ch. 5) assumes its shocks have one fixed
variance forever.

> **Picture it:** weather at sea. You can't say which way the next wave will roll, but if the
> last hour brought big waves, the next hour probably will too. Storms come in spells.

## ARCH(q): autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity

ARCH says: today's risk depends on how big yesterday's surprises were. A big shock yesterday —
up or down, the sign doesn't matter — raises today's variance.

**Heteroskedasticity** means "the variance changes" (the opposite, *homo*skedastic, is
"constant variance").

Engle (1982) modeled the conditional variance as a linear function of past squared
residuals:

$$
\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \quad z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)
$$

> **In plain words:** each shock $\varepsilon_t$ is a standard normal draw $z_t$ (mean 0, std 1)
> stretched by today's volatility $\sigma_t$. On a calm day $\sigma_t$ is small, so the
> shock is small; on a stormy day the same draw becomes a big move.

$$
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1 \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \cdots + \alpha_q \varepsilon_{t-q}^2
$$

> **In plain words:** today's variance = a baseline $\omega$ (omega) + weights $\alpha_i$ times
> the *squared* recent shocks. Squaring makes a drop of 3 and a jump of 3 count the same: 9.

$\omega > 0$, $\alpha_i \ge 0$. A large squared shock at $t-1$ increases $\sigma_t$.

The signs keep the variance positive: a variance can never be negative.

## GARCH(1,1): the workhorse

GARCH adds memory of the variance itself: today's risk is a mix of a baseline, yesterday's
surprise, and yesterday's risk level. That one extra term lets a single model keep storms going
for weeks.

Bollerslev (1986) added a lagged variance term:

$$
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\, \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\, \sigma_{t-1}^2
$$

- $\omega$: the baseline variance that is always there.
- $\alpha\, \varepsilon_{t-1}^2$: the reaction to yesterday's surprise.
- $\beta\, \sigma_{t-1}^2$: the carry-over of yesterday's variance.

This is the most common model. The persistence $\alpha + \beta < 1$ ensures stationarity;
typical values: $\alpha \approx 0.1$, $\beta \approx 0.85$ (high persistence).

**Persistence** is the share of today's extra variance that survives into tomorrow. When it is
below 1, every storm eventually calms down to the **unconditional variance**
$\omega / (1 - \alpha - \beta)$, the long-run average level. With the values below that is
$0.02 / 0.05 = 0.4$, so the long-run std is about 0.632.

> **Picture it:** a thermostat with a slow heater. A cold blast ($\varepsilon^2$) knocks the
> room temperature away, and because the heater is slow ($\beta$ near 1), the room takes a long
> time to drift back to its set point ($\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)$).

```python
import numpy as np
from arch import arch_model

rng   = np.random.default_rng(42)
n     = 500
omega = 0.02
alpha = 0.10
beta  = 0.85

# Simulate GARCH(1,1)
eps   = np.zeros(n)
sigma = np.zeros(n)
sigma[0] = np.sqrt(omega / (1 - alpha - beta))   # unconditional std
for t in range(1, n):
    sigma[t] = np.sqrt(omega + alpha*eps[t-1]**2 + beta*sigma[t-1]**2)
    eps[t]   = sigma[t] * rng.normal(0, 1)

print("Simulated returns statistics:")
print(f"  Mean:     {eps.mean():.4f}")
print(f"  Std:      {eps.std():.4f}")
print(f"  Kurtosis: {float(np.mean((eps - eps.mean())**4) / eps.std()**4):.2f}  (>3 = fat tails)")
```

```output
Simulated returns statistics:
  Mean:     -0.0091
  Std:      0.5629
  Kurtosis: 3.79  (>3 = fat tails)
```

The mean is $-0.0091$, essentially 0, and the std is 0.5629, a little under the theoretical
0.632 (500 points is a short sample for such a persistent process). The interesting number is
the kurtosis, 3.79. **Kurtosis** measures how heavy the tails are; a normal distribution has
exactly 3.

Excess kurtosis (kurtosis > 3) is the fingerprint of GARCH: the unconditional distribution has
fatter tails than Normal because periods of high variance generate extreme observations.

Every single shock here *is* normal, yet the mixture of calm and stormy days produces more
extreme values than one normal distribution would.

> **Watch out:** "kurtosis" and "excess kurtosis" are different numbers. Excess kurtosis is
> kurtosis minus 3, so a normal distribution has excess 0. `scipy.stats.kurtosis` returns the
> excess version by default.

## Fitting GARCH with the `arch` library

Fitting reverses the simulation: given the returns, find the $\omega$, $\alpha$, $\beta$
that make the observed sequence of calm and stormy days most likely.

The `arch` library does this by **maximum likelihood** — it searches for the parameters under
which the data would have been most probable. The code multiplies returns by 100 (percent
returns) because the optimiser works better when numbers are not tiny.

```python
import numpy as np
from arch import arch_model

rng   = np.random.default_rng(42)
n     = 500; omega = 0.02; alpha = 0.10; beta = 0.85
eps   = np.zeros(n); sigma = np.zeros(n)
sigma[0] = np.sqrt(omega / (1 - alpha - beta))
for t in range(1, n):
    sigma[t] = np.sqrt(omega + alpha*eps[t-1]**2 + beta*sigma[t-1]**2)
    eps[t]   = sigma[t] * rng.normal(0, 1)

# Scale to percent returns
returns = eps * 100

model  = arch_model(returns, vol='GARCH', p=1, q=1, dist='normal')
result = model.fit(disp='off')

print(result.params[['omega','alpha[1]','beta[1]']].round(4).to_string())
print(f"\nTrue:  omega={omega:.4f}  alpha={alpha:.4f}  beta={beta:.4f}")
print(f"Log-likelihood: {result.loglikelihood:.1f}")
```

```output
omega       493.2507
alpha[1]      0.1695
beta[1]       0.6766

True:  omega=0.0200  alpha=0.1000  beta=0.8500
Log-likelihood: -2705.7
```

The estimates are rough: $\hat\alpha = 0.1695$ (true 0.10) and $\hat\beta = 0.6766$ (true 0.85),
so the fitted persistence is 0.85 instead of 0.95. You might hope for estimates like
$\hat\alpha \approx 0.096$ and $\hat\beta \approx 0.862$, within a hair of the truth; 500 points
do not deliver that.
`omega` is scaled by $100^2$ because we multiplied returns by 100: the true value on this scale is
$0.02 \times 100^2 = 200$, and the estimate is 493.

This is not a scaling problem. Fitting the same data unscaled or times 10 gives the same
$\hat\alpha$ and $\hat\beta$. It is sample size: re-running the simulation with 5000 points
recovers $\hat\alpha \approx 0.097$ and $\hat\beta \approx 0.83$. Persistence is hard to pin
down because a few big storms carry most of the information.

> **Watch out:** GARCH parameters are noisy with a few hundred observations. Use thousands of
> daily points where you can, and look at the standard errors in `result.summary()` before
> trusting a single estimate.

## Conditional volatility and VaR

Once fitted, the model gives a volatility estimate for every day. Turn that into a risk number:
"on 95% of days you will not lose more than this".

The key output is the **conditional standard deviation** $\hat\sigma_t$ — the model's
estimate of the current volatility.

**VaR** (Value at Risk) at 95% is the loss you exceed on only 5% of days. If returns are normal
with std $\hat\sigma_t$, that threshold is $-1.645\,\hat\sigma_t$, because 5% of a normal
distribution lies below $-1.645$ standard deviations. A **violation** is a day when the real
return falls below the VaR line.

```python
import numpy as np
from arch import arch_model

rng   = np.random.default_rng(42)
n     = 500; omega = 0.02; alpha = 0.10; beta = 0.85
eps   = np.zeros(n); sigma = np.zeros(n)
sigma[0] = np.sqrt(omega/(1-alpha-beta))
for t in range(1, n):
    sigma[t] = np.sqrt(omega + alpha*eps[t-1]**2 + beta*sigma[t-1]**2)
    eps[t]   = sigma[t] * rng.normal(0, 1)
returns = eps * 100

result = arch_model(returns, vol='GARCH', p=1, q=1).fit(disp='off')
cond_vol = result.conditional_volatility   # σ̂_t series

# 1-day 95% VaR: lose more than this with 5% probability
var_95 = -1.645 * cond_vol   # Normal quantile

violations = (returns < var_95).mean()
print(f"Conditional vol — mean: {cond_vol.mean():.3f}  max: {cond_vol.max():.3f}")
print(f"True vol        — mean: {sigma.mean()*100:.3f}  max: {sigma.max()*100:.3f}")
print(f"VaR 95% violations: {violations:.3f}  (target: 0.050)")
```

```output
Conditional vol — mean: 55.186  max: 132.715
True vol        — mean: 57.733  max: 116.411
VaR 95% violations: 0.052  (target: 0.050)
```

The model's volatility averages 55.186 against a true 57.733, and its peak is 132.715 against
a true 116.411: the low $\hat\beta$ makes it react harder to single shocks. The violation rate
is 0.052, i.e. 26 days out of 500 dipped below the VaR line, against 25 expected.

VaR violation rate $\approx$ 5.2%, close to the nominal 5% — the model is reasonably calibrated.

So even with imperfect parameters, the *volatility path* is good enough for honest risk bands.

> **Watch out:** this check is in-sample: the model was fitted on the same days it is scored
> on. A real VaR backtest refits on past data only and scores the next day (ch. 6). And with
> fat-tailed real data, normal quantiles understate extreme losses; `dist='t'` (Student's t) is
> a common fix.

## Recap in plain words

- Volatility is the predictable part of risk: big moves cluster, so variance changes over time.
- ARCH makes today's variance depend on recent squared shocks.
- GARCH(1,1) adds yesterday's variance; $\alpha + \beta$ is the persistence and must be below 1.
- GARCH processes have fat tails (kurtosis above 3) even when every shock is normal.
- Parameter estimates are noisy on a few hundred points, but the fitted volatility path is still useful.
- VaR turns the volatility into a loss threshold; check that violations happen at the promised rate.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np
from arch import arch_model

rng   = np.random.default_rng(7)
n     = 400
# High persistence GARCH: alpha+beta=0.98
omega_h = 0.005; alpha_h = 0.08; beta_h = 0.90
eps   = np.zeros(n); sigma = np.zeros(n)
sigma[0] = np.sqrt(omega_h / (1 - alpha_h - beta_h))
for t in range(1, n):
    sigma[t] = np.sqrt(omega_h + alpha_h*eps[t-1]**2 + beta_h*sigma[t-1]**2)
    eps[t]   = sigma[t] * rng.normal(0, 1)
returns = eps * 100

result = arch_model(returns, vol='GARCH', p=1, q=1).fit(disp='off')
params = result.params
print(f"alpha+beta = {params['alpha[1]'] + params['beta[1]']:.4f}  (true: {alpha_h+beta_h:.2f})")
print(f"Half-life of volatility shock: {-np.log(2)/np.log(params['alpha[1]']+params['beta[1]']):.1f} days")
```

```output
alpha+beta = 0.8402  (true: 0.98)
Half-life of volatility shock: 4.0 days
```

The fit badly underestimates persistence: 0.8402 instead of 0.98. The **half-life** is how many
days a volatility shock takes to shrink by half, $-\ln 2 / \ln(\alpha + \beta)$. The model says
4.0 days; the truth is $-\ln 2 / \ln 0.98 \approx 34$ days. With $\alpha + \beta$ this close to
1, 400 points contain only a handful of storms, and the model can't tell "very slow decay" from
"medium decay".

Raise `n` to 4000 and re-run: the estimate should move much closer to 0.98 and the half-life
should grow accordingly. Then try `alpha_h = 0.02; beta_h = 0.97` to see an even harder case.

Simulate GARCH(1,1) with `alpha=0.15, beta=0.80`. Fit the model and compute the 99% VaR

```python
import numpy as np
from arch import arch_model

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n   = 600; omega = 0.01; alpha = 0.15; beta = 0.80
eps = np.zeros(n); sigma = np.zeros(n)
sigma[0] = np.sqrt(omega/(1-alpha-beta))
for t in range(1, n):
    sigma[t] = np.sqrt(omega + alpha*eps[t-1]**2 + beta*sigma[t-1]**2)
    eps[t]   = sigma[t] * rng.normal(0, 1)
returns = eps * 100

result  = arch_model(returns, vol='GARCH', p=1, q=1).fit(disp='off')
vol     = result.conditional_volatility
var_99  = -2.326 * vol
viols   = (returns < var_99).mean()
print(f"99% VaR violations: {viols:.3f}  (target: 0.010)")
print(f"alpha: {result.params['alpha[1]']:.3f}  beta: {result.params['beta[1]']:.3f}")
```

Running it prints a violation rate of 0.012 (7 of 600 days) against a target of 0.010, and
estimates $\hat\alpha = 0.164$, $\hat\beta = 0.783$ — close to the true 0.15 and 0.80 this
time. At 99% you expect only 6 violations in 600 days, so 7 is well within luck. Testing VaR
at extreme levels needs long histories: one extra violation moves the rate by 0.17 percentage
points.
