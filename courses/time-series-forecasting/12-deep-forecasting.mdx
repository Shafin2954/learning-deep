## Why deep models for time series

Classical models (ARIMA, ETS, gradient boosting with lags) handle one series at a time — they're **local** models. Deep forecasting models are **global**: a single model is trained across many related series simultaneously and learns shared patterns (trend shapes, seasonality profiles, response to external variables). This gives them leverage that per-series models lack.

Three situations where deep models consistently win:

- **Many series** (thousands of SKUs, sensor streams, store-item pairs) — the global model borrows strength across series.
- **Rich covariates** — promotions, holidays, weather, categorical metadata that ARIMA can't easily absorb.
- **Complex, long-range seasonality** — multiple overlapping periods that seasonal ARIMA struggles to parameterize.

The tradeoff: they require substantially more data, are harder to debug, and benefit from GPU training (the small models here run on CPU). For a single short series with no covariates, classical methods usually win.

## DeepAR: probabilistic autoregressive LSTM

DeepAR (Salinas et al., 2020 — Amazon) models each time series as:

$$
y_{t,i} \sim p_\theta(y \mid \mathbf{h}_{t,i}), \quad \mathbf{h}_{t,i} = \text{LSTM}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1,i},\ y_{t-1,i},\ \mathbf{x}_{t,i})
$$

where $i$ indexes the series, $\mathbf{x}_{t,i}$ are covariates (age of series, calendar features, item embeddings), and $p_\theta$ is a parametric distribution — typically Gaussian (for real-valued) or Negative Binomial (for count data like demand).

Training: maximize log-likelihood of the observed values given the LSTM hidden state. No separate decoder — it autoregressively conditions on previous actuals during training and on its own samples during inference.

The libraries that package these models (`neuralforecast`, GluonTS, PyTorch Forecasting) are not part of this course's environment, so every model below is a small PyTorch implementation you can read in full.
```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.set_num_threads(1)

Y = df.pivot(index="ds", columns="unique_id", values="y").values.T      # (5, 72)
train, L, H = 60, 24, 12                                                # train length, lookback, horizon
mu_s, sd_s = Y[:, :train].mean(1, keepdims=True), Y[:, :train].std(1, keepdims=True)
Ys = torch.tensor((Y - mu_s) / sd_s, dtype=torch.float32)

# Known-future covariates: month of year as sin/cos

CAL = torch.tensor(np.stack([np.sin(2*np.pi*month/12), np.cos(2*np.pi*month/12)], 1), dtype=torch.float32)
print("panel:", tuple(Ys.shape), " train:", train, " holdout:", Ys.shape[1] - train)
```

Below, a minimal DeepAR: an LSTM that sees the previous value, the calendar covariates, and a learned series embedding, and outputs the mean and scale of a Gaussian for the next value. Training minimizes the Gaussian negative log-likelihood; forecasting samples 200 paths autoregressively.

```python
class MiniDeepAR(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_series, hidden=32, emb=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb   = nn.Embedding(n_series, emb)
        self.rnn   = nn.LSTM(1 + 2 + emb, hidden, batch_first=True)
        self.mu    = nn.Linear(hidden, 1)
        self.sigma = nn.Linear(hidden, 1)
    def forward(self, y_prev, cal, sid):
        e = self.emb(sid).unsqueeze(1).expand(-1, y_prev.shape[1], -1)
        h, _ = self.rnn(torch.cat([y_prev.unsqueeze(-1), cal, e], -1))
        return self.mu(h).squeeze(-1), nn.functional.softplus(self.sigma(h)).squeeze(-1) + 1e-3

ctx = 24
xs, ys, ids, cs = [], [], [], []
for i in range(5):
    for s in range(0, train - ctx):
        w = Ys[i, s:s + ctx + 1]
        xs.append(w[:-1]); ys.append(w[1:]); ids.append(i); cs.append(CAL[s + 1:s + ctx + 1])
xs, ys, ids, cs = torch.stack(xs), torch.stack(ys), torch.tensor(ids), torch.stack(cs)

torch.manual_seed(0)
model = MiniDeepAR(5)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.01)
for step in range(400):
    mu, sg = model(xs, cs, ids)
    loss = -torch.distributions.Normal(mu, sg).log_prob(ys).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 100 == 0: print(f"step {step:3d}  NLL {loss.item():.3f}")
print(f"final NLL {loss.item():.3f}")

@torch.no_grad()
def sample_paths(model, hist, sid, start, steps, n=200):
    y, sid = hist.repeat(n, 1), sid.repeat(n)
    for k in range(steps):
        cal = CAL[start - hist.shape[0] + 1 : start - hist.shape[0] + 1 + y.shape[1]].unsqueeze(0).expand(n, -1, -1)
        mu, sg = model(y, cal, sid)
        nxt = mu[:, -1] + sg[:, -1] * torch.randn(n)
        y = torch.cat([y, nxt.unsqueeze(1)], 1)
    return y[:, -steps:]

torch.manual_seed(1)
cov, mae = [], []
for i in range(5):
    paths = sample_paths(model, Ys[i, train-ctx:train], torch.tensor(i), train, 12)
    lo, hi = paths.quantile(0.1, 0), paths.quantile(0.9, 0)
    act = Ys[i, train:]
    cov.append(((act >= lo) & (act <= hi)).float().mean().item())
    mae.append((paths.mean(0) - act).abs().mean().item())
sn = np.mean([(Ys[i, train-12:train] - Ys[i, train:]).abs().mean().item() for i in range(5)])
print(f"DeepAR mean-path MAE (scaled units): {np.mean(mae):.3f}")
print(f"Seasonal-naive MAE:                  {sn:.3f}")
print(f"80% interval coverage: {np.mean(cov):.2f}")
```

```output
step   0  NLL 1.529
step 100  NLL 0.031
step 200  NLL -0.426
step 300  NLL -0.099
final NLL -0.704
DeepAR mean-path MAE (scaled units): 0.606
Seasonal-naive MAE:                  0.640
80% interval coverage: 0.13
```

DeepAR forecasts a *distribution* per step, letting you sample many trajectories and derive any quantile. The coverage printed above is far below the nominal 80%: with 5 short series the model is overconfident, which is exactly why calibration must always be checked rather than assumed. Its point forecast barely beats seasonal naive here.

## N-BEATS: backward and forward residual blocks

N-BEATS (Oreshkin et al., 2020) is fully feed-forward (no recurrence, no attention). Each **block** does:

1. Takes a backcast window as input.
2. Produces two outputs via separate linear projections: a **backcast** (fit to the past window) and a **forecast** (h steps ahead).
3. The residual passed to the next block = input − backcast.

Stacking blocks removes structure layer by layer, so each successive block explains the residual of the previous one. Two stack configurations:

- **Generic** — unconstrained basis; best MSE for data without clear decomposition.
- **Interpretable** — polynomial basis for trend blocks, Fourier basis for seasonality blocks; you can read off the components.

The generic version needs about twenty lines of PyTorch. The final forecast is the sum of every block's forecast.

```python
class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, L, H, width=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.fc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(L, width), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(width, width), nn.ReLU())
        self.back = nn.Linear(width, L)
        self.fore = nn.Linear(width, H)
    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.fc(x)
        return self.back(h), self.fore(h)

class MiniNBEATS(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, L, H, n_blocks=3):
        super().__init__()
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([Block(L, H) for _ in range(n_blocks)])
    def forward(self, x):
        resid, forecast = x, 0
        for b in self.blocks:
            back, fore = b(resid)
            resid = resid - back          # remove what this block explained
            forecast = forecast + fore    # forecasts add up
        return forecast

X, T = [], []
for i in range(5):
    for s in range(0, train - L - H + 1):
        X.append(Ys[i, s:s+L]); T.append(Ys[i, s+L:s+L+H])
X, T = torch.stack(X), torch.stack(T)

torch.manual_seed(0)
nb = MiniNBEATS(L, H)
print("parameters:", sum(p.numel() for p in nb.parameters()))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(nb.parameters(), lr=0.003)
for step in range(500):
    loss = (nb(X) - T).abs().mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print(f"train MAE:   {loss.item():.3f}")
with torch.no_grad():
    pred = nb(Ys[:, train-L:train])
print(f"holdout MAE: {(pred - Ys[:, train:]).abs().mean().item():.3f}")
```

```output
parameters: 24300
train MAE:   0.032
holdout MAE: 0.575
```

The gap between training and holdout error is the signature of a deep model fed too little data: 24k parameters against only 125 training windows. With thousands of series the gap closes; with five it overfits.

**N-HiTS** (Neural Hierarchical Interpolation for Time Series) extends N-BEATS with multi-rate sampling: different blocks look at different resolutions (coarse, medium, fine) before interpolating back to the full resolution. It often outperforms N-BEATS on longer horizons and is faster to train.

## Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT)

TFT (Lim et al., 2021 — Google) is the most feature-rich architecture:

- **Variable selection networks** — learned attention per input variable (which covariate matters for this series?).
- **Static covariate encoder** — integrates fixed metadata (store type, item category).
- **LSTM encoder–decoder** — local processing of past and known-future inputs.
- **Multi-head attention** on the sequence — captures long-range dependencies.
- **Quantile output** — directly predicts $[q_{0.1}, q_{0.5}, q_{0.9}]$ (or any quantiles) via a single forward pass.

The variable importance weights are inspectable: you can see which lags, which calendar features, and which external regressors the model is relying on for each series.

A heavily simplified TFT-style model keeps three of those ideas: a static series embedding, an LSTM followed by multi-head attention, and a direct multi-horizon quantile head trained with pinball loss. The variable selection networks and the decoder are omitted.

```python
class MiniTFT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, L, H, n_series, hidden=32, n_q=3):
        super().__init__()
        self.H, self.n_q = H, n_q
        self.emb  = nn.Embedding(n_series, hidden)          # static covariate
        self.inp  = nn.Linear(1 + 2, hidden)                # value + calendar
        self.lstm = nn.LSTM(hidden, hidden, batch_first=True)
        self.attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(hidden, 4, batch_first=True)
        self.out  = nn.Linear(hidden, H * n_q)
    def forward(self, x, cal, sid):
        h = self.inp(torch.cat([x.unsqueeze(-1), cal], -1)) + self.emb(sid).unsqueeze(1)
        h, _ = self.lstm(h)
        a, w = self.attn(h, h, h)
        return self.out((h + a)[:, -1]).view(-1, self.H, self.n_q), w

def pinball(pred, y, qs=(0.1, 0.5, 0.9)):
    q = torch.tensor(qs)
    e = y.unsqueeze(-1) - pred
    return torch.maximum(q * e, (q - 1) * e).mean()

XC, IDS = [], []
for i in range(5):
    for s in range(0, train - L - H + 1):
        XC.append(CAL[s:s+L]); IDS.append(i)
XC, IDS = torch.stack(XC), torch.tensor(IDS)

torch.manual_seed(0)
tft = MiniTFT(L, H, 5)
print("parameters:", sum(p.numel() for p in tft.parameters()))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(tft.parameters(), lr=0.005)
for step in range(300):
    out, _ = tft(X, XC, IDS)
    loss = pinball(out, T)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print(f"train pinball:   {loss.item():.3f}")

with torch.no_grad():
    out, w = tft(Ys[:, train-L:train], CAL[train-L:train].unsqueeze(0).expand(5, -1, -1), torch.arange(5))
act = Ys[:, train:]
cov = ((act >= out[..., 0]) & (act <= out[..., 2])).float().mean().item()
print(f"holdout pinball: {pinball(out, act).item():.3f}")
print(f"[q10, q90] coverage: {cov:.2f}  (nominal 0.80)")
print("attention-weight matrix shape:", tuple(w.shape))
```

```output
parameters: 14148
train pinball:   0.053
holdout pinball: 0.196
[q10, q90] coverage: 0.30  (nominal 0.80)
attention-weight matrix shape: (5, 24, 24)
```

The attention matrix `w` (one 24-by-24 map per series) is what TFT exposes for interpretation: which historical timestamps the model attends to when forecasting. The same small-data caveat applies: the quantile band undercovers on the holdout.

## When to use deep forecasting models

Choose deep models when:

- You have **≥ 100 series** (ideally thousands) or very long individual series (> 5 years daily).
- You have **rich time-varying covariates** (promotions, price, weather, related series).
- You need a **probabilistic output** natively (inventory/safety-stock decisions).
- **Seasonality is complex** or multiple overlapping (weekly + annual + holiday).

Stick to classical/gradient boosting when:

- You have **few series** or **short history** (< 200 observations).
- You need **fast iteration** and interpretability for stakeholders.
- You're in a **low-data domain** where overfitting is a real risk.

```python
# Toy global forecasting: an N-BEATS-style MLP on 3 synthetic series, horizon = 6
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.set_num_threads(1)
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
series = []
for phase in [0, np.pi/3, 2*np.pi/3]:
    series.append(10 + 5*np.sin(2*np.pi*np.arange(60)/12 + phase) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, 60))
S = torch.tensor(np.stack(series), dtype=torch.float32)          # (3, 60)

L, H = 24, 6
X = torch.stack([S[i, s:s+L]   for i in range(3) for s in range(0, 60 - L - H + 1)])
T = torch.stack([S[i, s+L:s+L+H] for i in range(3) for s in range(0, 60 - L - H + 1)])

torch.manual_seed(0)
net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(L, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, H))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=0.003)
for step in range(500):
    loss = (net(X) - T).abs().mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    pred = net(S[:, -L:]).numpy()
dates = pd.date_range("2024-01", periods=H, freq="ME")
out = pd.DataFrame({f"s{i}": pred[i] for i in range(3)}, index=dates).round(2)
print(out)
```

## Under the Trench

Rare and advanced topics you should at least know the name of. You won't need them daily,
but you'll stop being surprised when you meet them.

- **State-space models & Kalman filter** — recursive estimation behind ETS and structural time series; the theoretically clean alternative to ARIMA with an exact likelihood.
- **Prophet** — additive trend + seasonality + holidays with robust defaults; useful when you need fast, explainable forecasts with minimal tuning.
- **Hierarchical forecasting & reconciliation** — make bottom-up and top-down forecasts agree; MinT reconciliation ensures coherency across aggregation levels.
- **Dynamic time warping** — align or compare series of different speeds; used in kNN classifiers and clustering for series.
- **Change-point & anomaly detection** — find regime shifts with PELT, BOCPD, or Isolation Forest on residuals; critical for adaptive retraining triggers.
- **Intermittent demand (Croston's method)** — forecasting sparse, spiky series where zero demand is common; splits frequency and size into separate models.
- **Spectral analysis / Fourier & wavelets** — frequency-domain features; Fourier terms as regressors in SARIMAX capture complex multi-period seasonality.
- **Global vs local models** — one model across many series (global) vs one per series (local); the tradeoff is data efficiency vs overfitting to individual patterns.
