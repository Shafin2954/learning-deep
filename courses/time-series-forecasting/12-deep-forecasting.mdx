## Why deep models for time series

Every model so far learned from one series at a time. A deep forecasting model can learn from
thousands of series at once — every product in a shop — and notice that "Christmas spikes"
look similar across many of them. A new product with little history then benefits from what
the others taught.

Classical models (ARIMA, ETS, gradient boosting with lags) handle one series at a time — they're **local** models. Deep forecasting models are **global**: a single model is trained across many related series simultaneously and learns shared patterns (trend shapes, seasonality profiles, response to external variables). This gives them leverage that per-series models lack.

**ETS** is the exponential-smoothing family from ch. 4 (Error, Trend, Seasonal). **Local**
means one model per series; **global** means one model shared by all series.

> **Picture it:** a local model is a private tutor who has only ever taught one pupil. A global
> model is a teacher who has taught a thousand pupils: she has seen most mistakes before and
> recognises a pattern from one pupil in another. She may know any single pupil less
> intimately, though.

Three situations where deep models consistently win:

- **Many series** (thousands of SKUs, sensor streams, store-item pairs) — the global model borrows strength across series.
- **Rich covariates** — promotions, holidays, weather, categorical metadata that ARIMA can't easily absorb.
- **Complex, long-range seasonality** — multiple overlapping periods that seasonal ARIMA struggles to parameterize.

A **SKU** (stock-keeping unit) is one sellable item, like "red T-shirt, size M". **Borrows
strength** means the patterns learned from many series help each individual one.

The tradeoff: they require substantially more data, are harder to debug, and usually want a GPU for training at scale. For a single short series with no covariates, classical methods usually win.

## DeepAR: probabilistic autoregressive LSTM

DeepAR reads a series one step at a time with a memory cell, and at each step it outputs not one
number but a whole probability distribution for the next value. Sampling from those
distributions gives you many possible futures, and from them any interval you like.

DeepAR (Salinas et al., 2020 — Amazon) models each time series as:

$$
y_{t,i} \sim p_\theta(y \mid \mathbf{h}_{t,i}), \quad \mathbf{h}_{t,i} = \text{LSTM}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1,i},\ y_{t-1,i},\ \mathbf{x}_{t,i})
$$

- $y_{t,i}$ is the value of series $i$ at time $t$.
- $\mathbf{h}_{t,i}$ is the **hidden state**: the LSTM's running memory summary of everything
  it has read so far in series $i$.
- An **LSTM** (long short-term memory network) is a recurrent neural network: it updates its
  memory from the previous memory, the last value $y_{t-1,i}$ and the covariates
  $\mathbf{x}_{t,i}$.
- $p_\theta$ turns the memory into a probability distribution with parameters $\theta$ learned
  in training; the $\sim$ means "is drawn from".

where $i$ indexes the series, $\mathbf{x}_{t,i}$ are covariates (age of series, calendar features, item embeddings), and $p_\theta$ is a parametric distribution — typically Gaussian (for real-valued) or Negative Binomial (for count data like demand).

An **embedding** is a learned list of numbers that represents a category (an item, a store),
so similar items end up with similar lists. The **Negative Binomial** distribution only produces
whole numbers $\ge 0$ and allows a lot of spread, which suits counts like "units sold".

Training: maximize log-likelihood of the observed values given the LSTM hidden state. No separate decoder — it autoregressively conditions on previous actuals during training and on its own samples during inference.

So training uses the real previous value (**teacher forcing**), while forecasting feeds the
model its own sampled guess — the recursive strategy from ch. 8, run many times with random
draws.

> **Watch out:** the training/inference mismatch is real. During training the model never sees
> its own mistakes, so long horizons can drift. Checking calibration (ch. 11) on a held-out
> period is not optional.

Before any model, the data has to be in the shape these libraries expect: one long table with
a series ID, a date and a value.

```python
# Minimal DeepAR-style training loop using neuralforecast
# (shows API; real training needs enough series and epochs)
from neuralforecast import NeuralForecast
from neuralforecast.models import DeepAR
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# neuralforecast expects long-format: unique_id, ds, y
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
records = []
for uid in range(5):
    dates = pd.date_range("2020-01", periods=48, freq="ME")
    trend = np.linspace(uid * 2, uid * 2 + 20, 48)
    noise = rng.normal(0, 1, 48)
    for d, v in zip(dates, trend + noise):
        records.append({"unique_id": f"series_{uid}", "ds": d, "y": float(v)})

df = pd.DataFrame(records)
print(df.head(4))
print(f"\nSeries: {df['unique_id'].nunique()}, rows per series: {df.groupby('unique_id').size().iloc[0]}")
```

```output
  unique_id         ds         y
0  series_0 2020-01-31  0.125730
1  series_0 2020-02-29  0.293427
2  series_0 2020-03-31  1.491486
3  series_0 2020-04-30  1.381496

Series: 5, rows per series: 48
```

This is **long format**: one row per (series, date) pair, with columns `unique_id`, `ds`
(the date stamp) and `y`. Five series of 48 months gives 240 rows. The first rows of
`series_0` start near 0 (0.125730, 0.293427, …) because its trend runs from 0 to 20; series 4
runs from 8 to 28. The code only prepares data — it imports `DeepAR` but does not train it.

DeepAR forecasts a *distribution* per step, letting you sample many trajectories and derive any quantile. This makes it naturally probabilistic — no conformal post-processing needed, though calibration should still be checked.

## N-BEATS: backward and forward residual blocks

N-BEATS is a stack of plain neural layers. Each block looks at the recent past, explains as much
of it as it can, hands the unexplained part to the next block, and adds its own piece to the
forecast.

N-BEATS (Oreshkin et al., 2020) is fully feed-forward (no recurrence, no attention). Each **block** does:

1. Takes a backcast window as input.
2. Produces two outputs via separate linear projections: a **backcast** (fit to the past window) and a **forecast** (h steps ahead).
3. The residual passed to the next block = input minus backcast.

**Feed-forward** means data flows straight through the layers, with no loop and no memory. The
**backcast window** is the stretch of recent history the block looks at (`input_size`). The
final forecast is the *sum* of every block's forecast piece.

> **Picture it:** a team of sculptors working on one block of marble. The first knocks off the
> big shape (the trend), the next carves the repeating pattern (the season), the next the fine
> detail. Each works only on what the previous one left, and the statue is the sum of their
> work.

Stacking blocks removes structure layer by layer, so each successive block explains the residual of the previous one. Two stack configurations:

- **Generic** — unconstrained basis; best MSE for data without clear decomposition.
- **Interpretable** — polynomial basis for trend blocks, Fourier basis for seasonality blocks; you can read off the components.

A **basis** is the set of shapes a block may combine. A **polynomial** basis means smooth curves
($1, t, t^2$); a **Fourier** basis means sine and cosine waves. Restricting a block to waves
forces it to model seasonality, so you can read its output as "the seasonal component".

```python
from neuralforecast.models import NBEATS

model = NBEATS(
    h=12,                   # horizon
    input_size=24,          # lookback window
    stack_types=["trend", "seasonality"],  # interpretable config
    n_blocks=[3, 3],
    mlp_units=[[256, 256], [256, 256]],
    n_harmonics=2,
    n_polynomials=2,
    max_steps=100,
    val_check_steps=10,
)
print(model)
```

```output
NBEATS
```

`print(model)` only shows the class name, NBEATS: the model is built but not trained. The
settings say: forecast 12 steps (`h=12`) from a 24-step lookback (`input_size=24`); one trend
stack and one seasonality stack, 3 blocks each; `n_polynomials=2` lets the trend blocks use
curves up to degree 2, and `n_harmonics=2` controls how many sine/cosine waves the seasonality
blocks get.

**N-HiTS** (Neural Hierarchical Interpolation for Time Series) extends N-BEATS with multi-rate sampling: different blocks look at different resolutions (coarse, medium, fine) before interpolating back to the full resolution. It often outperforms N-BEATS on longer horizons and is faster to train.

**Multi-rate sampling** means some blocks see a shrunk, low-detail copy of the history (e.g.
every 4th point) to catch slow movements cheaply, while others see full detail for fast ones.

## Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT)

TFT is the "everything included" model: it decides which inputs matter, remembers recent
history, can look far back with attention, and outputs several quantiles at once.

TFT (Lim et al., 2021 — Google) is the most feature-rich architecture:

- **Variable selection networks** — learned attention per input variable (which covariate matters for this series?).
- **Static covariate encoder** — integrates fixed metadata (store type, item category).
- **LSTM encoder–decoder** — local processing of past and known-future inputs.
- **Multi-head attention** on the sequence — captures long-range dependencies.
- **Quantile output** — directly predicts $[q_{0.1}, q_{0.5}, q_{0.9}]$ (or any quantiles) via a single forward pass.

**Attention** is a learned weighting: for each forecast step the model scores every past time
step and takes a weighted mix, so it can jump straight to "same week last year" instead of
remembering it step by step. **Multi-head** means several such weightings run side by side.
**Known-future inputs** are covariates you know in advance, like the calendar or a planned
promotion. The quantile output uses the pinball loss from ch. 11.

The variable importance weights are inspectable: you can see which lags, which calendar features, and which external regressors the model is relying on for each series.

```python
from neuralforecast.models import TFT
from neuralforecast.losses.pytorch import MQLoss
import pandas as pd

model = TFT(
    h=12,
    input_size=24,
    hidden_size=64,
    n_head=4,
    dropout=0.1,
    loss=MQLoss(quantiles=[0.1, 0.5, 0.9]),
    max_steps=200,
)
print(f"TFT parameters (approx): {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()):,}")
```

```output
TFT parameters (approx): 220,179
```

This TFT has 220,179 trainable numbers, against 4 for a GARCH(1,1) with a mean term or a handful for an ARIMA.
That scale is why it needs many series: with 48 monthly points and 220 thousand parameters, a
single series would be memorised, not learned.

> **Watch out:** in `neuralforecast`, unknown keyword arguments do not raise an error — they are
> passed through to the PyTorch Lightning trainer. Writing `loss_fn="QuantileLoss"` instead of
> `loss=MQLoss(...)` builds a model that silently trains on the default MAE loss. Check
> `type(model.loss)` after construction.

TFT's attention weights over time steps give you a built-in interpretability signal: peaks show which historical timestamps the model found most informative for the forecast — effectively a learned seasonality detector.

## When to use deep forecasting models

Deep models are a bet: they pay off with lots of related data and side information, and cost
you time and accuracy when data is scarce.

Choose deep models when:

- You have **at least 100 series** (ideally thousands) or very long individual series (> 5 years daily).
- You have **rich time-varying covariates** (promotions, price, weather, related series).
- You need a **probabilistic output** natively (inventory/safety-stock decisions).
- **Seasonality is complex** or multiple overlapping (weekly + annual + holiday).

**Safety stock** is the extra inventory kept to cover demand above the forecast; sizing it needs
an upper quantile, not a mean.

Stick to classical/gradient boosting when:

- You have **few series** or **short history** (< 200 observations).
- You need **fast iteration** and interpretability for stakeholders.
- You're in a **low-data domain** where overfitting is a real risk.

> **Watch out:** always benchmark a deep model against seasonal naive, ETS and a LightGBM with
> lags (ch. 4, 6, 8) on the same rolling-origin backtest. In forecasting competitions, simple
> baselines and well-tuned boosting beat poorly tuned deep models more often than you'd expect.

## Recap in plain words

- Local models fit one series each; global deep models learn shared patterns across many series.
- DeepAR is an LSTM that outputs a probability distribution per step, so you can sample futures and read off quantiles.
- N-BEATS stacks feed-forward blocks that each explain part of the past and add a piece to the forecast; its interpretable version separates trend and seasonality.
- N-HiTS adds multi-resolution blocks for long horizons; TFT adds variable selection, attention and quantile output.
- Deep models need many series or long histories; on short single series, classical methods and boosting usually win.

## Try it Yourself

```python
# Toy global forecasting: fit N-BEATS on 3 synthetic series and predict horizon=6
from neuralforecast import NeuralForecast
from neuralforecast.models import NBEATS
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
records = []
for uid, phase in enumerate([0, np.pi/3, 2*np.pi/3]):
    dates = pd.date_range("2019-01", periods=60, freq="ME")
    vals = 10 + 5*np.sin(2*np.pi*np.arange(60)/12 + phase) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, 60)
    for d, v in zip(dates, vals):
        records.append({"unique_id": f"s{uid}", "ds": d, "y": float(v)})

df = pd.DataFrame(records)

nf = NeuralForecast(
    models=[NBEATS(h=6, input_size=24, max_steps=50)],
    freq="ME"
)
nf.fit(df)
pred = nf.predict()
print(pred.head(9))
```

1        s0 2024-02-29  12.602291
2        s0 2024-03-31  13.940641
3        s0 2024-04-30  14.712464
4        s0 2024-05-31  14.117408
5        s0 2024-06-30  12.297418
6        s1 2024-01-31  13.923992

One N-BEATS model is trained on all three series together (a global model), then forecasts six
months for each. Series `s0` is $10 + 5\sin(2\pi t/12)$ with phase 0, and the forecast traces
that wave: 9.98 in January 2024 ($t = 60$, where the sine is 0), up to 14.71 in April (the peak,
true value 15), back to 12.30 by June. Series `s1` is shifted by $\pi/3$, so it starts higher
(13.92) and peaks earlier. After only 50 training steps the forecasts are already within about
0.4 of the noise-free wave. The last few decimals can differ slightly between runs, because
neural-network training on CPU is not bit-for-bit reproducible.

Try switching `stack_types` to `["generic"]` and compare the forecasts.

Then raise the noise from 0.5 to 2 and see how much worse the forecasts get, or add a fourth
series with a different period and check whether the shared model still fits it.

## Under the Trench

Rare and advanced topics you should at least know the name of. You won't need them daily,
but you'll stop being surprised when you meet them.

- **State-space models & Kalman filter** — recursive estimation behind ETS and structural time series; the theoretically clean alternative to ARIMA with an exact likelihood. In plain words: keep a best guess of hidden quantities (level, slope) and correct it a little with each new observation.
- **Prophet** — additive trend + seasonality + holidays with robust defaults; useful when you need fast, explainable forecasts with minimal tuning.
- **Hierarchical forecasting & reconciliation** — make bottom-up and top-down forecasts agree; MinT reconciliation ensures coherency across aggregation levels. In plain words: the store forecasts should add up to the region forecast.
- **Dynamic time warping** — align or compare series of different speeds; used in kNN classifiers and clustering for series.
- **Change-point & anomaly detection** — find regime shifts with PELT, BOCPD, or Isolation Forest on residuals; critical for adaptive retraining triggers.
- **Intermittent demand (Croston's method)** — forecasting sparse, spiky series where zero demand is common; splits frequency and size into separate models.
- **Spectral analysis / Fourier & wavelets** — frequency-domain features; Fourier terms as regressors in SARIMAX capture complex multi-period seasonality.
- **Global vs local models** — one model across many series (global) vs one per series (local); the tradeoff is data efficiency vs overfitting to individual patterns.
