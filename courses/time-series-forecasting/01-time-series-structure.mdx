## What makes a time series different

A time series is a list of measurements where the order matters: today's value comes after
yesterday's, and yesterday's value helps you guess today's. Shuffle the rows of a normal
table and nothing breaks; shuffle a time series and you destroy the very thing you wanted to
learn.

A time series is a sequence of observations $y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_T$ ordered by time. The
key difference from ordinary tabular data: observations are **serially dependent** — knowing
$y_{t-1}$ tells you something about $y_t$. That dependence is the signal you want to model.

Here $t$ is the time step (month 1, month 2, …) and $T$ is the last one you have. **Serially
dependent** means "each value leans on the ones before it".

> **Picture it:** the temperature outside your window. If it was 20 degrees an hour ago, it
> is almost certainly not $-5$ now. Each reading is a strong clue about the next one. A
> table of unrelated customers has no such clue: customer 41 tells you nothing about
> customer 42.

Three structural components appear in almost every real series:

$$
y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t \quad \text{(additive)}
$$

> **In plain words:** what you observe = slow drift + repeating wiggle + leftover jitter,
> all *added* together.

- $T_t$ — **trend**: the long-run direction (up, down, flat)
- $S_t$ — **seasonal**: a repeating pattern at a fixed frequency (weekly, monthly, yearly)
- $R_t$ — **residual**: everything left over; ideally unpredictable noise

> **Picture it:** ice-cream sales over five years. The shop gets more popular every year
> (trend), sales jump every summer and dip every winter (seasonal), and a rainy Saturday
> knocks a random week down (residual).

When the seasonal amplitude grows proportionally with the level, use the **multiplicative**
form instead:

$$
y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t
$$

> **In plain words:** here the pieces are *multiplied*. The seasonal part is a percentage
> ("summer is 30% above normal"), so when the trend doubles, the summer bump doubles too.
> The **seasonal amplitude** is the size of that bump — the distance from peak to trough.

Taking logs of a multiplicative series converts it to additive: $\log y_t = \log T_t + \log S_t + \log R_t$.

This works because the logarithm turns products into sums: $\log(a \times b) = \log a + \log b$.
After the log, a "30% bump" becomes a bump of fixed size, no matter how big the level is.

> **Watch out:** the log only exists for positive numbers. A series with zeros or negative
> values (profit, temperature in Celsius) cannot be logged directly; you would need a shift
> or a different transform first.

## Datetime indexing in pandas

pandas can label every row of a series with a real date instead of a row number. Once it
knows the dates, it can do calendar arithmetic for you: group by year, shift by a month, or
spot that the data is monthly.

pandas carries a `DatetimeIndex` that unlocks resampling, shifting, and frequency inference.

A **`DatetimeIndex`** is a row label made of timestamps. **Resampling** means changing the
time grain — for example, turning 12 monthly values into 1 yearly value. **Frequency
inference** means pandas noticing on its own that the gaps are "one month-end apart".

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# Build a monthly index for 5 years
idx = pd.date_range(start='2019-01-01', periods=60, freq='ME')
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Additive series: linear trend + monthly seasonality + noise
t = np.arange(60)
trend    = 100 + 0.8 * t
seasonal = 15 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 12)
noise    = rng.normal(0, 4, 60)
y = trend + seasonal + noise

series = pd.Series(y, index=idx, name='value')
print(series.head())
print(f"\nFrequency: {series.index.freq}")
print(f"Annual mean:\n{series.resample('YE').mean().round(1)}")
```

```output
2019-01-31    101.218868
2019-02-28    104.140064
2019-03-31    117.592186
2019-04-30    121.162259
2019-05-31    108.386240
Freq: ME, Name: value, dtype: float64

Frequency: <MonthEnd>
Annual mean:
2019-12-31    103.8
2020-12-31    114.4
2021-12-31    124.6
2022-12-31    133.6
2023-12-31    142.8
Freq: YE-DEC, Name: value, dtype: float64
```

Look at the first block: every date is a month-end (Jan 31, Feb 28, …) because `freq='ME'`
means "month end". The line `Frequency: <MonthEnd>` confirms pandas recorded that grain. In
the yearly table, the averages climb 103.8, 114.4, 124.6, 133.6, 142.8.

`resample('YE').mean()` collapses the series to one value per year-end — the annual averages
increase ~7 units each year, confirming the upward trend.

To be exact, the steps are 10.6, 10.2, 9.0 and 9.2 — about 10 units per year. That matches
the code: the trend rises $0.8$ per month, and $0.8 \times 12 = 9.6$ per year. Averaging a
whole year also cancels the seasonal wave (the sine sums to roughly zero over 12 months),
which is why the trend shows up so cleanly.

> **Watch out:** recent pandas versions renamed the frequency codes. Use `'ME'` (month end)
> and `'YE'` (year end); the old `'M'` and `'Y'` now raise an error.

## Simulating additive vs multiplicative series

The quickest way to tell the two shapes apart is to measure the seasonal swing early in the
series and late in the series. If the swing stays the same size, the series is additive. If
the swing grows as the level grows, it is multiplicative.

Seeing both structures side by side clarifies when to apply the log transform.

The code below builds both kinds from the same trend. It measures **peak-to-trough** — the
biggest value in a 12-month window minus the smallest — in the first year and the last year.

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
idx = pd.date_range('2020-01', periods=48, freq='ME')
t   = np.arange(48)

# Additive: seasonal amplitude is constant
trend_add  = 50 + 1.2 * t
seas_add   = 10 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 12)
y_add      = trend_add + seas_add + rng.normal(0, 3, 48)

# Multiplicative: seasonal amplitude scales with trend
trend_mul  = 50 + 1.2 * t
seas_mul   = 1 + 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 12)  # multiplier around 1
y_mul      = trend_mul * seas_mul + rng.normal(0, 3, 48)

s_add = pd.Series(y_add, index=idx)
s_mul = pd.Series(y_mul, index=idx)

# Check: seasonal amplitude early vs late
print("Additive — early peak-to-trough:", round(y_add[:12].max() - y_add[:12].min(), 1))
print("Additive — late  peak-to-trough:", round(y_add[36:].max() - y_add[36:].min(), 1))
print("Multiplicative — early:", round(y_mul[:12].max() - y_mul[:12].min(), 1))
print("Multiplicative — late: ", round(y_mul[36:].max() - y_mul[36:].min(), 1))
```

```output
Additive — early peak-to-trough: 16.9
Additive — late  peak-to-trough: 20.0
Multiplicative — early: 26.7
Multiplicative — late:  59.1
```

Read the four numbers. The additive series swings 16.9 in year one and 20.0 in year four:
roughly the same size (the gap is noise plus a little trend inside each window). The
multiplicative series swings 26.7 early and 59.1 late — more than double.

The additive series keeps a roughly constant seasonal swing (~20). The multiplicative series
has a larger swing late than early because the seasonal factor scales with the
growing trend.

Why more than double? The seasonal factor goes from 0.7 to 1.3: the peak is 30% above the
trend and the trough 30% below it, so the swing is proportional to the trend level. The trend
grows from about 57 in year one to about 100 in year four, so the swing grows with it (noise
adds a few units on top).

> **Picture it:** a rubber band with marks on it. In an additive series the marks stay the
> same distance apart. In a multiplicative series you stretch the band (the trend grows) and
> the marks spread out with it.

## Recap in plain words

- A time series is ordered data where each value carries information about the next.
- Most series split into a trend (slow drift), a seasonal pattern (repeating wiggle) and a residual (leftover noise).
- Additive means the seasonal swing has a fixed size; multiplicative means it is a percentage of the level.
- A log transform turns a multiplicative series into an additive one.
- A pandas `DatetimeIndex` lets you resample, shift and group by calendar periods.
- Comparing early and late peak-to-trough swings is a fast test for additive vs multiplicative.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
idx = pd.date_range('2021-01', periods=36, freq='ME')
t   = np.arange(36)

# Start with an additive series
amp    = 10                  # seasonal amplitude
trend  = 80 + 0.5 * t
seas   = amp * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 12)
noise  = rng.normal(0, 2, 36)
y      = trend + seas + noise

series = pd.Series(y, index=idx)

# Basic summary
print("Mean:", round(series.mean(), 2))
print("Std: ", round(series.std(), 2))
print("Min: ", round(series.min(), 2))
print("Max: ", round(series.max(), 2))
```

```output
Mean: 87.86
Std:  7.6
Min:  73.26
Max:  102.56
```

The code builds a 36-month additive series: trend from 80 rising $0.5$ per month, a seasonal
wave of amplitude 10, and noise with standard deviation 2. The mean 87.86 sits near the
trend's middle value ($80 + 0.5 \times 17.5 = 88.75$). The range 73.26 to 102.56 is about
29 wide: the seasonal wave alone spans 20 (from $-10$ to $+10$), and the trend rise plus noise add the rest.

Try `amp = 30`: the standard deviation should jump, because the seasonal wave now dominates
the spread. Then swap to a multiplicative seasonal factor (`trend * (1 + 0.2 * np.sin(...))`)
and repeat the early-vs-late swing check from the previous section.

## Exercise

Create a multiplicative time series with 60 monthly observations where the trend grows from
100 to 200. Apply a log transform and verify that the seasonal amplitude becomes constant
after the transform.

> **Hint:** build the trend with `np.linspace`-style growth, multiply it by a factor like
> `1 + 0.25 * sin(...)`, and compare the peak-to-trough of the first 12 months with the last
> 12 months, before and after `np.log`.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
idx = pd.date_range('2019-01', periods=60, freq='ME')
t   = np.arange(60)

trend = 100 + (100/59) * t                    # grows from 100 to 200
seas  = 1 + 0.25 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 12)
noise = np.exp(rng.normal(0, 0.05, 60))       # multiplicative noise
y = trend * seas * noise

series = pd.Series(y, index=idx)
log_series = np.log(series)

early_swing = y[:12].max() - y[:12].min()
late_swing  = y[48:].max() - y[48:].min()
log_early   = log_series[:12].max() - log_series[:12].min()
log_late    = log_series[48:].max() - log_series[48:].min()

print(f"Original  — early swing: {early_swing:.1f},  late swing: {late_swing:.1f}")
print(f"Log-trans — early swing: {log_early:.3f}, late swing: {log_late:.3f}")
```

The log-transformed series shows nearly equal early and late seasonal swings, confirming the
multiplicative structure has been made additive.

Running it prints an original swing of 48.2 early and 91.4 late — almost double, because the
trend doubled. After the log the swings are 0.438 and 0.486: close, with the small gap coming
from the trend's rise inside each 12-month window and the noise. For reference,
$\log 1.25 - \log 0.75 \approx 0.51$ is the pure seasonal swing in log units. Compare
windows of the same length; a longer late window would contain more trend and inflate the
swing.
