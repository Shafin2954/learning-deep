## Why stationarity matters

A series is "stationary" when its basic behaviour does not change over time: it hovers around
the same level, wobbles by the same amount, and relates to its own past in the same way in
year one as in year ten. Models learn rules from the past, so they need the rules to stay put.

Most time-series models assume the series is **weakly stationary**: constant mean,
constant variance, and autocovariance that depends only on the lag $k$, not on when
you are in the series:

$$
E[y_t] = \mu, \quad \text{Var}(y_t) = \sigma^2, \quad \text{Cov}(y_t, y_{t-k}) = \gamma_k
$$

- $E[y_t] = \mu$: the average level $\mu$ is the same at every time $t$.
- $\text{Var}(y_t) = \sigma^2$: the spread around that level is the same at every time.
- $\text{Cov}(y_t, y_{t-k}) = \gamma_k$: how strongly a value moves with the value $k$ steps
  earlier (its **autocovariance** — "covariance with itself, shifted") depends only on the
  gap $k$, the **lag**, and not on the date.

> **Picture it:** a dog on a fixed-length leash tied to a post. It runs around, but always
> near the post and never further than the leash allows — that is stationary. A dog with no
> leash wanders off and can end up anywhere — that is non-stationary.

A trending or seasonally-varying series violates this. Fitting ARMA to a non-stationary
series produces spurious correlations and unreliable forecasts.

**ARMA** is the family of models you will meet in ch. 5. A **spurious correlation** is a
relationship that looks strong in the data but is an accident: two unrelated series that both
trend upward will look highly correlated just because both go up.

### The unit root: why trends make series non-stationary

The question is: how much of yesterday survives into today? If only a fraction survives,
shocks fade and the series stays near its centre. If all of it survives, every shock is
permanent and the series drifts forever.

An AR(1) process $y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ is stationary when $|\phi| < 1$.
When $\phi = 1$ you get a **random walk**: $y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$. The variance
grows without bound — it's non-stationary.

Here **AR(1)** ("autoregressive of order 1") means today = $\phi$ times yesterday plus a fresh
random shock $\varepsilon_t$. With $\phi = 0.7$, a shock keeps 70% of its size after one step,
49% after two, and fades to almost nothing. With $\phi = 1$, nothing fades: $y_t$ is the sum
of all past shocks, so after $t$ steps its variance is $t$ times the shock variance. That
endless growth is what "unit root" means — the root of the model's equation sits exactly at 1.

> **Watch out:** a random walk does not have to look like it is trending. It can wander down,
> then up, then sideways. "Non-stationary" is about the rules changing (the variance growing),
> not about a visible slope.

## ADF test: detecting a unit root

You cannot always tell a random walk apart from a slow-fading AR(1) by eye, so you use a
statistical test. The ADF test asks the data: "is $\phi$ exactly 1?"

The Augmented Dickey-Fuller test tests $H_0$: the series has a unit root (non-stationary).
A low p-value (< 0.05) rejects $H_0$ and is evidence of stationarity.

$H_0$ is the **null hypothesis** — the default story the test tries to knock down. The
**p-value** is how surprising your data would be if that story were true; small means "very
surprising, so probably not true" (Probability & Statistics covers testing in depth).

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Non-stationary: random walk (phi=1)
rw = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 1, 200))

# Stationary: AR(1) with phi=0.7
ar1 = np.zeros(200)
for t in range(1, 200):
    ar1[t] = 0.7 * ar1[t-1] + rng.normal(0, 1)

adf_rw  = adfuller(rw,  autolag='AIC')
adf_ar1 = adfuller(ar1, autolag='AIC')

print(f"Random walk  — ADF stat: {adf_rw[0]:.3f},  p-value: {adf_rw[1]:.4f}")
print(f"AR(1) phi=.7 — ADF stat: {adf_ar1[0]:.3f}, p-value: {adf_ar1[1]:.4f}")
```

```output
Random walk  — ADF stat: -1.302,  p-value: 0.6282
AR(1) phi=.7 — ADF stat: -5.905, p-value: 0.0000
```

The random walk scores an ADF statistic of $-1.302$ with p-value 0.6282: nowhere near 0.05,
so the test cannot reject "unit root". The AR(1) scores $-5.905$ with a p-value that rounds to
0.0000, a decisive rejection. The more negative the statistic, the stronger the evidence of
stationarity.

The random walk has p = 0.63 — fail to reject non-stationarity. The AR(1) with $\phi=0.7$
has p $\approx$ 0 — stationary.

> **Watch out:** "fail to reject" is not "proven non-stationary". A short or very persistent
> stationary series (say $\phi = 0.97$) often fails the test too. You will see exactly that in
> the Try it block below.

## Differencing to achieve stationarity

If the level wanders, look at the *changes* instead. Today's change ("up 3", "down 1") often
behaves nicely even when the level itself does not.

First differencing removes a linear trend: $\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$.

**Differencing** means replacing each value with "this value minus the previous one". For a
random walk, $y_t - y_{t-1} = \varepsilon_t$: the difference is exactly the shock, which is
pure noise.

```python
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
rw  = np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 1, 200))

diff_rw = np.diff(rw)   # first difference

adf_orig = adfuller(rw,      autolag='AIC')
adf_diff = adfuller(diff_rw, autolag='AIC')

print(f"Original   — p-value: {adf_orig[1]:.4f}")
print(f"Differenced — p-value: {adf_diff[1]:.6f}")
print(f"Diff mean: {diff_rw.mean():.3f}  std: {diff_rw.std():.3f}")
```

```output
Original   — p-value: 0.6282
Differenced — p-value: 0.000000
Diff mean: -0.032  std: 0.882
```

Same random walk as before (same seed), so the original p-value is again 0.6282. After one
difference the p-value is 0.000000 and the changes have mean $-0.032$ (essentially 0) and
standard deviation 0.882, close to the shock's true value of 1.

After one difference the random walk becomes white noise (mean $\approx$ 0, constant variance,
stationary). The number of differences needed is the `d` parameter in ARIMA(p,d,q).

**White noise** is a series of independent random shocks with no pattern at all — nothing left
to predict. The "I" in **ARIMA** stands for "integrated", and `d` counts how many times you
difference before modelling (ch. 5).

> **Picture it:** you can't predict where a drunk walker will be in an hour, but you can
> describe each step: "about one pace, random direction". Differencing turns the question from
> "where is he?" into "what was the last step?"

> **Watch out:** differencing too often hurts. Differencing white noise again creates a
> fake negative correlation between neighbours. Difference only until the ADF test passes.

## ACF and PACF: reading the autocorrelation structure

Two charts tell you how a series remembers its past. One shows the total link between today
and $k$ steps ago. The other shows only the *direct* link, after the in-between steps are
accounted for.

**ACF** (Autocorrelation Function): $r_k = \text{Corr}(y_t, y_{t-k})$.
**PACF** (Partial ACF): correlation at lag $k$ after removing the effect of lags $1, \ldots, k-1$.

> **Picture it:** gossip in a line of people. Person 3 knows what person 1 said, but only
> because person 2 passed it on. The ACF at lag 2 counts that relayed link. The PACF at lag 2
> asks: "does person 3 know anything from person 1 that did *not* come through person 2?"

Pattern recognition:
- **AR(p)**: ACF decays slowly (exponential or oscillating); PACF cuts off after lag $p$.
- **MA(q)**: ACF cuts off after lag $q$; PACF decays slowly.
- **ARMA**: both decay slowly.

An **AR(p)** model uses the last $p$ values; an **MA(q)** ("moving average") model uses the
last $q$ random shocks. "Cuts off" means drops to about zero right after that lag. Why does the
AR PACF cut off? In AR(2) only lags 1 and 2 have a direct effect; lag 3 matters only through
them, so its partial correlation is zero.

```python
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import acf, pacf

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Simulate AR(2): y_t = 0.6*y_{t-1} + 0.3*y_{t-2} + eps
y = np.zeros(300)
for t in range(2, 300):
    y[t] = 0.6 * y[t-1] + 0.3 * y[t-2] + rng.normal(0, 1)

acf_vals  = acf(y,  nlags=10, alpha=0.05)
pacf_vals = pacf(y, nlags=10, alpha=0.05)

print("ACF at lags 1-5: ", [round(x, 3) for x in acf_vals[0][1:6]])
print("PACF at lags 1-5:", [round(x, 3) for x in pacf_vals[0][1:6]])
```

```output
ACF at lags 1-5:  [np.float64(0.841), np.float64(0.782), np.float64(0.691), np.float64(0.624), np.float64(0.546)]
PACF at lags 1-5: [np.float64(0.844), np.float64(0.263), np.float64(-0.049), np.float64(-0.0), np.float64(-0.047)]
```

Read the ACF: 0.841, 0.782, 0.691, 0.624, 0.546 — a slow, steady decline. Read the PACF: 0.844
at lag 1, 0.263 at lag 2, then $-0.049$, $-0.0$, $-0.047$. The big drop after lag 2 is the
cut-off. (The `np.float64(...)` wrapper is how NumPy 2 prints its numbers inside a list.)

ACF decays slowly. PACF is significant at lags 1 and 2, then drops to near zero at lag 3 —
the signature of AR(2). This matches the true model ($\phi_1 = 0.6$, $\phi_2 = 0.3$).

"Near zero" has a concrete meaning: with $n = 300$ points, values inside about
$\pm 1.96/\sqrt{300} \approx \pm 0.113$ are indistinguishable from zero at the 95% level. All
three later PACF values are well inside that band.

## Recap in plain words

- Stationary means the mean, the spread and the lag-to-lag relationships do not change over time.
- A random walk ($\phi = 1$) keeps every shock forever, so its variance keeps growing.
- The ADF test's null is "unit root"; a p-value below 0.05 is evidence of stationarity.
- Differencing (this value minus the previous one) usually turns a wandering series into a stable one; the count of differences is ARIMA's `d`.
- The ACF measures total correlation with lag $k$; the PACF measures only the direct part.
- AR(p) shows a PACF cut-off at $p$; MA(q) shows an ACF cut-off at $q$.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, acf, pacf

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)

# Near-unit-root AR(1): phi=0.97
y = np.zeros(300)
for t in range(1, 300):
    y[t] = 0.97 * y[t-1] + rng.normal(0, 1)

adf = adfuller(y, autolag='AIC')
print(f"phi=0.97 — ADF p-value: {adf[1]:.4f}")

# Difference and re-test
dy = np.diff(y)
adf_d = adfuller(dy, autolag='AIC')
print(f"Differenced — ADF p-value: {adf_d[1]:.6f}")

a = acf(y, nlags=5)
print("ACF lags 1-5:", [round(v, 3) for v in a[1:6]])
```

```output
phi=0.97 — ADF p-value: 0.1415
Differenced — ADF p-value: 0.000000
ACF lags 1-5: [np.float64(0.965), np.float64(0.929), np.float64(0.893), np.float64(0.858), np.float64(0.822)]
```

This series is stationary by construction ($\phi = 0.97 < 1$), yet the ADF p-value is 0.1415:
the test fails to reject a unit root. With $\phi$ this close to 1, 300 points are not enough
to tell it apart from a random walk. The ACF decays very slowly (0.965, 0.929, …, 0.822),
roughly like $0.97^k$. After differencing, the p-value is 0.000000.

Try $\phi = 0.9$ and then $\phi = 0.5$: watch the ADF p-value fall and the ACF drop faster.
Then raise the length from 300 to 3000 with $\phi = 0.97$ and see whether the test now detects
stationarity.

## Exercise

Simulate an MA(2) process: $y_t = \varepsilon_t + 0.8\varepsilon_{t-1} + 0.4\varepsilon_{t-2}$.
Compute its ACF and PACF. Verify that ACF cuts off after lag 2 and PACF decays slowly —
the signature of MA.

> **Hint:** draw all the shocks first (`rng.normal` with a couple of extra values), then build
> each $y_t$ from three neighbouring shocks. `acf` and `pacf` from `statsmodels` do the rest.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import acf, pacf

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
eps = rng.normal(0, 1, 302)

y = np.zeros(300)
for t in range(2, 300):
    y[t] = eps[t+2] + 0.8*eps[t+1] + 0.4*eps[t]

a = acf(y,  nlags=6)
p = pacf(y, nlags=6)
print("ACF  lags 1-6:", [round(v, 3) for v in a[1:7]])
print("PACF lags 1-6:", [round(v, 3) for v in p[1:7]])
```

ACF should be significant at lags 1 and 2 then drop to near zero. PACF should show a slow
oscillating decay — confirming this is MA(2), not AR.

Running it gives ACF 0.609 and 0.225 at lags 1–2, close to the theory values
$1.12/1.8 \approx 0.62$ and $0.4/1.8 \approx 0.22$. The later lags (0.126, 0.166, 0.083, 0.005)
are small but noisy; two of them sit just above the $\pm 0.113$ band, which is normal sampling
noise for 300 points. The PACF mostly alternates in sign (0.611, $-0.235$, 0.168, 0.08, $-0.135$) and
shrinks slowly — the oscillating decay that marks an MA process.
