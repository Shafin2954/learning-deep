## Decomposing a series into components

Decomposition takes one tangled line and pulls it apart into the three pieces from ch. 1: the
slow drift, the repeating wave, and the leftover noise. Once they are separate, you can look at
each one on its own.

Decomposition separates the series into trend, seasonal, and residual so you can model each
independently or diagnose which component dominates forecast error.

> **Picture it:** a smoothie of banana, strawberry and ice. Decomposition is un-blending it
> back into three cups so you can see how much of each went in. The recipe you assume
> (additive or multiplicative, ch. 1) decides how you pour it apart.

Two main approaches:
- **Classical** (moving-average based): simple, transparent, but assumes stable seasonality
  and loses a half-period at each endpoint.
- **STL** (Seasonal-Trend decomposition via Loess): iterative, handles changing seasonality,
  robust to outliers.

A **moving average** replaces each point with the average of its neighbours, which smooths out
short wiggles. **Loess** is a smarter local smoother: it fits a small weighted line around each
point, giving nearby points more say. A **half-period** for monthly data with yearly seasons is
6 months.

## Classical decomposition

The recipe is three steps: smooth the series to get the trend, average what is left by calendar
month to get the seasonal pattern, and call whatever remains the residual.

For an additive series with period $m$:

1. **Trend** $\hat{T}_t$: apply a centered $2\times m$ moving average
   (so the sum of weights is 1 and the seasonal effects cancel).
2. **Seasonal** $\hat{S}_t$: average $(y_t - \hat{T}_t)$ for each period (Jan, Feb, …)
   across all years; normalize so seasonal effects sum to zero.
3. **Residual** $\hat{R}_t = y_t - \hat{T}_t - \hat{S}_t$.

The **period** $m$ is the length of one seasonal cycle: 12 for monthly data with a yearly
pattern, 4 for quarterly, 7 for daily data with a weekly pattern. The hat on $\hat{T}_t$ means
"our estimate of". Why a "$2\times m$" average? With $m = 12$ (an even number) a plain 12-month
window has no middle month. Averaging two neighbouring 12-month windows centres it, and covers
exactly one full year, so the summer highs and winter lows cancel.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
idx = pd.date_range('2018-01', periods=72, freq='ME')
t   = np.arange(72)

# Retail sales: upward trend + monthly seasonality (summer peak)
trend    = 200 + 2.5 * t
seasonal = 30 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (t - 3) / 12)  # peak in July (t = 6)
noise    = rng.normal(0, 8, 72)
y        = trend + seasonal + noise

series = pd.Series(y, index=idx, name='sales')

result = seasonal_decompose(series, model='additive', period=12)

print("Trend first 5 values:")
print(result.trend.dropna().head().round(1).to_string())
print("\nSeasonal (first cycle):")
print(result.seasonal[:12].round(1).to_string())
print("\nResidual std:", round(result.resid.dropna().std(), 2))
```


The trend starts at 2018-07-31, not January: the centred 12-month window needs 6 months on each
side, so the first and last 6 months have no trend value (the "half-period" loss). The trend
then climbs about 2.5 per month (213.8, 216.9, 220.1, …), as built. The seasonal pattern peaks
in July (+32.6) and bottoms in January ($-33.5$), close to the true $\pm 30$ sine wave.

The residual std is 5.36, while the noise we injected had std 8. The decomposition
successfully separated trend and seasonal from noise — but the estimates also soaked up part of
the noise. The trend averages only 13 points and each seasonal value averages only 5–6 years,
so some noise leaks into them, and the leftover looks calmer than the truth.

> **Watch out:** a decomposition residual is usually *smaller* than the real noise, because
> the trend and seasonal estimates absorb some of it. Don't treat the residual std as an
> honest estimate of forecast error; measure error on held-out data instead (ch. 6).

## STL decomposition

STL keeps the same three pieces but lets the seasonal shape change from year to year. It
does this by smoothing back and forth — trend, then season, then trend again — until both
settle.

STL (Cleveland et al., 1990) iterates between smoothing the trend (Loess) and extracting
the seasonal component. Key advantages:
- Handles **changing seasonality** (e.g., seasonal amplitude growing over time).
- Robust option (`robust=True`) down-weights outliers so they don't distort trend/seasonal.
- Requires only one parameter: `period`.

An **outlier** is a freak value, like a one-day power cut in an electricity series.
**Down-weights** means STL lets that point count less when it smooths. Beyond `period`, the
optional `seasonal=` argument (an odd number) sets how many years the seasonal smoother looks
across: small means the season can change fast, large means it stays almost fixed.

> **Picture it:** classical decomposition makes one cookie cutter for "a typical year" and
> stamps it on every year. STL lets the cutter slowly grow or shrink as the years go by.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
idx = pd.date_range('2015-01', periods=96, freq='ME')
t   = np.arange(96)

# Changing seasonal amplitude: grows from 10 to 40 over 8 years
amp      = 10 + 30 * (t / 95)
seasonal = amp * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 12)
trend    = 100 + 1.5 * t
y        = trend + seasonal + rng.normal(0, 5, 96)

series = pd.Series(y, index=idx)

stl    = STL(series, period=12, seasonal=13)
result = stl.fit()

print("STL seasonal — first vs last amplitude:")
first_amp = result.seasonal[:12].max() - result.seasonal[:12].min()
last_amp  = result.seasonal[84:].max() - result.seasonal[84:].min()
print(f"  First year amplitude: {first_amp:.1f}")
print(f"  Last  year amplitude: {last_amp:.1f}")
print(f"Residual std: {result.resid.std():.2f}")
```

```output
STL seasonal — first vs last amplitude:
  First year amplitude: 22.4
  Last  year amplitude: 85.4
Residual std: 3.65
```

In the simulation the amplitude grows from 10 to 40, so the true peak-to-trough is about 20–27
in year 1 and about 73–80 in year 8. STL reports 22.4 for the first year and 85.4 for the last
— it tracks the growth, nearly a fourfold increase. The last-year value overshoots a little,
because some noise leaks into the seasonal estimate (residual std is 3.65 against the injected
noise of 5).

STL correctly captures the growing amplitude — the extracted seasonal component spans about
22 in year 1 but about 85 in year 8, matching the simulated growth. Classical decomposition
would average these and miss the trend in seasonality.

> **Watch out:** the more freedom you give the seasonal part (a small `seasonal=` window), the
> more noise it can swallow. A smooth-looking residual can mean "great model" or "the
> seasonal part ate the noise". Check by comparing with a larger window.

## Recap in plain words

- Decomposition splits a series into trend, seasonal and residual pieces.
- Classical decomposition uses a centred moving average for the trend and month-by-month averages for the season.
- It assumes the same seasonal shape every year and loses half a period at each end.
- STL uses Loess smoothing in a loop, so the seasonal shape can change and outliers can be down-weighted.
- Residuals from either method usually look less noisy than the true noise, because the fitted pieces absorb some of it.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
idx = pd.date_range('2020-01', periods=60, freq='ME')
t   = np.arange(60)

y = (150 + t * 1.2
     + 20 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 12)
     + rng.normal(0, 6, 60))

series = pd.Series(y, index=idx)
result = STL(series, period=12).fit()

print(f"Trend range:    {result.trend.min():.1f} – {result.trend.max():.1f}")
print(f"Seasonal range: {result.seasonal.min():.1f} – {result.seasonal.max():.1f}")
print(f"Residual std:   {result.resid.std():.2f}")
print(f"Residual skew:  {result.resid.skew():.3f}")
```

```output
Trend range:    149.2 – 218.4
Seasonal range: -31.3 – 20.5
Residual std:   3.35
Residual skew:  0.113
```

The true trend runs from 150 to $150 + 1.2 \times 59 = 220.8$; STL's trend spans 149.2 to 218.4,
close at both ends. The true seasonal wave goes from $-20$ to $+20$, but STL's seasonal ranges
from $-31.3$ to 20.5: in at least one month it absorbed a noise spike into the season. The
residual std of 3.35 is again below the injected noise (6), for the same reason. A skew of
0.113 is close to 0, so the residuals are roughly symmetric.

Try `STL(series, period=12, seasonal=13)` (a stiffer seasonal) and compare the seasonal range
and residual std. Then add an outlier (`y[30] += 80`) and compare `robust=True` against the
default.

## Exercise

Decompose a quarterly series (period=4) using `seasonal_decompose`. Then apply the
Ljung-Box test to the residuals to check whether they are white noise (no remaining
autocorrelation).

> **Hint:** the **Ljung-Box test** checks many autocorrelation lags at once; its null is "no
> autocorrelation up to this lag". Use `acorr_ljungbox(resid, lags=[4, 8], return_df=True)`
> after dropping the NaN ends.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose
from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import acorr_ljungbox

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
idx = pd.date_range('2010-Q1', periods=40, freq='QE')
t   = np.arange(40)

y = (50 + 0.5*t
     + 10 * np.sin(2*np.pi*t/4)
     + rng.normal(0, 2, 40))

series = pd.Series(y, index=idx)
result = seasonal_decompose(series, model='additive', period=4)

lb = acorr_ljungbox(result.resid.dropna(), lags=[4, 8], return_df=True)
print(lb[['lb_stat', 'lb_pvalue']].round(4))
# p-values > 0.05 → residuals are white noise
```

Running it gives p-values 0.0652 at lag 4 and 0.3455 at lag 8. Both are above 0.05, so you
cannot reject "no autocorrelation": the residuals pass as white noise. Lag 4 is borderline,
though. That is typical of classical decomposition: the moving-average trend makes neighbouring
residuals share noise, which plants a little autocorrelation of its own. With only 36 residual
points the test has little power, so a borderline result deserves a look at the ACF (ch. 2).
</details>
