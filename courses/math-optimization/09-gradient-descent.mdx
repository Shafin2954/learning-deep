## The update rule

**Gradient descent** is "feel which way is downhill, take a step that way, repeat". The
gradient (ch. 3) tells you which way is uphill and how steep it is; you step the opposite
way.

Gradient descent iteratively moves in the direction of steepest descent:

$$
w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta \nabla f(w_t)
$$

where $\eta > 0$ is the **learning rate** (step size). The gradient $\nabla f(w_t)$ points uphill, so subtracting it moves downhill.

> **In plain words:** new weights = old weights − (step size) × (slope). A steep slope means
> a big step; a flat slope means a small one; at the bottom the slope is zero and you stop.
> ($\eta$ is the Greek letter "eta"; $t$ counts the steps.)

> **Picture it:** walking down a foggy hill, you can only feel the ground under your feet.
> Each step, you check which way the ground tilts and step that way. The learning rate is
> your stride length.

```python
import numpy as np

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, w0, eta, n_iters):
    w = w0.copy().astype(np.float64)
    history = [float(np.sum(w**2))]   # placeholder — caller tracks loss
    ws = [w.copy()]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        w -= eta * grad_fn(w)
        ws.append(w.copy())
    return np.array(ws)

# f(w) = (w1 - 3)^2 + 2*(w2 + 1)^2  → minimum at (3, -1)
def loss(w):
    return (w[0] - 3)**2 + 2*(w[1] + 1)**2

def grad(w):
    return np.array([2*(w[0] - 3), 4*(w[1] + 1)])

w0 = np.array([0.0, 0.0])
ws = gradient_descent(grad, w0, eta=0.1, n_iters=50)
losses = [loss(w) for w in ws]

print(f"Initial loss: {losses[0]:.4f}")
print(f"After 50 steps: {losses[-1]:.6f}")
print(f"Final w: {ws[-1].round(6)}")
```

```output
Initial loss: 11.0000
After 50 steps: 0.000000
Final w: [ 2.999957 -1.      ]
```

Starting at $(0, 0)$, the loss is $(0-3)^2 + 2(0+1)^2 = 11$. After 50 steps it's zero to six
decimals and $w$ sits at the true minimum $(3, -1)$, with $w_1$ a hair short at 2.999957.
Notice $w_2$ got there faster than $w_1$: its direction is steeper (coefficient 2 vs 1), so
its gradient — and its steps — are bigger.

## Learning rate sensitivity

The learning rate is the most important hyperparameter. Too large and the loss diverges; too small and convergence is glacially slow.

To **diverge** means the loss grows instead of shrinking — each step overshoots the valley
floor and lands higher up the other side.

### L-smoothness and the optimal rate

**$L$-smooth** means the slope can't change faster than a rate $L$ — the bowl's walls never
curve more sharply than $L$. Knowing $L$ tells you the biggest safe step.

A function is **$L$-smooth** if $\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \le L \|x - y\|$ for all $x, y$. For an $L$-smooth convex function, gradient descent with $\eta = 1/L$ converges at rate $O(L/k)$ after $k$ steps.

For $f(w) = (w_1-3)^2 + 2(w_2+1)^2$, the Hessian is $\text{diag}(2, 4)$, so $L = 4$ and the optimal rate is $\eta^* = 1/4 = 0.25$.

> **In plain words:** for a quadratic, $L$ is the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian — the
> curvature of the steepest direction (ch. 3, ch. 4). $O(L/k)$ means the gap to the minimum
> shrinks at least like $L/k$: after 10× more steps, at most a tenth of the error.

```python
import numpy as np

def loss(w):
    return (w[0] - 3)**2 + 2*(w[1] + 1)**2

def grad(w):
    return np.array([2*(w[0] - 3), 4*(w[1] + 1)])

w0 = np.array([0.0, 0.0])
n_iters = 30

results = {}
for eta in [0.05, 0.25, 0.45, 0.6]:
    w = w0.copy()
    ls = []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        w = w - eta * grad(w)
        ls.append(loss(w))
    results[eta] = ls

print(f"{'eta':>6}  {'loss@10':>12}  {'loss@30':>12}  {'status':>12}")
for eta, ls in results.items():
    final = ls[-1]
    status = "diverged" if final > 1e6 or np.isnan(final) else "converged"
    print(f"{eta:>6.2f}  {ls[9]:>12.4f}  {final:>12.6f}  {status:>12}")
```

```output
   eta       loss@10       loss@30        status
  0.05        1.1172      0.016176     converged
  0.25        0.0000      0.000000     converged
  0.45        0.0231      0.000003     converged
  0.60     1673.3651  1171418656.114190      diverged
```

Read the four rows. $\eta = 0.05$ is safe but slow (still 0.016 after 30 steps).
$\eta = 0.25$ hits zero almost immediately — in the steep direction, one step of
$0.25 \times 4 = 1$ lands exactly on the bottom. $\eta = 0.45$ still converges, but by
zig-zagging across the steep direction. $\eta = 0.6$ explodes to over a billion.

With $\eta = 0.25$ (the theoretical optimum), GD converges in under 10 steps. At $\eta = 0.6 > 1/L$, it diverges.

Why does 0.45 survive when it's also bigger than $1/L$? On a quadratic, each step multiplies
the distance to the minimum along an eigen-direction by $(1 - \eta\lambda)$. That shrinks
only while $|1 - \eta\lambda| < 1$, i.e. while $\eta < 2/\lambda$. The steepest direction has
$\lambda = 4$, so the true cliff edge is $\eta < 2/L = 0.5$: 0.45 zig-zags inward (factor
$|1 - 1.8| = 0.8$), 0.6 zig-zags outward (factor $|1 - 2.4| = 1.4$). $\eta = 1/L$ is the safe
choice that works for every $L$-smooth function, not the edge.

> **Watch out:** a loss that goes up, or turns into `nan`, in the first few steps almost
> always means the learning rate is above $2/L$. Divide it by 10 before debugging anything
> else.

## Backtracking line search

When you don't know how curvy the bowl is, try a big step and check whether it actually
lowered the loss enough. If not, halve the step and check again. That's **backtracking**.

When you don't know $L$, backtracking line search picks the step size automatically. Start with a large trial step $\eta_0$; if the **Armijo condition** is not satisfied, halve $\eta$:

$$
f(w - \eta \nabla f) \le f(w) - \alpha \eta \|\nabla f\|^2
$$

where $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ is typically $0.01$–$0.3$.

> **In plain words:** "the new loss must be lower than the old loss by at least a fraction
> $\alpha$ of what the slope promised". A straight-line prediction says a step of $\eta$
> should lower the loss by $\eta\|\nabla f\|^2$; Armijo accepts the step if you get at least
> $\alpha$ of that.

```python
import numpy as np

def loss(w):
    return (w[0] - 3)**2 + 2*(w[1] + 1)**2

def grad(w):
    return np.array([2*(w[0] - 3), 4*(w[1] + 1)])

def backtracking_gd(grad_fn, loss_fn, w0, n_iters, alpha=0.3, beta=0.5):
    w = w0.copy().astype(np.float64)
    losses = [loss_fn(w)]
    total_backtracks = 0
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        g = grad_fn(w)
        eta = 1.0
        f0 = loss_fn(w)
        slope = np.dot(g, g)
        while loss_fn(w - eta * g) > f0 - alpha * eta * slope:
            eta *= beta
            total_backtracks += 1
        w -= eta * g
        losses.append(loss_fn(w))
    return losses, total_backtracks

w0 = np.array([0.0, 0.0])
losses_bt, bt_count = backtracking_gd(grad, loss, w0, n_iters=20)
losses_fixed = []
w = w0.copy()
for _ in range(20):
    w -= 0.25 * grad(w)
    losses_fixed.append(loss(w))

print(f"Backtracking: loss={losses_bt[-1]:.2e}  total backtracks={bt_count}")
print(f"Fixed eta=0.25: loss={losses_fixed[-1]:.2e}")
```

```output
Backtracking: loss=0.00e+00  total backtracks=3
Fixed eta=0.25: loss=8.19e-12
```

Backtracking reached a loss of exactly zero in 20 steps and only had to halve the step 3
times in total — it found a good step size on its own. The fixed $\eta = 0.25$ (which you
could only choose because you knew $L = 4$) got to $8 \times 10^{-12}$. Without knowing $L$,
backtracking is as good or better.

## Stochastic gradient descent

The exact gradient averages over every training example — far too slow for millions of
examples. **SGD** (stochastic gradient descent) estimates it from a small random handful
each step: noisier, but thousands of times cheaper.

Full-batch GD computes $\nabla f = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \nabla \ell_i$, which is expensive for large $n$. SGD uses a **mini-batch** of $B$ samples:

$$
g_t = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}_t} \nabla \ell_i(w_t) \approx \nabla f(w_t)
$$

> **In plain words:** $\ell_i$ is the loss on example $i$; $\mathcal{B}_t$ is the random set
> of $B$ examples drawn at step $t$. Averaging their gradients gives an estimate of the full
> gradient — right on average, wrong by a bit each time.

The noisy gradient acts as implicit regularisation. For convergence, the learning rate must decay: $\eta_t = \eta_0 / \sqrt{t}$ or $\eta_0 / (1 + \lambda t)$.

> **Picture it:** instead of polling the whole country before every decision, you ask 32
> random people. You'll be a little off each time, but you can ask again a thousand times in
> the time one full poll takes. Shrinking the step over time stops the noise from making you
> wander around the bottom forever.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, d = 1000, 5
X = rng.standard_normal((n, d))
w_true = rng.standard_normal(d)
y = X @ w_true + rng.standard_normal(n) * 0.5

def sgd(X, y, w0, n_epochs, batch_size, eta0):
    w = w0.copy()
    n = len(y)
    losses = []
    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        idx = rng.permutation(n)
        for start in range(0, n, batch_size):
            bi = idx[start:start+batch_size]
            Xb, yb = X[bi], y[bi]
            g = 2 * Xb.T @ (Xb @ w - yb) / len(bi)
            eta = eta0 / (1 + 0.01 * epoch)
            w -= eta * g
        losses.append(np.mean((X @ w - y)**2))
    return w, losses

w0 = np.zeros(d)
w_sgd, losses = sgd(X, y, w0, n_epochs=30, batch_size=32, eta0=0.1)
w_exact = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)

print(f"SGD final MSE  : {losses[-1]:.6f}")
print(f"Exact MSE      : {np.mean((X @ w_exact - y)**2):.6f}")
print(f"||w_sgd - w*|| : {np.linalg.norm(w_sgd - w_exact):.6f}")
```

```output
SGD final MSE  : 0.253031
Exact MSE      : 0.247150
||w_sgd - w*|| : 0.079022
```

After 30 passes over the data (**epochs**) of 32-example batches, SGD's MSE (0.2530) is
within about 2% of the exact least-squares optimum (0.2472), and its weights are within 0.08
of the exact ones. The remaining gap is the noise of the mini-batches; a slower-decaying or
smaller final learning rate would close it further. The optimum's MSE is about 0.25 because
the data's built-in noise has standard deviation 0.5, and $0.5^2 = 0.25$.

## Recap in plain words

- Gradient descent: $w \leftarrow w - \eta \nabla f(w)$ — step against the slope.
- The learning rate is the critical knob: too small is slow, too big diverges.
- For an $L$-smooth function, $\eta = 1/L$ is always safe; on a quadratic, divergence starts
  at $\eta = 2/L$.
- Backtracking line search finds a good step size automatically using the Armijo test.
- SGD uses mini-batch gradient estimates — cheap and noisy — with a decaying learning rate.

```python
import numpy as np

# Implement GD on a quadratic and verify convergence to the known minimum
# f(w) = (w - 5)^2 + 3*(w2 - 2)^2 → minimum at (5, 2), f* = 0

def loss(w): return (w[0] - 5)**2 + 3*(w[1] - 2)**2
def grad(w): return np.array([2*(w[0] - 5), 6*(w[1] - 2)])

w = np.array([0.0, 0.0])
eta = 1.0 / 6.0   # L = 6 (largest eigenvalue of Hessian)

for t in range(1, 21):
    w -= eta * grad(w)
    if t in [1, 5, 10, 20]:
        print(f"step {t:2d}: loss={loss(w):.6f}  w={w.round(4)}")
```

```output
step  1: loss=11.111111  w=[1.6667 2.    ]
step  5: loss=0.433538  w=[4.3416 2.    ]
step 10: loss=0.007518  w=[4.9133 2.    ]
step 20: loss=0.000002  w=[4.9985 2.    ]
```

With $\eta = 1/6$ and the steep direction's curvature $L = 6$, the second coordinate jumps to
its target 2 in a single step ($1 - \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot 6 = 0$). The first coordinate has
curvature 2, so each step only removes a third of its remaining distance
($1 - \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot 2 = \tfrac{2}{3}$): 1.67, then 4.34 after five steps, 4.998 after
twenty. One learning rate has to serve every direction, and the flattest direction sets the
pace. Try $\eta = 0.33$ (just under $2/L$) and watch the second coordinate oscillate.

## Exercise

Implement backtracking line search and compare the number of steps needed to reach $f < 10^{-8}$ against a fixed learning rate on $f(w) = w_1^2 + 50 w_2^2$ (ill-conditioned, $L = 100$).

> **Hint:** **ill-conditioned** means the curvatures differ hugely (2 vs 100). A fixed
> $\eta = 1/L$ is tiny for the flat direction, so that direction will set the step count.

<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

def loss(w): return w[0]**2 + 50*w[1]**2
def grad(w): return np.array([2*w[0], 100*w[1]])

tol = 1e-8

# Fixed LR: eta = 1/L = 0.01
w = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
steps_fixed = 0
while loss(w) > tol and steps_fixed < 10000:
    w -= 0.01 * grad(w)
    steps_fixed += 1
print(f"Fixed eta=0.01: {steps_fixed} steps, final loss={loss(w):.2e}")

# Backtracking
w = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
steps_bt = 0
while loss(w) > tol and steps_bt < 10000:
    g = grad(w)
    eta = 1.0
    while loss(w - eta * g) > loss(w) - 0.3 * eta * np.dot(g, g):
        eta *= 0.5
    w -= eta * g
    steps_bt += 1
print(f"Backtracking  : {steps_bt} steps, final loss={loss(w):.2e}")
```

Both converge but backtracking typically selects larger steps on the well-conditioned dimensions, reaching tolerance faster.
The deeper lesson is the condition number $\kappa = L / \mu = 100/2 = 50$ (largest over
smallest curvature): plain gradient descent needs roughly $\kappa$ times more steps than on a
perfectly round bowl. Momentum and Adam (ch. 10) exist to fight exactly this.
</details>
