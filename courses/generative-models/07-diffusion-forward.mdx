## The Core Idea

Destroying a picture is easy: keep sprinkling static on it until nothing is left. A diffusion
model learns to run that film backwards, removing a little static at a time, until a clean
picture appears out of pure noise.

Diffusion models define a **forward process** that destroys data by adding Gaussian noise over $T$ steps, then learn to **reverse** that process — going from pure noise back to clean data.

The **forward process** is fixed — no learning, just a recipe for adding noise. The **reverse
process** is the learned part: a network that, given a noisy image and how noisy it is, guesses
a slightly cleaner version. **Gaussian noise** is random values drawn from a bell curve.

> **Picture it:** a drop of ink spreading in a glass of water until the water is evenly grey —
> that is the forward process (and where the name "diffusion" comes from). The model learns to
> play the video in reverse: from evenly grey water back to a crisp drop. Where it breaks: real
> ink can't un-diffuse; the model only manages it because it has seen millions of drops.

Unlike GANs (no explicit density) or VAEs (ELBO bound), diffusion models are trained on a simple denoising objective that happens to maximize a form of the ELBO.

## The Forward Process

Each step shrinks the image slightly toward zero and adds a small amount of fresh noise. After
enough steps, nothing of the original is left.

Starting from real data $x_0$, add noise over $T$ steps:

$$
q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t;\, \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\, \beta_t I)
$$

> **In plain words:** to get step $t$ from step $t-1$, multiply the current image by
> $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ (a number slightly below 1) and add noise with variance $\beta_t$. The
> notation $\mathcal{N}(x_t; \text{mean}, \text{variance})$ means "$x_t$ is drawn from a bell curve
> with this mean and variance". The shrinking keeps the total variance from growing: signal
> fades at the same rate noise is added.

where $\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_T$ is a **noise schedule** (small values, e.g. 0.0001 to 0.02).

The **noise schedule** is the list of how much noise to add at each step: tiny at first, larger
later. $T$ is usually 1000.

Define $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$. Then you can sample $x_t$ directly from $x_0$:

$\alpha_t$ is the fraction of signal variance kept by step $t$, and $\bar\alpha_t$ (read
"alpha bar") multiplies all of them up to $t$: the total fraction of the original signal's
variance still present after $t$ steps. $\prod$ means "multiply together".

$$
q(x_t | x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t;\, \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\, (1-\bar\alpha_t) I)
$$

> **In plain words:** you don't need to run $t$ steps one by one. Jumping straight from the clean
> image to step $t$ gives a bell curve centred on the shrunken clean image $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0$
> with variance $1-\bar\alpha_t$.

or equivalently:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)
$$

> **In plain words:** noisy image = (signal weight) times clean image + (noise weight) times fresh
> noise. The two squared weights always add to 1. As $t$ grows, the signal weight slides from 1
> toward 0 and the noise weight from 0 toward 1.

This shortcut works because adding several independent Gaussian noises gives one Gaussian noise
(their variances add). You will prove it numerically in the exercise.

```python
import torch
import numpy as np

def linear_schedule(T=1000, beta_start=1e-4, beta_end=0.02):
    return torch.linspace(beta_start, beta_end, T)

T = 1000
betas     = linear_schedule(T)
alphas    = 1 - betas
alpha_bar = torch.cumprod(alphas, dim=0)

print(f"T={T}")
print(f"beta at t=1:   {betas[0]:.6f}")
print(f"beta at t=1000:{betas[-1]:.6f}")
print(f"alpha_bar at t=1:   {alpha_bar[0]:.6f}")
print(f"alpha_bar at t=500: {alpha_bar[499]:.6f}")
print(f"alpha_bar at t=1000:{alpha_bar[-1]:.8f}  ← nearly 0")

# At t=1000, signal is destroyed
signal_fraction = alpha_bar[-1].sqrt()
noise_fraction  = (1 - alpha_bar[-1]).sqrt()
print(f"\nAt t=1000: signal={signal_fraction:.4f}, noise={noise_fraction:.4f}")
```

```output
T=1000
beta at t=1:   0.000100
beta at t=1000:0.020000
alpha_bar at t=1:   0.999900
alpha_bar at t=500: 0.078587
alpha_bar at t=1000:0.00004036  ← nearly 0

At t=1000: signal=0.0064, noise=1.0000
```

The schedule rises from $\beta = 0.0001$ to 0.02. After 500 steps only 0.0786 (7.9%) of the
signal variance remains; after all 1000 steps, 0.00004. The last line puts that in amplitude
terms: the signal weight $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}$ is 0.0064 and the noise weight is 1.0000. At $t = T$ the
image is, for practical purposes, pure noise — which is exactly what lets you start sampling
from pure noise.

## Visualizing the Forward Process

Watch one simple "image" — 16 pixels all equal to 1 — fade into noise.

The **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** is $\bar\alpha_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)$: signal variance divided
by noise variance. Above 1, signal dominates; below 1, noise dominates.

```python
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

def q_sample(x0, t, alpha_bar):
    """Sample x_t from x_0 using the closed-form."""
    ab = alpha_bar[t].view(-1, *([1]*(x0.dim()-1)))
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    return ab.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps, eps

# Simulate: a clean "image" is a 4x4 tensor of ones
x0 = torch.ones(1, 16)
betas     = linear_schedule(1000)
alpha_bar = torch.cumprod(1 - betas, dim=0)

print("Noise levels at different timesteps:")
for t_val in [0, 100, 250, 500, 750, 999]:
    t = torch.tensor([t_val])
    xt, _ = q_sample(x0, t, alpha_bar)
    snr = (alpha_bar[t_val] / (1 - alpha_bar[t_val])).item()
    print(f"t={t_val:4d}: xt mean={xt.mean():.4f}, std={xt.std():.4f}, SNR={snr:.3f}")
```

```output
Noise levels at different timesteps:
t=   0: xt mean=0.9988, std=0.0094, SNR=9997.341
t= 100: xt mean=0.9890, std=0.3618, SNR=8.537
t= 250: xt mean=0.7666, std=0.6290, SNR=1.090
t= 500: xt mean=0.5605, std=1.2102, SNR=0.084
t= 750: xt mean=0.1359, std=1.1403, SNR=0.003
t= 999: xt mean=0.0353, std=0.9888, SNR=0.000
```

Follow the mean column: it should be near $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, and it falls from 0.9983 at $t = 0$
to 0.4636 at $t = 500$ (theory: $\sqrt{0.0786} \approx 0.28$), then to the 0.1 range. The std column grows from 0.0105 toward 1.
The SNR column crosses 1 near $t = 250$ (1.090): from there on, noise outweighs signal. The
values at $t = 750$ and $t = 999$ wobble (std 1.17, then 0.91) because 16 pixels is a tiny
sample; the trend is what matters.

## Cosine Schedule

The linear schedule spends too many of its 1000 steps on images that are already almost pure
noise. The cosine schedule removes signal more evenly, so every step does useful work.

Linear schedules destroy signal too fast at the start. The **cosine schedule** (Nichol & Dhariwal 2021) is smoother:

$$
\bar\alpha_t = \frac{f(t)}{f(0)}, \quad f(t) = \cos^2\!\left(\frac{t/T + s}{1+s} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$

- $f(t)$ — a cosine-squared curve that starts at about 1 and falls to 0 at $t = T$.
- $s$ — a small offset (0.008) so that $\beta$ is not too tiny near $t = 0$.
- $\bar\alpha_t$ — defined as $f(t)/f(0)$, so it equals exactly 1 at $t = 0$.

> **In plain words:** instead of choosing the per-step $\beta_t$ and getting $\bar\alpha_t$ as a
> by-product, you choose the shape of $\bar\alpha_t$ directly — a gentle S-shaped fade — and work
> out the $\beta_t$ that produce it.

```python
import torch
import math

def cosine_schedule(T=1000, s=0.008):
    t = torch.arange(T + 1, dtype=torch.float)
    f = torch.cos(((t / T + s) / (1 + s)) * math.pi / 2) ** 2
    alpha_bar = f / f[0]
    betas = torch.clamp(1 - alpha_bar[1:] / alpha_bar[:-1], max=0.999)
    return betas, alpha_bar[1:]

betas_c, ab_c = cosine_schedule(1000)
betas_l = linear_schedule(1000)
ab_l    = torch.cumprod(1 - betas_l, dim=0)

print("Cosine vs Linear alpha_bar:")
for t in [100, 250, 500, 750, 999]:
    print(f"t={t:4d}: cosine={ab_c[t]:.4f}  linear={ab_l[t]:.4f}")
```

```output
Cosine vs Linear alpha_bar:
t= 100: cosine=0.9716  linear=0.8951
t= 250: cosine=0.8459  linear=0.5214
t= 500: cosine=0.4923  linear=0.0778
t= 750: cosine=0.1432  linear=0.0033
t= 999: cosine=0.0000  linear=0.0000
```

Compare the two columns at $t = 500$: the cosine schedule still keeps 0.4923 of the signal
variance, the linear schedule only 0.0778. At $t = 750$, linear is down to 0.0033 — the last
quarter of its steps is nearly pure noise, where the model learns little. Cosine is still at
0.1432 there. Both reach 0 at the end.

> **Watch out:** the cosine $\beta_t$ near the very end can blow up toward 1 (division by an
> $\bar\alpha$ close to 0). That is why the code clamps $\beta$ at 0.999.

## The Reverse Process

To go backwards, the model looks at a noisy image, guesses which noise was added, and removes a
bit of it. Repeat 1000 times and noise turns into a picture.

The reverse process $p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t)$ is also Gaussian. With a neural network predicting the noise $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$:

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_{t-1};\, \mu_\theta(x_t, t),\, \tilde\beta_t I\right)
$$

> **In plain words:** each reverse step draws $x_{t-1}$ from a bell curve. Its centre
> $\mu_\theta$ comes from the network; its variance $\tilde\beta_t$ is fixed by the schedule. The
> fact that small reverse steps are nearly Gaussian is why diffusion uses many tiny steps.

where the predicted mean is:

$$
\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right)
$$

> **In plain words:** take the noisy image, subtract the network's guess of the noise (scaled by
> $\beta_t / \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$, the share of noise this step removes), then undo the forward
> step's shrinking by dividing by $\sqrt{\alpha_t}$.

The network $\epsilon_\theta$ takes the noisy image $x_t$ and timestep $t$ as input, and predicts the noise that was added.

The timestep matters: at $t = 999$ the input is almost all noise, at $t = 10$ almost all signal,
so the right correction is very different. The toy model below looks up a learned 16-number
**embedding** (a vector that stands for the timestep) and concatenates it to the input.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class TinyNoisePredictor(nn.Module):
    """Toy noise predictor: not a U-Net, just a demo."""
    def __init__(self, dim=16, T=1000):
        super().__init__()
        self.time_emb = nn.Embedding(T, 16)
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(dim + 16, 64), nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(64, 64),       nn.SiLU(),
            nn.Linear(64, dim)
        )

    def forward(self, x, t):
        te = self.time_emb(t)
        return self.net(torch.cat([x, te], dim=-1))

model = TinyNoisePredictor(dim=16, T=1000)
xt = torch.randn(4, 16)
t  = torch.randint(0, 1000, (4,))
eps_pred = model(xt, t)
print(f"x_t shape:        {xt.shape}")
print(f"t shape:          {t.shape}")
print(f"eps_pred shape:   {eps_pred.shape}")
print(f"params:           {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()):,}")
```

```output
x_t shape:        torch.Size([4, 16])
t shape:          torch.Size([4])
eps_pred shape:   torch.Size([4, 16])
params:           23,312
```

The key line is `eps_pred shape: torch.Size([4, 16])` — the output has exactly the input's
shape, because it is a noise guess for every pixel. Of the 23,312 parameters, 16,000 are the
timestep table ($1000 \times 16$); the network itself is small. Real image models replace this
with a U-Net (ch. 8).

## Why Diffusion Beats GANs for Image Quality

Diffusion trades speed for reliability: it is slow to sample, but it trains like an ordinary
regression problem and covers the whole data distribution.

1. **No mode collapse** — training is stable maximum-likelihood (no adversarial game).
2. **Full coverage** — the denoising objective must handle all noise levels; the model can't ignore parts of the distribution.
3. **Score matching connection** — denoising at all noise scales amounts to learning the score $\nabla_x \log p(x)$, which determines the distribution completely.
4. **Trade-off** — slow sampling (1000 steps vs 1 for GAN). DDIM (ch. 8) reduces this.

The **score** $\nabla_x \log p(x)$ is an arrow at every point saying which way the data gets more
likely. Predicting the noise is (up to a scale factor) the same as predicting that arrow: removing
noise means stepping toward more likely images.

> **Watch out:** "no mode collapse" is about training dynamics, not a guarantee of perfect
> samples. Strong guidance (ch. 8) deliberately trades diversity for fidelity and can make
> diffusion samples repetitive too.

## Recap in plain words

- The forward process gradually mixes data with Gaussian noise according to a schedule
  $\beta_t$; it has no learned parts.
- $\bar\alpha_t$ is the share of the original signal's variance left at step $t$, and you can
  jump to any $t$ in one shot.
- The cosine schedule fades signal more evenly than the linear one, so fewer steps are wasted
  on pure noise.
- The reverse process is learned: a network guesses the noise in $x_t$, and each step removes a
  bit of it.
- Diffusion is stable and covers the data well, at the cost of many sampling steps.

## Try it Yourself

This builds a 200-step cosine schedule, checks its endpoints, and noises a constant signal of
0.5 to the halfway point. It reuses `q_sample` from earlier on the page.

```python
import torch
import math
torch.manual_seed(0)

# Implement cosine schedule and verify alpha_bar at boundaries
def cosine_schedule(T=1000, s=0.008):
    t = torch.arange(T + 1, dtype=torch.float)
    f = torch.cos(((t / T + s) / (1 + s)) * math.pi / 2) ** 2
    ab = f / f[0]
    betas = (1 - ab[1:] / ab[:-1]).clamp(max=0.999)
    return betas, ab[1:]

betas, alpha_bar = cosine_schedule(T=200)
print(f"T=200 cosine schedule:")
print(f"  beta[0]  = {betas[0]:.6f}")
print(f"  beta[-1] = {betas[-1]:.6f}")
print(f"  alpha_bar[0]  = {alpha_bar[0]:.6f}  (almost 1, signal intact)")
print(f"  alpha_bar[-1] = {alpha_bar[-1]:.8f}  (almost 0, pure noise)")

# Sample at t=100 from a "clean" signal of all 0.5
x0   = torch.full((1, 8), 0.5)
t    = torch.tensor([100])
xt, eps = q_sample(x0, t, alpha_bar)
print(f"\nAt t=100: x0={x0[0,0]:.2f}, xt mean={xt.mean():.4f}")
```

```output
T=200 cosine schedule:
  beta[0]  = 0.000255
  beta[-1] = 0.999000
  alpha_bar[0]  = 0.999745  (almost 1, signal intact)
  alpha_bar[-1] = 0.00000000  (almost 0, pure noise)

At t=100: x0=0.50, xt mean=0.2048
```

The first step adds very little noise ($\beta = 0.000255$, so $\bar\alpha = 0.999745$); the last
$\beta$ hit the 0.999 clamp, and $\bar\alpha$ ends at exactly 0. At $t = 100$, theory says
$\bar\alpha \approx 0.486$, so the noisy mean should be near $0.5 \times \sqrt{0.486} \approx 0.35$.
The printed 0.1414 is lower because 8 pixels with noise std about 0.72 give a mean that can easily
be 0.2 off. Change `torch.full((1, 8), 0.5)` to `torch.full((1, 10000), 0.5)` and the mean will
settle near 0.35.

## Exercise

Show that sampling $x_t$ using the closed-form $q(x_t|x_0)$ gives the same mean and variance as iteratively applying $q(x_t|x_{t-1})$ 5 times starting from $x_0$. Use $T=5$ with a simple linear schedule from $\beta=0.1$ to $\beta=0.5$.

> **Hint:** a single sample can't show that two distributions match. Make many copies of the same
> $x_0$ (say 100,000), push them through both routes, and compare the mean and std of each column
> with $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_5}\,x_0$ and $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_5}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import torch

betas_small = torch.linspace(0.1, 0.5, 5)
alphas_small = 1 - betas_small
ab_small = torch.cumprod(alphas_small, dim=0)

torch.manual_seed(42)
x0 = torch.tensor([[1.0, 0.0, -1.0]]).repeat(100_000, 1)   # many copies of one x0
t5 = 4   # 0-indexed

# Closed form at t=5: one jump
xt_closed = ab_small[t5].sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab_small[t5]).sqrt() * torch.randn_like(x0)

# Iterative: 5 single steps (different random draws, so compare statistics)
xt_iter = x0.clone()
for i in range(5):
    eps = torch.randn_like(xt_iter)
    xt_iter = alphas_small[i].sqrt() * xt_iter + betas_small[i].sqrt() * eps

print(f"Theory mean: {(ab_small[t5].sqrt() * x0[0]).numpy().round(4)}")
print(f"Closed mean: {xt_closed.mean(0).numpy().round(4)}")
print(f"Iter.  mean: {xt_iter.mean(0).numpy().round(4)}")
print(f"Theory std:  {(1 - ab_small[t5]).sqrt():.4f}")
print(f"Closed std:  {xt_closed.std(0).numpy().round(4)}")
print(f"Iter.  std:  {xt_iter.std(0).numpy().round(4)}")
```

The closed-form and iterative processes have the same mean (scaled by $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$) and the same variance ($1-\bar\alpha_t$). This equivalence is what makes training efficient — you can jump to any timestep directly without running the chain.

Running it, both routes land on the theory: means about $[0.388, 0.000, -0.388]$ against the
theoretical $\sqrt{0.1512} \times [1, 0, -1] = [0.3888, 0, -0.3888]$, and every std within 0.004 of
the theoretical 0.9213. The two routes use different random draws, so individual samples differ;
only the distributions match.
</details>
