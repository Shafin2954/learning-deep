## The Core Idea

A flow takes a plain bell curve and bends, stretches, and squeezes it, step by step, into the
shape of your data. Because every step can be undone exactly, you can always trace a data point
back to the bell curve and read off its exact probability.

A normalizing flow transforms a simple distribution (standard Gaussian) into a complex one via a sequence of invertible, differentiable functions. The key requirement: you can compute the Jacobian determinant efficiently.

- **Invertible** means every output comes from exactly one input, so the step can be run
  backwards.
- **Differentiable** means smooth enough to take slopes, so you can train it by gradient.
- The **Jacobian** is the matrix of all slopes of the outputs with respect to the inputs. Its
  **determinant** says how much the function stretches or squeezes a tiny region of space: a
  determinant of 2 doubles a small area, 0.5 halves it.

> **Picture it:** a lump of clay with a fixed amount of material (total probability = 1). Where
> you stretch the clay thin, the density drops; where you squash it, the density rises. The
> Jacobian determinant is the local "stretch factor" that keeps the books balanced.

If $z \sim p_z(z)$ and $x = f(z)$ with $f$ invertible, the change-of-variables formula gives:

$$
\log p_x(x) = \log p_z(f^{-1}(x)) + \log \left|\det \frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial x}\right|
$$

> **In plain words:** to score a data point $x$, map it back to its code $f^{-1}(x)$, score the
> code under the simple bell curve, then correct for how much the mapping stretched space at
> that point. Stretched regions get lower density; compressed regions get higher density.

Or equivalently, writing $z = f^{-1}(x) = g(x)$:

$$
\log p_x(x) = \log p_z(g(x)) + \log |\det J_g(x)|
$$

> **In plain words:** same formula, with $g$ naming the "data to code" direction and $J_g$ its
> Jacobian. Training a flow means maximizing this number on real data.

This is **exact** — no variational bound, no discriminator. You can compute the exact log-likelihood of any data point.

The smallest flow is one line: $x = e^z$. Going back, $g(x) = \log x$, whose slope is
$1/x$, so the correction term is $\log(1/x) = -\log x$. This builds the **log-normal**
distribution.

```python
import torch
import numpy as np

# Simple 1D example: flow x = exp(z), z ~ N(0, 1)
# p_x(x) = p_z(log(x)) / |x|   (change of variables)
def log_prob_transformed(x):
    z = torch.log(x)              # g(x) = log(x)
    log_pz = -0.5 * z**2 - 0.5 * np.log(2*np.pi)   # N(0,1)
    log_det = -torch.log(x)       # log|dg/dx| = log(1/x) = -log(x)
    return log_pz + log_det

x = torch.tensor([0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
lp = log_prob_transformed(x)
print("x     | log p(x)")
for xi, lpi in zip(x, lp):
    print(f"{xi:.1f}   | {lpi:.4f}")
```

```output
x     | log p(x)
0.5   | -0.4660
1.0   | -0.9189
2.0   | -1.8523
3.0   | -2.6210
```

At $x = 1$, the code is $z = 0$ and the stretch correction is 0, so you get the bell curve's peak,
$-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi) \approx -0.9189$. At $x = 0.5$ the log-density is *higher* ($-0.4660$):
mapping $z$ through $e^z$ squeezes all negative codes into $(0, 1)$, piling probability up there.
At $x = 3$ it falls to $-2.6210$. These match the textbook log-normal density exactly.

## Affine Coupling Layers (RealNVP)

A general neural network's Jacobian determinant is expensive to compute. Coupling layers are
designed so that the determinant is the product of a few numbers you already have.

Computing $\det J$ for an arbitrary NN is $O(d^3)$. RealNVP uses coupling layers with triangular Jacobians — $O(d)$.

$O(d^3)$ means the cost grows with the cube of the dimension $d$: for a $64 \times 64$ colour
image ($d = 12{,}288$), that is about $1.9 \times 10^{12}$ operations per determinant. A
**triangular** matrix (zeros on one side of the diagonal) has a determinant equal to the product
of its diagonal, which is $O(d)$.

Split $x$ into two halves: $x_1$ and $x_2$.
- $x_1$ passes through unchanged.
- $x_2$ is scaled and shifted by functions of $x_1$:

$$
y_1 = x_1 \qquad y_2 = x_2 \odot e^{s(x_1)} + t(x_1)
$$

> **In plain words:** keep the first half as it is. Multiply each entry of the second half by a
> scale factor $e^{s}$ and add a shift $t$, where both $s$ and $t$ are computed by small
> networks that look only at the first half. $e^{s}$ is always positive, so the scale can never be
> zero and the step can always be undone.

> **Picture it:** two people walking side by side. One walks normally; the other changes stride
> and position based on what the first one is doing. If you know where the first person is, you
> can always work out where the second person started.

The Jacobian is lower-triangular with diagonal $e^{s(x_1)}$, so:

$$
\log |\det J| = \sum_i s_i(x_1)
$$

> **In plain words:** the log of the stretch factor is the sum of the scale outputs $s_i$. No
> matrix work at all. The networks $s$ and $t$ can be as complicated as you like, because you
> never need to invert them — inversion only needs to subtract $t$ and divide by $e^{s}$.

An **affine** map is one that scales and shifts. The layer below implements both directions.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)

class CouplingLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        half = dim // 2
        # s and t are NNs mapping x1 → scale/shift for x2
        self.scale_net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(half, 32), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(32, dim - half))
        self.shift_net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(half, 32), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(32, dim - half))

    def forward(self, x):
        x1, x2 = x.chunk(2, dim=-1)
        s = self.scale_net(x1)
        t = self.shift_net(x1)
        y2 = x2 * s.exp() + t
        log_det = s.sum(-1)       # sum over output dims
        return torch.cat([x1, y2], dim=-1), log_det

    def inverse(self, y):
        y1, y2 = y.chunk(2, dim=-1)
        s = self.scale_net(y1)
        t = self.shift_net(y1)
        x2 = (y2 - t) * (-s).exp()
        return torch.cat([y1, x2], dim=-1)

layer = CouplingLayer(dim=4)
x = torch.randn(8, 4)
y, log_det = layer(x)
x_recon = layer.inverse(y)
print(f"Input  x: {x[0].detach().numpy().round(3)}")
print(f"Output y: {y[0].detach().numpy().round(3)}")
print(f"Reconstructed x: {x_recon[0].detach().numpy().round(3)}")
print(f"Max recon error: {(x - x_recon).abs().max().item():.2e}")
print(f"Log-det per sample: {log_det[:3].detach().numpy().round(3)}")
```

```output
Input  x: [ 1.935  1.008  1.005 -0.434]
Output y: [ 1.935  1.008  1.068 -0.813]
Reconstructed x: [ 1.935  1.008  1.005 -0.434]
Max recon error: 1.19e-07
Log-det per sample: [ 0.429  0.441 -0.13 ]
```

The first two entries of the output (1.935, 1.008) are identical to the input — that is
the half that passes through. The third entry changed from 1.005 to 1.068 and the fourth from
$-0.434$ to $-0.813$. The reconstruction matches the input to $1.19 \times 10^{-7}$, which is
float32 rounding noise: the inverse is exact by construction, not learned. The log-dets (0.429,
0.441, $-0.13$) differ per sample because $s$ depends on each sample's first half.

## Building a Full Flow

One coupling layer never changes its first half. Stack several, swapping which half is frozen
each time, and every dimension gets transformed.

Stack multiple coupling layers, alternating which dimensions are masked so all dimensions eventually interact.

Here "masked" means held fixed. The code swaps halves with `torch.flip` before odd-numbered
layers and swaps back after. The total log-det is the sum of each layer's log-det, because
stretch factors multiply and logs add. `log_prob` sends $x$ to $z$, scores $z$ under the
standard normal, and adds the total log-det. Sampling runs the other way: draw $z$ and call
`inverse`.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)

class RealNVP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim=2, n_layers=6):
        super().__init__()
        self.layers = nn.ModuleList([CouplingLayer(dim) for _ in range(n_layers)])

    def forward(self, x):
        log_det_total = torch.zeros(x.size(0))
        for i, layer in enumerate(self.layers):
            if i % 2 == 1:
                x = torch.flip(x, dims=[-1])   # alternate which half is masked
            x, ld = layer(x)
            if i % 2 == 1:
                x = torch.flip(x, dims=[-1])
            log_det_total += ld
        return x, log_det_total    # z, log_det

    def inverse(self, z):
        for i, layer in reversed(list(enumerate(self.layers))):
            if i % 2 == 1:
                z = torch.flip(z, dims=[-1])
            z = layer.inverse(z)
            if i % 2 == 1:
                z = torch.flip(z, dims=[-1])
        return z

    def log_prob(self, x):
        z, log_det = self.forward(x)
        log_pz = -0.5 * (z**2 + torch.log(torch.tensor(2*3.14159))).sum(-1)
        return log_pz + log_det

flow = RealNVP(dim=2, n_layers=6)
x = torch.randn(32, 2)
lp = flow.log_prob(x)
print(f"Log probabilities: min={lp.min():.2f}, mean={lp.mean():.2f}, max={lp.max():.2f}")

# Sample: draw z from N(0,I), invert through flow
z_samples = torch.randn(100, 2)
x_samples  = flow.inverse(z_samples)
print(f"Samples shape: {x_samples.shape}")
print(f"Sample range: [{x_samples.min():.2f}, {x_samples.max():.2f}]")
```

```output
Log probabilities: min=-17.50, mean=-4.76, max=-1.05
Samples shape: torch.Size([100, 2])
Sample range: [-5.41, 2.47]
```

This flow is untrained, so the numbers only show the machinery works. Mean log-probability on
standard-normal inputs is $-4.76$; a perfect model of $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ in 2-D would score about
$-2.84$ ($= -\log(2\pi) - 1$). The random layers stretch space unevenly — one point scores
$-17.50$ — and the samples spread from $-5.41$ to 2.47, lopsided compared with the bell curve
they came from.

## Training a Flow

Training a flow is the most direct recipe in this course: make the real data as probable as
possible. There is nothing else to balance.

Maximize average log-likelihood — no reconstruction term, no discriminator.

The loss is the **negative log-likelihood (NLL)**, the average of $-\log p(x)$ over a batch;
lower is better. The data is **two moons**: two interlocking half-circles, a standard 2-D test
that a single bell curve can't fit.

```python
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

# Train flow on 2D two-moons shape
def make_moons(n=1000):
    import numpy as np
    theta1 = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n//2)
    theta2 = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n//2)
    x1 = np.stack([np.cos(theta1), np.sin(theta1)], axis=1)
    x2 = np.stack([1 - np.cos(theta2), 1 - np.sin(theta2) - 0.5], axis=1)
    X  = np.vstack([x1, x2]) + np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal((n, 2)) * 0.1
    return torch.tensor(X, dtype=torch.float32)

torch.manual_seed(0)
data  = make_moons(2000)
flow  = RealNVP(dim=2, n_layers=8)
opt   = torch.optim.Adam(flow.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

for epoch in range(1, 6):
    idx = torch.randperm(len(data))
    losses = []
    for i in range(0, len(data), 128):
        x = data[idx[i:i+128]]
        loss = -flow.log_prob(x).mean()
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
        losses.append(loss.item())
    print(f"Epoch {epoch}  NLL={sum(losses)/len(losses):.3f}")
```

```output
Epoch 1  NLL=3.165
Epoch 2  NLL=2.151
Epoch 3  NLL=1.996
Epoch 4  NLL=1.939
Epoch 5  NLL=1.904
```

The NLL drops from 3.165 to 1.904 nats per point in 5 epochs (a **nat** is the unit of log
probability when using natural logs). The big drop happens in the first two epochs, after which
gains shrink. Keep this in perspective: the best single 2-D Gaussian fitted to this data scores
about 1.91. After 5 epochs the flow is only level with "one blob"; it needs many more epochs
(at 100 epochs the same loop reaches an NLL of about 1.08) before the two moon shapes appear in
samples.

> **Watch out:** always compare a generative model's NLL with a simple baseline such as one
> Gaussian. A falling loss proves the model is learning, not that it has learned anything a
> five-number model (two means, three covariance entries) couldn't.

> **Watch out:** `torch.manual_seed` does not seed NumPy. That is why `make_moons` draws its
> noise from `np.random.default_rng(0)`; with plain `np.random.randn` the NLL values would change
> from run to run in the third decimal.

## Flows vs VAEs

Flows and VAEs both decode a bell-curve code into data, but flows insist on being exactly
reversible, and pay for it with a code as large as the data.

| Property | Normalizing Flow | VAE |
|----------|-----------------|-----|
| Likelihood | Exact | Lower bound (ELBO) |
| Latent space | Same dim as $x$ | Smaller than $x$ |
| Invertibility | Required | Not required |
| Training | NLL | ELBO |
| Sampling | Fast (invert flow) | Fast (decode from $z$) |

The "same dim" row is forced: an invertible map can't lose information, so it can't squeeze
784 numbers into 32. That makes flows memory-hungry on images and is one reason diffusion and
latent models took over.

## Recap in plain words

- A flow is a chain of reversible, smooth steps from a bell curve to the data.
- The change-of-variables formula gives the exact log-likelihood: score the code, then correct
  for stretching with the log Jacobian determinant.
- Affine coupling layers keep half the input fixed and scale-and-shift the other half, so their
  log-det is a cheap sum of scales.
- Stacking coupling layers with alternating halves lets every dimension change.
- Flows train by plain maximum likelihood, and their codes are as large as the data.

## Try it Yourself

This checks the two promises of a coupling flow on 6-dimensional inputs: the inverse is exact,
and log-dets add across layers.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

# Test the coupling layer's invertibility guarantee
layer = CouplingLayer(dim=6)
x_orig = torch.randn(4, 6)
y, log_det = layer(x_orig)
x_back = layer.inverse(y)

print("Invertibility check:")
for i in range(4):
    err = (x_orig[i] - x_back[i]).abs().max().item()
    print(f"  Sample {i}: max error = {err:.2e}")

# Show that log_det accumulates over layers
flow2 = RealNVP(dim=6, n_layers=4)
x = torch.randn(8, 6)
_, total_ld = flow2(x)
print(f"\nTotal log-det (4 layers): mean={total_ld.mean():.3f}")
```

```output
Invertibility check:
  Sample 0: max error = 0.00e+00
  Sample 1: max error = 5.96e-08
  Sample 2: max error = 1.19e-07
  Sample 3: max error = 2.38e-07

Total log-det (4 layers): mean=-0.392
```

All four reconstruction errors are at or below $2.38 \times 10^{-7}$ — sample 0 is exactly 0 —
which is float32 rounding, not approximation. The 4-layer flow's mean total log-det is $-0.392$:
on average it slightly compresses space. Swap `Tanh` for `ReLU` in `CouplingLayer` and check that
invertibility still holds (it does: invertibility comes from the coupling structure, not from
$s$ and $t$). Then multiply the output of `scale_net` by 10 and watch the errors grow, since
$e^{s}$ becomes huge and float32 runs out of precision.

## Exercise

Show that a flow trained on a Gaussian mixture can assign reasonable log-likelihoods to in-distribution vs out-of-distribution points. Train the `RealNVP` on samples from $\mathcal{N}(-2,0.5)$ and evaluate log-prob on both in-distribution points (near -2) and OOD points (near +5).

> **Hint:** **OOD** means out-of-distribution — points unlike the training data. Train with the
> NLL loss from the Training a Flow section for a few hundred mini-batch steps, then compare
> `flow.log_prob(...).mean()` on the two test sets under `torch.no_grad()`.

<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import torch

torch.manual_seed(7)
in_dist  = torch.randn(1000,2)*0.5 + torch.tensor([-2., 0.])
flow_ood = RealNVP(dim=2, n_layers=6)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(flow_ood.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

for _ in range(200):
    idx = torch.randperm(len(in_dist))[:64]
    loss = -flow_ood.log_prob(in_dist[idx]).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    x_in  = torch.randn(50,2)*0.3 + torch.tensor([-2., 0.])
    x_ood = torch.randn(50,2)*0.3 + torch.tensor([5., 0.])
    lp_in  = flow_ood.log_prob(x_in).mean()
    lp_ood = flow_ood.log_prob(x_ood).mean()
    print(f"In-distribution  log-prob: {lp_in:.2f}")
    print(f"Out-of-distribution log-prob: {lp_ood:.2f}")
    print(f"Difference: {lp_in - lp_ood:.2f} nats")
```

The flow correctly assigns much lower log-probability to OOD points. This is the anomaly detection use case — likelihood-based models give you a natural score.

Running it gives about $-0.84$ for points near $(-2, 0)$ and $-84.42$ for points near $(5, 0)$, a
gap of 83.59 nats — the OOD points are about $e^{83}$ times less likely. Be careful generalizing:
here the OOD points are far from the data in raw space, which is the easy case. On images, flows
famously give high likelihood to some OOD datasets, so check before trusting the score.
