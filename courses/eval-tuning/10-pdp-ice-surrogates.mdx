## What shape did the model learn?

A linear model hands you its story in one number per feature: "each extra month of tenure adds
0.6 to spend". A forest or a boosted model has no such number. The tools in this chapter recover
that story by turning one knob at a time and watching how the predictions move.

SHAP (ch. 9) tells you how much a feature contributed to one prediction. Partial dependence asks
a different question: **as this feature sweeps across its range, how does the model's output move
on average?** It is the closest thing to reading a coefficient out of a black box.

A **black box** is a model whose inner rules are too complex to read directly. The demo predicts
monthly spend from three features: tenure (months as a customer), discount (percent off), and
support tickets. The data hides an **interaction** — the effect of one feature depends on another:
a discount *raises* spend for customers under 24 months and *lowers* it for longer-tenured ones.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 4_000
tenure = rng.uniform(0, 60, n).round(1)                    # months as a customer
discount = rng.uniform(0, 40, n).round(1)                  # percent off
support = rng.poisson(1.2, n)                              # support tickets last quarter

# a discount retains new customers and annoys long-tenured ones (they feel over-charged before)
effect_of_discount = np.where(tenure < 24, +0.9, -0.7) * (discount / 40)
spend = (50 + 0.6 * tenure + 20 * effect_of_discount - 3 * support
         + rng.normal(0, 4, n)).round(2)

df = pd.DataFrame({"tenure": tenure, "discount": discount, "support_tickets": support})
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(df, spend, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)
model = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=150, min_samples_leaf=10,
                              random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
print(f"train R2 {model.score(Xtr, ytr):.4f} | test R2 {model.score(Xte, yte):.4f}")
print(f"mean spend {ytr.mean():.2f}")
```

```output
train R2 0.8265 | test R2 0.7650
mean spend 63.70
```

The forest explains 76.5% of the variation in spend on held-out rows ($R^2$ 0.7650), against
0.8265 on training rows — a modest gap, so it has learned real structure.

There is a real interaction buried in that data. Watch partial dependence walk straight past it.

## Partial dependence, from the definition

To see how spend depends on discount, give *every* customer the same discount, ask the model
for all their predictions, and average. Repeat for 0%, 10%, 20%, and so on. The line through
those averages is the partial dependence.

$$
\mathrm{PD}_j(z) = \mathbb{E}_{X_{-j}}\bigl[f(z, X_{-j})\bigr]
\;\approx\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f\bigl(z,\; x^{(i)}_{-j}\bigr)
$$

> **In plain words:** $\mathrm{PD}_j(z)$, the partial dependence of feature $j$ at value $z$, is the
> average prediction when feature $j$ is forced to $z$ and all other features ($X_{-j}$) keep each
> row's real values. The right side is how you compute it: loop over the $n$ rows, replace feature
> $j$ by $z$ in row $i$, predict, and average.

Set feature $j$ to $z$ for **every** row, predict, average. Repeat across a grid of $z$. That is
the whole algorithm — the same interventional replacement SHAP uses, restricted to one feature.

```python
def partial_dependence(model, X, feature, grid):
    out = []
    for z in grid:
        Xz = X.copy()
        Xz[feature] = z
        out.append(float(model.predict(Xz).mean()))
    return np.array(out)

sample = Xte.sample(400, random_state=0)
grid = np.linspace(0, 40, 5)
pd_discount = partial_dependence(model, sample, "discount", grid)
for z, v in zip(grid, pd_discount):
    print(f"discount {z:5.1f}%  ->  mean predicted spend {v:7.3f}")
print(f"\ntotal swing across the range: {pd_discount.max() - pd_discount.min():.3f}")
```

```output
discount   0.0%  ->  mean predicted spend  64.604
discount  10.0%  ->  mean predicted spend  64.762
discount  20.0%  ->  mean predicted spend  63.596
discount  30.0%  ->  mean predicted spend  63.460
discount  40.0%  ->  mean predicted spend  62.940

total swing across the range: 1.822
```

The averages barely move: 64.604 at 0% discount, 62.940 at 40%. The whole swing is 1.822 units of
spend, which looks like "discount has almost no effect, maybe a slight negative".

Read that as "discount barely matters". It is wrong, and it is wrong in the specific way partial
dependence is always wrong: **averaging destroys anything that cancels**.

> **Picture it:** half a class finds a new teaching method much better and the other half much
> worse. The class average does not change. "The method has no effect" is true of the average and
> false of every student.

Before fixing that, a check against the library. scikit-learn's `partial_dependence` should give
the same numbers once its two hidden choices are matched.

```python
from sklearn.inspection import partial_dependence as sk_pd

sk = sk_pd(model, sample, features=["discount"], grid_resolution=9,
           kind="average", method="brute")               # "recursion" is a different estimator
sk_grid = np.asarray(sk["grid_values"][0])          # sklearn picks its own grid (percentiles)
print("sklearn grid:", np.round(sk_grid, 2))
print("sklearn PD  :", np.round(sk["average"][0], 3))
print("ours, same  :", np.round(partial_dependence(model, sample, "discount", sk_grid), 3))
```

```output
sklearn grid: [ 1.7   6.17 10.64 15.11 19.58 24.05 28.52 32.99 37.46]
sklearn PD  : [64.613 65.059 64.694 64.326 63.558 63.709 63.49  63.498 63.028]
ours, same  : [64.613 65.059 64.694 64.326 63.558 63.709 63.49  63.498 63.028]
```

The two bottom lines are identical at all 9 grid points. Notice sklearn's grid starts at 1.7 and
ends at 37.46, not at 0 and 40: it stays inside the bulk of the observed data.

Identical, once you match two things sklearn chooses for you. It picks its grid by *percentile* of
the observed feature rather than by `linspace`, so it never asks about values the data does not
contain. And `method` defaults to `"recursion"` for tree ensembles, which walks the trees instead
of replacing values — faster, but a different estimator that ignores the rows entirely. Pass
`method="brute"` when you want the definition above, which is the one that matches SHAP's
interventional value function.

## ICE: one curve per row, no averaging

Skip the averaging step. Draw one line per customer showing how *their* prediction changes as the
discount changes. If all lines go the same way, the average is fine. If they go different ways,
the average was lying.

Individual Conditional Expectation plots the same sweep **per row** instead of averaging. If the
curves fan out, the average was hiding something.

An **ICE curve** (individual conditional expectation) is exactly that per-row line. Its **slope**
— how much the prediction rises per unit of the feature — summarises it in one number.

```python
def ice(model, X, feature, grid):
    curves = []
    for z in grid:
        Xz = X.copy()
        Xz[feature] = z
        curves.append(model.predict(Xz))
    return np.array(curves).T                              # (rows, grid)

sub = Xte.sample(200, random_state=1)
curves = ice(model, sub, "discount", grid)
slopes = (curves[:, -1] - curves[:, 0]) / (grid[-1] - grid[0])
print(f"per-row slope of spend vs discount:")
print(f"  mean {slopes.mean():+.4f}  (this is the PDP slope)")
print(f"  rows with a POSITIVE slope: {(slopes > 0).mean():.0%}")
print(f"  rows with a NEGATIVE slope: {(slopes < 0).mean():.0%}")
print(f"  10th / 90th percentile slope: {np.percentile(slopes, 10):+.4f} / "
      f"{np.percentile(slopes, 90):+.4f}")
```

```output
per-row slope of spend vs discount:
  mean -0.0009  (this is the PDP slope)
  rows with a POSITIVE slope: 44%
  rows with a NEGATIVE slope: 56%
  10th / 90th percentile slope: -0.3230 / +0.4020
```

The average slope is $-0.0009$, essentially zero, matching the flat PDP. But 44% of customers have
a positive slope and 56% a negative one, and the 10th and 90th percentiles are $-0.3230$ and
$+0.4020$ — big effects in both directions.

Half the customers respond positively to a discount and half negatively, so the average is flat.
A PDP alone would have told the business "discounts do nothing"; the ICE spread says "discounts do
a lot, in opposite directions, to different people".

### Splitting the ICE curves reveals the driver

Once you know the curves fan out, the next question is *who* goes up and *who* goes down. Split
the curves by a second feature you suspect is responsible.

A feature that changes another feature's effect is called a **moderator**. Here the suspect is
tenure, split at 24 months.

```python
new_cust = sub["tenure"].values < 24
for label, mask in (("tenure < 24 months", new_cust), ("tenure >= 24 months", ~new_cust)):
    mean_curve = curves[mask].mean(axis=0)
    print(f"{label:22} n={mask.sum():>3} | "
          f"spend at 0% {mean_curve[0]:7.2f} -> at 40% {mean_curve[-1]:7.2f} "
          f"({mean_curve[-1] - mean_curve[0]:+.2f})")
```

```output
tenure < 24 months     n= 87 | spend at 0%   54.37 -> at 40%   69.44 (+15.07)
tenure >= 24 months    n=113 | spend at 0%   70.29 -> at 40%   58.63 (-11.66)
```

For the 87 newer customers, a 40% discount raises predicted spend by 15.07. For the 113 longer-
tenured ones it lowers it by 11.66. The two groups pull in opposite directions, and the PDP showed
only their sum.

That is the interaction the data generator put in, recovered from the model. The workflow: PDP
looks flat, so check the ICE spread, then group the curves by a candidate moderator. Colouring ICE
curves by a second feature is the standard visual version of these three lines.

### Centred ICE

Raw ICE curves are stacked at different heights because rows differ in their other features.
Subtract each curve's value at the left edge and only the *shape* remains:

**Centred ICE** (also called c-ICE) starts every curve at zero, so you compare changes, not levels.

```python
centred = curves - curves[:, [0]]
print(f"{'discount':>9} {'mean':>8} {'new custs':>11} {'old custs':>11}")
for j, z in enumerate(grid):
    print(f"{z:>9.1f} {centred[:, j].mean():>+8.2f} "
          f"{centred[new_cust, j].mean():>+11.2f} {centred[~new_cust, j].mean():>+11.2f}")
```

```output
 discount     mean   new custs   old custs
      0.0    +0.00       +0.00       +0.00
     10.0    +0.51       +4.01       -2.19
     20.0    -0.30       +6.70       -5.69
     30.0    +0.09      +11.81       -8.94
     40.0    -0.04      +15.07      -11.66
```

Read across the 40% row: $+15.07$ for new customers, $-11.66$ for old ones, $-0.04$ on average.

The centred view is what you show a stakeholder: from a common starting point, a 40% discount is
worth +15.07 to a new customer and -11.66 to a long-tenured one, and the `mean` column — never
more than 0.51 away from zero at any point on the grid — is the flat line that told you nothing.

## PDP extrapolates into data that does not exist

Forcing every row to the same value creates customers that cannot exist: a brand-new customer with
the credit limit of a five-year one. The model was never trained on such rows, so its predictions
for them are guesses.

The replacement step evaluates $f(z, x_{-j})$ for every row, including combinations that never
occur. With correlated features that means asking the model about impossible customers.

The demo adds `credit_limit`, which is almost a copy of tenure (it grows by 8 per month of tenure,
plus noise), and has **no effect on spend at all** in the generator. For each grid point it also
counts how many rows really have a credit limit within 40 of it.

```python
df_corr = df.copy()
df_corr["credit_limit"] = (200 + 8 * df_corr["tenure"] + rng.normal(0, 15, n)).round(0)
Xc_tr, Xc_te, yc_tr, yc_te = train_test_split(df_corr, spend, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)
mc = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=150, min_samples_leaf=10,
                           random_state=0).fit(Xc_tr, yc_tr)
corr = np.corrcoef(df_corr["tenure"], df_corr["credit_limit"])[0, 1]

sample_c = Xc_te.sample(400, random_state=0)
grid_limit = np.linspace(df_corr["credit_limit"].quantile(0.05),
                         df_corr["credit_limit"].quantile(0.95), 7)
pd_limit = partial_dependence(mc, sample_c, "credit_limit", grid_limit)
print(f"corr(tenure, credit_limit) = {corr:.3f}")
for z, v in zip(grid_limit, pd_limit):
    share = float(((sample_c["credit_limit"] - z).abs() < 40).mean())
    print(f"limit {z:6.0f} -> PDP {v:7.3f}   (rows actually near this value: {share:.0%})")
```

```output
corr(tenure, credit_limit) = 0.994
limit    222 -> PDP  62.069   (rows actually near this value: 12%)
limit    294 -> PDP  62.926   (rows actually near this value: 15%)
limit    367 -> PDP  63.459   (rows actually near this value: 16%)
limit    439 -> PDP  63.612   (rows actually near this value: 14%)
limit    511 -> PDP  64.216   (rows actually near this value: 20%)
limit    584 -> PDP  65.176   (rows actually near this value: 18%)
limit    656 -> PDP  65.997   (rows actually near this value: 12%)
```

The correlation is 0.994 — tenure and credit limit move almost in lockstep. The PDP rises from
62.069 to 65.997 across the range, even though credit limit does nothing in the generator. And the
last column shows that at each grid point only 12% to 20% of rows actually have a credit limit nearby.

Every PDP point averages over 400 rows, but only 12–20% of them plausibly *could* have that credit
limit given their tenure. The rest are fabricated customers, and the model's answer about them is
an extrapolation you have no way to check.

> **Watch out:** **extrapolation** means predicting outside the region the model was trained on.
> Tree models answer every question confidently, even about impossible rows, so nothing in the
> output warns you.

### ALE: only ask about differences that are local

**Accumulated Local Effects (ALE)** avoids made-up rows. It cuts the feature into small bins, and
inside each bin it only moves the rows that are already there, and only by a little — from the
bin's lower edge to its upper edge. Then it adds up those small changes bin by bin.

Accumulated Local Effects fixes this by staying local: inside each bin of the feature,
compute the prediction *difference* between the bin edges **for the rows that are actually in that
bin**, then accumulate.

$$
\mathrm{ALE}_j(z) = \sum_{k:\,z_k \le z}\;
\frac{1}{|S_k|}\sum_{i \in S_k}\bigl[f(z_k, x^{(i)}_{-j}) - f(z_{k-1}, x^{(i)}_{-j})\bigr]
$$

> **In plain words:**
> - $z_0, z_1, \dots$ are the bin edges; $S_k$ is the set of rows whose value falls in bin $k$, and
>   $|S_k|$ is how many there are.
> - For each row in bin $k$, compute the prediction at the bin's upper edge $z_k$ minus the prediction
>   at its lower edge $z_{k-1}$, keeping the row's other features.
> - Average that difference within the bin, then add up the bin averages for every bin up to $z$.

> **Picture it:** measuring the slope of a hill by walking it in short steps and noting how much
> you climb in each step, then adding the climbs. PDP instead teleports everyone to the same
> spot on the hill — including people who would have to stand in mid-air.

```python
def ale(model, X, feature, n_bins=8):
    edges = np.quantile(X[feature], np.linspace(0, 1, n_bins + 1))
    edges = np.unique(edges)
    idx = np.clip(np.searchsorted(edges, X[feature], side="left") - 1, 0, len(edges) - 2)
    deltas = np.zeros(len(edges) - 1)
    for k in range(len(edges) - 1):
        rows = X[idx == k]
        if len(rows) == 0:
            continue
        lo, hi = rows.copy(), rows.copy()
        lo[feature], hi[feature] = edges[k], edges[k + 1]
        deltas[k] = float((model.predict(hi) - model.predict(lo)).mean())
    acc = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(deltas)])
    return edges, acc - acc.mean()                          # centred

edges, ale_vals = ale(mc, sample_c, "credit_limit")
pd_at_edges = partial_dependence(mc, sample_c, "credit_limit", edges)
pd_centred = pd_at_edges - pd_at_edges.mean()
print(f"{'credit_limit':>13} {'PDP (centred)':>15} {'ALE':>9}")
for e, p, a in zip(edges, pd_centred, ale_vals):
    print(f"{e:>13.0f} {p:>15.3f} {a:>9.3f}")
```

```output
 credit_limit   PDP (centred)       ALE
          186          -2.066    -5.035
          264          -1.329    -3.489
          326          -0.710    -1.925
          391          -0.462    -1.093
          460          -0.152     0.204
          518           0.328     1.552
          569           0.817     2.187
          615           1.501     3.035
          694           2.074     4.564
```

Both columns are centred (they average to zero) so they can be compared. ALE runs from $-5.035$ to
$+4.564$; PDP from $-2.066$ to $+2.074$.

The two curves disagree, and ALE is the *steeper* one: it spans about 9.6 units of spend against
the PDP's 4.1. That is the opposite of what people expect, and it is the right result. Inside a
narrow band of `credit_limit`, every row has a similar tenure, so nudging the limit to the band's
edges moves the model a lot — the forest is using `credit_limit` as a stand-in for tenure. The PDP
dilutes exactly that signal: forcing a limit of 222 onto a five-year customer lands the row in a
region where the model's tenure splits dominate and pin the prediction back where it was.

Neither curve tells you that `credit_limit` has no causal effect on spend, which is the truth of
the generator. Both describe the *model*, and the model genuinely leans on the proxy. Use ALE when
features are correlated and you want an effect estimate that never fabricates a row; use PDP when
you specifically want the "what if we forced this value on everyone" answer, and say so.

## Global surrogates: approximate the model with something readable

If the model is too complex to read, train a simple model to imitate it and read that instead —
but first check how good the imitation is.

Fit an interpretable model to the black box's **predictions** (not to `y`), then read the simple
model — but only if it is faithful.

A **global surrogate** is that imitation, here a shallow decision tree. Its **fidelity** is how well
it reproduces the black box's predictions, measured as $R^2$ against the black box's outputs on
held-out rows. Its **accuracy** is how well it predicts the real target.

```python
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor, export_text

black_box_preds = model.predict(Xtr)
print(f"{'depth':>6} {'fidelity R2 vs model':>22} {'accuracy R2 vs truth':>22}")
for depth in (1, 2, 3, 5, 8):
    surrogate = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=depth, random_state=0).fit(Xtr, black_box_preds)
    fidelity = surrogate.score(Xte, model.predict(Xte))     # does it mimic the model?
    truth = surrogate.score(Xte, yte)                       # is it any good on its own?
    print(f"{depth:>6} {fidelity:>22.4f} {truth:>22.4f}")
```

```output
 depth   fidelity R2 vs model   accuracy R2 vs truth
     1                 0.2556                 0.1902
     2                 0.3940                 0.2938
     3                 0.4725                 0.3595
     5                 0.7752                 0.5882
     8                 0.9658                 0.7334
```

Fidelity climbs with depth: 0.2556 at depth 1, 0.4725 at depth 3, 0.7752 at depth 5, 0.9658 at
depth 8. The accuracy column is lower at every depth.

Fidelity is the number that licenses the explanation, and on this model it is brutal: a depth-3
tree reproduces only 47% of the forest's variance. That tree is not an explanation of the forest;
it is a different, worse model. You need depth 8 — 256 leaves, unreadable — before fidelity
reaches 0.97. Report fidelity whenever you show a surrogate, and never let the surrogate's *own*
accuracy against the truth (the right-hand column) stand in for it: those numbers are lower still
and answer a question nobody asked.

Here is what the depth-3 surrogate actually says:

```python
surrogate = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=3, random_state=0).fit(Xtr, black_box_preds)
print(f"fidelity R2 = {surrogate.score(Xte, model.predict(Xte)):.4f}\n")
print(export_text(surrogate, feature_names=list(Xtr.columns), decimals=1))
```

each leaf shows the average predicted spend for customers who reach it, from 51.8 to 76.4.

The rules are readable and directionally sensible — tenure dominates, support tickets subtract —
which is exactly what makes them dangerous at a fidelity of 0.47. Notice also what the tree does
with `discount`: it splits on it only inside the long-tenure branch, and there *higher* discount
maps to lower predicted spend. That fragment of the interaction is real, but a reader would take
the whole rule list as a description of the model, and less than half of the model is in it. A
surrogate is a summary you must earn with a fidelity number, not a translation.

> **Watch out:** a surrogate with low fidelity is not "a rough explanation". It is a different
> model. Quote its rules only next to its fidelity number.

## Recap in plain words

- Partial dependence forces one feature to each value for every row and averages the predictions.
- Averaging hides effects that cancel; ICE curves show one line per row and expose them.
- Splitting ICE curves by a second feature finds the moderator behind an interaction.
- PDP invents impossible rows when features are correlated; ALE only moves rows a little, locally.
- Both PDP and ALE describe the model, not cause and effect.
- A surrogate tree is only an explanation if its fidelity to the model is high.

```python
def inspect_feature(feature, n_grid=7, n_rows=200):
    lo, hi = df[feature].quantile(0.05), df[feature].quantile(0.95)
    g = np.linspace(lo, hi, n_grid)
    rows = Xte.sample(n_rows, random_state=1)
    c = ice(model, rows, feature, g)
    slopes = (c[:, -1] - c[:, 0]) / (g[-1] - g[0])

          f"mean slope {slopes.mean():+.4f} | "
          f"share positive {(slopes > 0).mean():.0%} | "
          f"slope spread (p90-p10) {np.percentile(slopes, 90) - np.percentile(slopes, 10):.4f}")

for f in df.columns:
    inspect_feature(f)
```

```output

discount         PDP swing   0.852 | mean slope +0.0002 | share positive 44% | slope spread (p90-p10) 0.7923
support_tickets  PDP swing   7.573 | mean slope -2.5243 | share positive 0% | slope spread (p90-p10) 1.2296
```

`inspect_feature` sweeps each feature from its 5th to 95th percentile and summarises the ICE

(`mc`, `Xc_te`) to see what a spurious feature looks like.

```python
    findings = []
    for f in features:
        g = np.linspace(X[f].quantile(0.05), X[f].quantile(0.95), n_grid)
        c = ice(model, rows, f, g)
        slopes = (c[:, -1] - c[:, 0]) / (g[-1] - g[0])
        share_pos = float((slopes > 0).mean())
        direction = "up" if slopes.mean() > 0 else "down"
        heterogeneous = 0.2 < share_pos < 0.8
        flags = []
        if direction != expected[f]:
            flags.append("DIRECTION DISAGREES WITH DOMAIN")
        if heterogeneous:
            flags.append("HETEROGENEOUS (PDP is misleading)")
        findings.append({
            "feature": f, "expected": expected[f], "observed": direction,
            "mean_slope": round(float(slopes.mean()), 4),
            "share_positive": f"{share_pos:.0%}",
            "flags": "; ".join(flags) or "ok",
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(findings)

report = behaviour_report(model, Xte, list(df.columns), EXPECTED)
print(report.to_string(index=False))

surrogate = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=3, random_state=0).fit(Xtr, model.predict(Xtr))
fid = surrogate.score(Xte, model.predict(Xte))
print(f"\nsurrogate (depth 3) fidelity R2 vs the model: {fid:.4f}")
print(f"model test R2 vs truth: {model.score(Xte, yte):.4f}")
print("verdict:", "surrogate is a fair summary" if fid > 0.9 else
      "surrogate is NOT faithful enough to quote as an explanation")

flagged = report[report["flags"] != "ok"]
print(f"\n{len(flagged)} feature(s) need a human look:")
for _, r in flagged.iterrows():
    print(f"  - {r['feature']}: {r['flags']}")
```

Only `discount` is flagged, and it trips both flags. Tenure and support tickets match their
expected directions with unanimous slopes. The surrogate's fidelity, 0.4725, is far below the 0.9
bar, so the report refuses to quote it.

The report catches the thing a PDP chart would have hidden. `discount` trips both flags at once: a
mean slope of -0.018 nominally disagrees with the expected direction, and 41% of rows still slope
upward, so the direction flag is *not* the real finding — the heterogeneity flag is. That ordering
matters when you read one of these: on a heterogeneous feature the mean direction is an artefact
of the mix, and the reviewer should be sent looking for the moderator (tenure), not asked to
explain a sign. `tenure` (100% positive) and `support_tickets` (0% positive) are unanimous, so a
one-line summary of each is defensible.

The surrogate line is the other half of the discipline, and here it fails: depth 3 reproduces
0.4725 of the forest, so the check prints its refusal rather than a rule list. That verdict is the
feature, not a bug in the exercise — a fidelity below roughly 0.9 means the readable rules
describe a model you are not shipping. The honest options at that point are to deepen the
surrogate (and lose readability), to fit surrogates per segment (which would work well here, since
the population splits cleanly at 24 months), or to drop the rule list and fall back to
per-prediction attribution (ch. 9).

Two extensions worth building once you need them. First, make `EXPECTED` part of the repository
and run `behaviour_report` in CI: a monotonicity violation that appears after a retrain is exactly
the kind of silent regression that reaches production otherwise. Second, when a feature is flagged
heterogeneous, automate the moderator search — fit a shallow tree with the ICE slope as the target
and the other features as inputs; its first split is the moderator, found without a chart.
