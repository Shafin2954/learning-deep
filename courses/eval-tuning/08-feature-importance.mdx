## Three questions people confuse

"Which inputs matter most?" sounds like one question, but it hides three. They have different
answers, and mixing them up leads to deleting a column you needed or keeping one you did not.

A **feature** is one input column. **Feature importance** is any score that ranks features by how
much they matter — but "matter" can mean three things:

"Which features matter?" is three different questions with three different answers:

1. **What did this model use?** — gain/impurity importance, a property of the fitted trees.
2. **What does this model need?** — permutation importance, measured on held-out data.
3. **What would I lose by not collecting this?** — drop-column importance, which requires refitting.

Only the third is about the *data*; the first two are about a particular fitted model.

> **Picture it:** a football team. Question 1 is "who touched the ball most?" Question 2 is "how
> badly does *this* team play if one player suddenly plays blindfolded?" Question 3 is "how well
> would we play if we had never signed that player and trained without them?" A player can top
> the first list and barely register on the third.

The test bed has four columns, built so you know the truth: two carry real signal (one
continuous, one only 0/1), and two are pure noise — one continuous, and one with thousands of
distinct values (**high cardinality**, like an ID number).

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 4_000
signal = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "useful_cont": signal + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n),          # real, continuous
    "useful_bin": (signal > 0).astype(int),                 # real, but only 2 levels
    "noise_cont": rng.normal(0, 1, n),                      # pure noise, continuous
    "noise_id": rng.integers(0, n, n),                      # pure noise, high cardinality
})
y = (signal + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n) > 0).astype(int)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(df, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, min_samples_leaf=5,
                            random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
print(f"test AUC {roc_auc_score(yte, rf.predict_proba(Xte)[:, 1]):.4f}")
print(f"columns: {list(df.columns)}")
```

```output
test AUC 0.8559
columns: ['useful_cont', 'useful_bin', 'noise_cont', 'noise_id']
```

The random forest scores AUC 0.8559 on held-out rows, so it learned something real.

Two of those four columns are noise by construction. Watch what each importance method says about
them.

## Gain (impurity) importance is biased by cardinality

Trees come with a free importance score: how much each feature helped the splits during training.
It is free, and it is biased toward features with many distinct values, even useless ones.

A tree's built-in importance sums the impurity decrease at every split on a feature, weighted by
the samples reaching it. A feature with many distinct values offers many candidate split points,
so it wins splits by chance alone — especially deep in the tree where few samples remain.

**Impurity** measures how mixed the labels in a tree node are (a node of all-yes or all-no is
pure). A split's **impurity decrease** is how much purer its two children are than the parent.
scikit-learn's `feature_importances_` adds these up per feature; it is also called **gain** or
**MDI** (mean decrease in impurity).

```python
gain = pd.Series(rf.feature_importances_, index=df.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)
for name, v in gain.items():
    print(f"{name:12} {v:.4f}  {'#' * int(round(v * 60))}")
```

```output
useful_cont  0.3838  #######################
useful_bin   0.3346  ####################
noise_id     0.1424  #########
noise_cont   0.1392  ########
```

The two real features lead (0.3838 and 0.3346), but `noise_id` (0.1424) and `noise_cont` (0.1392)
together take 0.28 of the total — for columns that cannot predict anything.

Two columns that contain nothing at all take 28% of the importance mass, and the high-cardinality
one edges ahead of the continuous one. The effect grows with tree depth, because deep nodes hold
few samples and a feature with thousands of candidate split points can always find one that looks
good on those samples:

```python
deep = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, min_samples_leaf=1,
                              random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
for name, v in pd.Series(deep.feature_importances_, index=df.columns).items():
    print(f"{name:12} {v:.4f}")
```

```output
useful_cont  0.3670
useful_bin   0.2160
noise_cont   0.2073
noise_id     0.2097
```

With leaves of a single row allowed, `noise_cont` (0.2073) and `noise_id` (0.2097) sit level with
the genuinely useful `useful_bin` (0.2160).

Fully grown, the two noise columns draw level with `useful_bin`, a genuinely predictive feature —
42% of the reported importance goes to columns with no relationship to the target. This is the
documented cardinality bias of mean-decrease-in-impurity, and it is why `feature_importances_`
should never be the last word. Gain importance is also computed on the **training** data, so it
cannot distinguish "this feature helped the model fit" from "this feature helped it generalize".

> **Watch out:** the bar chart of `feature_importances_` is the first thing most tutorials show.
> On real data with ID-like or timestamp columns, it can rank pure noise in the top half.

## Permutation importance asks the model to do without

Take one column in the test set and shuffle it, so its values land on random rows. If the
model's score drops a lot, the model was relying on that column. If the score barely moves, it
was not.

Shuffle one column in the *held-out* set, breaking its relationship with the target while keeping
its marginal distribution, and measure how far the score falls:

$$
I_j = s(\hat{f}, X, y) - \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R} s\!\left(\hat{f}, X^{(j,r)}_{\text{perm}}, y\right)
$$

where $X^{(j,r)}_{\text{perm}}$ is the data with column $j$ shuffled. No refitting — the same
fitted model is scored on corrupted inputs.

> **In plain words:** $I_j$, the importance of feature $j$, is the score $s$ on clean data minus the
> average score over $R$ shuffles of column $j$. The **marginal distribution** — the set of values
> in that column — stays exactly the same; only which row gets which value changes.

> **Picture it:** to see whether a recipe needs its salt, you do not cook a new dish without salt;
> you swap the salt for a random ingredient from the cupboard and taste. Same amount of "stuff",
> wrong information.

```python
def permutation_importance(model, X, y, metric=roc_auc_score, repeats=10, seed=0):
    base = metric(y, model.predict_proba(X)[:, 1])
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    out = {}
    for col in X.columns:
        drops = []
        for _ in range(repeats):
            Xp = X.copy()
            Xp[col] = r.permutation(Xp[col].values)
            drops.append(base - metric(y, model.predict_proba(Xp)[:, 1]))
        out[col] = (float(np.mean(drops)), float(np.std(drops)))
    return base, out

base, perm = permutation_importance(rf, Xte, yte)
print(f"baseline AUC {base:.4f}")
for col, (m, s) in sorted(perm.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1][0]):
    print(f"{col:12} drop {m:+.4f} +- {s:.4f}")
```

```output
baseline AUC 0.8559
useful_bin   drop +0.1541 +- 0.0092
useful_cont  drop +0.1041 +- 0.0097
noise_cont   drop +0.0036 +- 0.0023
noise_id     drop +0.0022 +- 0.0023
```

Shuffling `useful_bin` drops AUC by 0.1541; shuffling `useful_cont` drops it by 0.1041. The noise
columns cost 0.0036 and 0.0022 — about one standard deviation, so effectively zero.

Now the ordering is right: the two real features carry the model, and the two noise columns cost
nothing to destroy. Read the standard deviations too — an importance smaller than its own spread
is indistinguishable from zero.

### Train-set permutation importance detects memorisation

Run the same shuffle test on the *training* rows. A feature the model only used to memorise
particular rows will look important there and useless on the test set.

```python
_, perm_tr = permutation_importance(rf, Xtr, ytr)
print(f"{'feature':12} {'test':>10} {'train':>10}")
for col in df.columns:
    print(f"{col:12} {perm[col][0]:>+10.4f} {perm_tr[col][0]:>+10.4f}")
```

```output
feature            test      train
useful_cont     +0.1041    +0.1908
useful_bin      +0.1541    +0.1807
noise_cont      +0.0036    +0.0657
noise_id        +0.0022    +0.0691
```

Both noise columns jump on the training set: `noise_cont` from +0.0036 to +0.0657 and `noise_id`
from +0.0022 to +0.0691. The real features also rise on train, but they matter on test too.

`noise_id` is worth nothing on the test set but visibly non-zero on the training set: the forest
memorised individual rows through it. A feature that is important on train and worthless on test
is a leak or an overfitting handle — that gap is a diagnostic, not a nuisance.

## Correlated features split the credit

If two columns hold the same information, shuffling one of them hurts little, because the model
can still read the other. Each looks unimportant on its own, even though together they are vital.

Permutation importance answers "what happens if this column becomes noise *while the others stay
intact*". If two columns carry the same information, neither is needed.

The demo adds `useful_copy`, a near-duplicate of `useful_cont` with a tiny bit of noise, and
retrains.

```python
df2 = df.copy()
df2["useful_copy"] = df2["useful_cont"] + rng.normal(0, 0.05, n)   # near-duplicate
X2tr, X2te, y2tr, y2te = train_test_split(df2, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)
rf2 = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, min_samples_leaf=5,
                             random_state=0).fit(X2tr, y2tr)
_, perm2 = permutation_importance(rf2, X2te, y2te)
print(f"{'feature':12} {'alone':>9} {'with a twin':>13}")
for col in df.columns:
    twin = perm2[col][0]
    print(f"{col:12} {perm[col][0]:>+9.4f} {twin:>+13.4f}")
print(f"{'useful_copy':12} {'-':>9} {perm2['useful_copy'][0]:>+13.4f}")
```

```output
feature          alone   with a twin
useful_cont    +0.1041       +0.0266
useful_bin     +0.1541       +0.1126
noise_cont     +0.0036       +0.0026
noise_id       +0.0022       +0.0016
useful_copy          -       +0.0198
```

`useful_cont` falls from +0.1041 to +0.0266, and its twin collects only +0.0198.

Adding a near-duplicate cuts the measured importance of `useful_cont` from 0.1041 to 0.0266, and
the copy only picks up 0.0198 — the pair together is now worth less than half what the single
column was worth, because destroying either one leaves the model the other. Nothing about the
data changed; one column was added. **Never drop features by ranking permutation importance when
the candidates are correlated** — you can delete both halves of a pair that is jointly essential,
and each deletion looks harmless right up until you make the second one.

### Grouped permutation fixes it

Permute correlated columns *together* and the group's true importance reappears.

"Together" means one shared shuffle: both columns move to the same new rows, so they still agree
with each other but no longer with the label.

```python
def group_importance(model, X, y, groups, repeats=10, seed=0):
    base = roc_auc_score(y, model.predict_proba(X)[:, 1])
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    out = {}
    for name, cols in groups.items():
        drops = []
        for _ in range(repeats):
            Xp = X.copy()
            idx = r.permutation(len(Xp))
            for c in cols:                       # one shared shuffle keeps them aligned
                Xp[c] = Xp[c].values[idx]
            drops.append(base - roc_auc_score(y, model.predict_proba(Xp)[:, 1]))
        out[name] = float(np.mean(drops))
    return out

groups = {"signal_block": ["useful_cont", "useful_copy"],
          "binary": ["useful_bin"],
          "noise_block": ["noise_cont", "noise_id"]}
for name, v in sorted(group_importance(rf2, X2te, y2te, groups).items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
    print(f"{name:14} {v:+.4f}")
```

```output
signal_block   +0.1420
binary         +0.1126
noise_block    +0.0050
```

The twin pair as a block is worth +0.1420 — more than `useful_cont` alone was worth before the
twin existed (+0.1041). The noise block is worth +0.0050, still close to zero.

## Drop-column importance is the honest, expensive one

The most direct test is also the slowest: retrain the model without the column and see how much
worse it gets. This is the only method where the model gets a chance to adapt to the missing column.

Refit the model without the column and compare. This answers the business question — *should we
keep collecting this?* — and it is the only one that lets the model compensate by leaning on
whatever remains.

```python
def drop_column_importance(X, y, Xv, yv, seed=0):
    def fit_score(cols):
        m = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, min_samples_leaf=5,
                                   random_state=seed).fit(X[cols], y)
        return roc_auc_score(yv, m.predict_proba(Xv[cols])[:, 1])
    full = fit_score(list(X.columns))
    return full, {c: full - fit_score([k for k in X.columns if k != c]) for c in X.columns}

full, drops = drop_column_importance(X2tr, y2tr, X2te, y2te)
print(f"all columns AUC {full:.4f}")
for col, v in sorted(drops.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
    print(f"without {col:12} costs {v:+.4f}")
```

```output
all columns AUC 0.8566
without useful_bin   costs +0.0348
without noise_cont   costs +0.0068
without noise_id     costs +0.0042
without useful_copy  costs +0.0006
without useful_cont  costs -0.0014
```

Only one column is genuinely load-bearing: without `useful_bin` the model loses 3.5 points.
Compare with the permutation table, where `useful_cont` and `useful_copy` measured +0.0266 and
+0.0198 — under refitting they cost +0.0006 and **-0.0014**, because whichever one you remove, the
refitted model leans on its twin. Read the small numbers as noise, in both directions: dropping
`noise_cont` appears to cost 0.0068 and dropping `useful_cont` appears to *help* by 0.0014, and
neither is real. Both are the sampling noise of one train/test split (ch. 5), which is why a
drop-column table on a small validation set needs repeated splits before you act on it.

The cost is $k+1$ refits. Use it on a shortlist, not on a thousand columns.

> **Watch out:** drop-column importance says "you can drop `useful_cont`" and also "you can drop
> `useful_copy`". Both are true one at a time. Dropping both at once removes the signal — always
> re-test after each removal.

## Which to use when

Each method answers its own question; pick by the question, and know each one's trap.

| Question | Method | Cost | Main trap |
|----------|--------|------|-----------|
| What did the trees split on? | gain / impurity | free | cardinality bias, train-only |
| What does this fitted model rely on? | permutation on test | $k \times R$ scorings | splits credit across correlated columns |
| Do we still need to collect this? | drop-column | $k+1$ refits | slow; single-split noise |
| Which *block* of features matters? | grouped permutation | cheap | you must define the groups |

Here $k$ is the number of features and $R$ the number of shuffles per feature.

## Recap in plain words

- Gain importance is free but rewards high-cardinality noise and only reflects training data.
- Permutation importance shuffles one column on held-out data and measures the score drop.
- A feature important on train but not on test was used for memorising.
- Correlated features share credit, so each looks unimportant; permute them as a group.
- Drop-column importance retrains without the feature; it answers "do we need to collect this?"
- Small importances are often noise — compare them with their spread.

## Exercise

Ranking by importance leaves you guessing where to cut. Build a **null-calibrated** selector: add
a column of pure noise (a "shadow" feature, as Boruta does), or shuffle the target to build the
null distribution of importance, then keep only features whose importance exceeds the null by a
margin. Compare its selection against naive top-k on data where some features are correlated.

> **Hint:** a **shadow feature** is a real column with its rows shuffled, so it has realistic values
> but no link to the label. The **null distribution** is the importance that meaningless columns
> get by luck; the best shadow's importance is a natural cutoff.

```python
def add_shadows(X, n_shadow=10, seed=0):
    """Shadow features: real columns with their rows shuffled. Meaningless by construction."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    Xs = X.copy()
    for i in range(n_shadow):
        src = X.columns[i % len(X.columns)]
        Xs[f"shadow_{i}"] = r.permutation(X[src].values)
    return Xs

Xs_tr = add_shadows(X2tr, seed=0)
Xs_te = add_shadows(X2te, seed=1)
model_s = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, min_samples_leaf=5,
                                 random_state=0).fit(Xs_tr, y2tr)

_, imp = permutation_importance(model_s, Xs_te, y2te, repeats=10)
shadow_scores = [imp[c][0] for c in Xs_tr.columns if c.startswith("shadow_")]
cutoff = max(shadow_scores)                                 # the best a fake feature managed
print(f"shadow importances: {[f'{v:+.4f}' for v in shadow_scores]}")
print(f"cutoff (max shadow) = {cutoff:+.4f}\n")

real = {c: imp[c][0] for c in X2tr.columns}
for c, v in sorted(real.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
    verdict = "KEEP" if v > cutoff else "drop"
    print(f"{c:12} {v:+.4f}   {verdict}")

top_k = [c for c, _ in sorted(real.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:2]]
kept = [c for c, v in real.items() if v > cutoff]
print(f"\nnaive top-2 : {top_k}")
print(f"null-calibrated: {sorted(kept)}")

def auc_with(cols):
    m = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, min_samples_leaf=5,
                               random_state=0).fit(X2tr[cols], y2tr)
    return roc_auc_score(y2te, m.predict_proba(X2te[cols])[:, 1])

print(f"\nAUC all 5 columns    : {auc_with(list(X2tr.columns)):.4f}")
print(f"AUC naive top-2      : {auc_with(top_k):.4f}")
print(f"AUC null-calibrated  : {auc_with(sorted(kept)):.4f}")
```

```output
shadow importances: ['+0.0021', '+0.0002', '+0.0016', '+0.0017', '+0.0004', '+0.0013', '-0.0004', '-0.0007', '-0.0004', '+0.0004']
cutoff (max shadow) = +0.0021

useful_bin   +0.0912   KEEP
useful_copy  +0.0271   KEEP
useful_cont  +0.0200   KEEP
noise_cont   +0.0021   drop
noise_id     +0.0010   drop

naive top-2 : ['useful_bin', 'useful_copy']
null-calibrated: ['useful_bin', 'useful_cont', 'useful_copy']

AUC all 5 columns    : 0.8566
AUC naive top-2      : 0.8377
AUC null-calibrated  : 0.8476
```

Ten shadows score between $-0.0007$ and $+0.0021$, so the cutoff is +0.0021. The three real
columns (+0.0912, +0.0271, +0.0200) clear it easily; `noise_cont` ties it and `noise_id` falls
below. Naive top-2 keeps `useful_bin` and `useful_copy` and throws away `useful_cont`.

The shadow features give you a *scale*: any real feature that cannot beat a deliberately
meaningless column is not evidence of anything. That is the missing piece in every "top-10
features" chart — a ranking with no zero point, where the tenth feature might be doing nothing at
all. Here the ten shadows top out at +0.0021, both noise columns land at or below that line, and
all three real columns clear it. Note how close the call is on `noise_cont`: it ties the cutoff
exactly. With fewer shadows the cutoff would sit lower and the noise column would survive — the
cutoff is an estimate of the null's upper tail, so give it enough shadows (and enough permutation
repeats) to be stable.

Now read the AUCs, which do not flatter the method: keeping all five columns scores 0.8566, the
null-calibrated three score 0.8476, and naive top-2 scores 0.8377. **Dropping the noise cost a
point of AUC.** That is the correct result and worth sitting with — with five columns and a
random forest, useless features are nearly free, since the forest stops splitting on them,
and removing them mostly removes a little beneficial split-level randomness. Feature selection
earns its keep when there are hundreds of columns (variance, training time, serving latency,
data-collection cost), not on a five-column table. The selector's job here is to tell you which
columns are *evidence*, not to promise a better score.

Two caveats this exercise cannot escape. Correlated features still split their credit, so a
jointly-essential pair can both fall below the cutoff; run the selector on *groups* when you know
the blocks. And selection is itself a fitting step — it looked at the labels — so the AUC you
print after selecting is optimistic unless the whole selector runs inside the cross-validation
folds (ch. 5, pitfall 1). Wrap it in a function, call it per fold, and compare the *procedure*,
not this single number.
