## Why [CLS] Is a Poor Sentence Embedding

BERT's `[CLS]` token was trained with Next Sentence Prediction, not similarity. Two semantically
opposite sentences often produce similar `[CLS]` vectors because BERT never learned to separate
them in embedding space. When you measure cosine similarity of raw BERT `[CLS]` vectors, you
get near-random rankings — often worse than averaging GloVe vectors. You will see this below with a
small encoder we build ourselves: before contrastive training, every sentence pair looks almost identical.

The fix is contrastive training: show the model pairs of similar and dissimilar sentences and
push their embeddings apart.

## Sentence-BERT (SBERT)

SBERT (Reimers & Gurevych 2019) fine-tunes BERT on Natural Language Inference and STS (Semantic
Textual Similarity) datasets with a siamese network. Two sentences pass through the same BERT
encoder, their token embeddings are mean-pooled to produce fixed-size vectors $u$ and $v$, and
the loss penalizes wrong similarity judgements.

### Mean Pooling

Average all token embeddings (excluding padding) rather than taking `[CLS]`:

$$\bar{h} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} h_t \cdot m_t$$

where $m_t \in \{0,1\}$ is the attention mask.

Pretrained SBERT checkpoints cannot be downloaded in this course environment. Instead we train a **tiny sentence encoder with a contrastive objective** on synthetic data: sentences are bags of words drawn from a topic's vocabulary (finance, pets, machine learning, ...) and two sentences about the same topic are treated as a positive pair, much like NLI entailment pairs in SBERT. The size and data are toy, but the mechanism (siamese encoder, mean pooling, in-batch contrastive loss) is the real one.

```python
import re, random, numpy as np, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.set_num_threads(1)
torch.manual_seed(0)
rng = random.Random(0)

TOPICS = {
 "ml":   "machine learning deep neural networks layered gradient descent optimizers loss function model weights training data learn pattern recognition iteratively minimizes update".split(),
 "geo":  "paris france capital eiffel tower city europe river country tourists visit landmark monument french".split(),
 "bio":  "photosynthesis plants sunlight oxygen energy converts absorb release leaves green carbon cells grow".split(),
 "lit":  "shakespeare wrote hamlet macbeth renaissance reshaped european art poet plays novel painting writer theatre".split(),
 "fin":  "stock market crashed investors huge losses equities financial markets fell sharply prices trading bank money".split(),
 "pets": "cat dog kitten puppy slept napped playing outside keyboard laptop warm runs park fur".split(),
 "acct": "reset password login forgot credentials account locked attempts access sign cannot log into my i".split(),
 "bill": "cancel subscription billing payment information update invoice methods accept refund card pay".split(),
 "tech": "app crashes startup export data error bug install screen freezes update files".split(),
}
FILL = "the a in is on and of to how do".split()
names = list(TOPICS)

def make_sentence(topic, n=4):
    ws = rng.sample(TOPICS[topic], n) + rng.sample(FILL, 2)
    rng.shuffle(ws)
    return " ".join(ws)

tokenize = lambda t: re.findall(r"\w+", t.lower())
docs = ["Machine learning automates pattern recognition.", "Deep learning uses layered neural networks.",
        "Paris is the capital of France.", "The Eiffel Tower is in Paris.",
        "Photosynthesis converts sunlight to energy.", "Plants absorb carbon and release oxygen.",
        "Gradient descent minimizes the loss function.", "Optimizers update model weights iteratively.",
        "Shakespeare wrote Hamlet and Macbeth.", "The Renaissance reshaped European art."]
extra = ["how do neural networks learn?", "I cannot log into my account.", "The app crashes on startup.",
         "A dog runs in the park.", "A puppy is playing outside.",
         "The stock market crashed yesterday.", "Investors saw huge losses in equities.",
         "A cat slept on the warm keyboard.", "Financial markets fell sharply.", "The kitten napped on the laptop."]
vocab = ["[PAD]", "[CLS]", "[UNK]"] + sorted({w for ws in TOPICS.values() for w in ws} | set(FILL)
                                            | {w for t in docs + extra for w in tokenize(t)})
stoi = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}

def encode(texts):
    rows = [[1] + [stoi.get(w, 2) for w in tokenize(t)] for t in texts]
    L = max(map(len, rows))
    ids = torch.tensor([r + [0] * (L - len(r)) for r in rows])
    return ids, (ids != 0).long()

class SentEncoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, V, d=48):
        super().__init__()
        self.tok, self.pos = nn.Embedding(V, d), nn.Embedding(32, d)
        layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(d, 4, 4 * d, dropout=0.0, batch_first=True, norm_first=True)
        self.enc = nn.TransformerEncoder(layer, 2, enable_nested_tensor=False)
    def embed(self, texts, pool="mean"):
        ids, mask = encode(texts)
        h = self.enc(self.tok(ids) + self.pos(torch.arange(ids.shape[1])), src_key_padding_mask=(mask == 0))
        if pool == "cls":
            e = h[:, 0]
        else:                                           # mean pooling over real tokens only
            m = mask.unsqueeze(-1).float()
            e = (h * m).sum(1) / m.sum(1)
        return F.normalize(e, dim=-1)

model = SentEncoder(len(vocab))
test = ["The stock market crashed yesterday.", "Investors saw huge losses in equities.",
        "A cat slept on the warm keyboard.", "Financial markets fell sharply.", "The kitten napped on the laptop."]

def sim_matrix(m, pool):
    with torch.no_grad():
        E = m.embed(test, pool)
    return (E @ E.T).numpy().round(2)

print("untrained, [CLS] pooling: off-diagonal range",
      sim_matrix(model, "cls")[np.triu_indices(5, 1)].min(), "to", sim_matrix(model, "cls")[np.triu_indices(5, 1)].max())
print("untrained, mean pooling:  off-diagonal range",
      sim_matrix(model, "mean")[np.triu_indices(5, 1)].min(), "to", sim_matrix(model, "mean")[np.triu_indices(5, 1)].max())
```

```output
untrained, [CLS] pooling: off-diagonal range 0.95 to 0.98
untrained, mean pooling:  off-diagonal range 0.33 to 0.74
```

Before training, `[CLS]` vectors of unrelated sentences are nearly identical (cosine 0.95-0.98): nothing forces them to carry sentence meaning. Mean pooling already spreads them out a little because it averages token content.

### Contrastive training

Each batch holds 48 sentences with random topics. The loss treats every other sentence of the same topic as a positive and everything else as a negative (a supervised version of the in-batch InfoNCE loss used by SBERT-style training):

$$
\mathcal{L}_i = -\frac{1}{|P(i)|}\sum_{p \in P(i)} \log \frac{\exp(u_i \cdot u_p / \tau)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(u_i \cdot u_k / \tau)}
$$

```python
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
eye = torch.eye(48, dtype=torch.bool)
for step in range(300):
    labs = [rng.randrange(len(names)) for _ in range(48)]
    E = model.embed([make_sentence(names[l]) for l in labs])
    S = (E @ E.T / 0.1).masked_fill(eye, -1e9)               # temperature tau = 0.1
    y = torch.tensor(labs)
    pos = (y[:, None] == y[None, :]) & ~eye
    loss = (-(S.log_softmax(-1) * pos).sum(1) / pos.sum(1).clamp(min=1)).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if (step + 1) % 100 == 0:
        print(f"step {step+1}: loss {loss.item():.3f}")
model.eval()

print("\nsimilarity matrix after training (mean pooling):")
print(sim_matrix(model, "mean"))
```

```output
step 100: loss 1.588
step 200: loss 1.560
step 300: loss 1.591

similarity matrix after training (mean pooling):
[[1.   0.91 0.08 0.92 0.03]
 [0.91 1.   0.22 0.94 0.23]
 [0.08 0.22 1.   0.02 0.89]
 [0.92 0.94 0.02 1.   0.07]
 [0.03 0.23 0.89 0.07 1.  ]]
```

Sentences 0, 1, 3 (finance) now have cosine 0.91-0.94 with each other and only 0.02-0.23 with sentences 2 and 4 (pets), which are close to each other (0.89). The tiny model has never seen those exact sentences.

## Embedding and normalising a batch

`SentenceTransformer.encode(...)` in the `sentence-transformers` library wraps tokenize + encode + pool + normalize. Our `embed` does the same, so `embed(texts)` plays the role of `model.encode(texts, normalize_embeddings=True)`:

```python
embeddings = model.embed(docs).detach().numpy()
print(f"Shape: {embeddings.shape}")

```

## Semantic Search

Given a query, rank documents by cosine similarity. With normalized vectors, cosine similarity
is just the dot product:

```python
query = "how do neural networks learn?"
q_emb = model.embed([query]).detach().numpy()

scores = (q_emb @ embeddings.T)[0]
ranked = np.argsort(scores)[::-1]

print("Top 3 matches:")
for i, idx in enumerate(ranked[:3]):
    print(f"  {i+1}. [{scores[idx]:.4f}] {docs[idx]}")
```

```output
Top 3 matches:
  1. [0.9664] Optimizers update model weights iteratively.
  2. [0.9455] Deep learning uses layered neural networks.
  3. [0.9371] Gradient descent minimizes the loss function.
```

The top three are all machine-learning documents; the exact order among them is not meaningful with a model this small.

## Approximate Nearest Neighbours (FAISS-style)

Brute-force cosine search is $O(N \cdot d)$ per query — fine for thousands of docs, slow for
millions. FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) builds an index for sub-linear lookup. FAISS is not installed here, so we implement the two ideas it offers in NumPy: an exact flat inner-product index, and an IVF index that clusters vectors with k-means and only scans the `nprobe` closest clusters.

```python
class FlatIP:
    def __init__(self, d): self.X = np.zeros((0, d), dtype=np.float32)
    def add(self, X): self.X = np.vstack([self.X, X])
    def search(self, q, k):
        s = q @ self.X.T
        idx = np.argsort(-s, axis=1)[:, :k]
        return np.take_along_axis(s, idx, 1), idx

d = embeddings.shape[1]
index = FlatIP(d)
index.add(embeddings.astype(np.float32))
scores_f, indices = index.search(q_emb.astype(np.float32), k=3)
print("Flat top-3:")
for score, idx in zip(scores_f[0], indices[0]):
    print(f"  [{score:.4f}] {docs[idx]}")
```

```output
Flat top-3:
  [0.9664] Optimizers update model weights iteratively.
  [0.9455] Deep learning uses layered neural networks.
  [0.9371] Gradient descent minimizes the loss function.
```

For larger corpora use an inverted file (IVF): cluster first, search within the closest clusters.

```python
class IVFFlat:
    def __init__(self, nlist, seed=0):
        self.nlist, self.seed = nlist, seed
    def train_add(self, X):
        r = np.random.default_rng(self.seed)
        self.C = X[r.choice(len(X), self.nlist, replace=False)].copy()
        for _ in range(10):                                      # k-means on inner product
            a = np.argmax(X @ self.C.T, axis=1)
            for c in range(self.nlist):
                if (a == c).any():
                    self.C[c] = X[a == c].mean(0)
        self.X, self.assign = X, np.argmax(X @ self.C.T, axis=1)
    def search(self, q, k, nprobe=2):
        cells = np.argsort(-(q @ self.C.T)[0])[:nprobe]          # nprobe closest cells
        cand = np.where(np.isin(self.assign, cells))[0]
        s = (q @ self.X[cand].T)[0]
        order = np.argsort(-s)[:k]
        return cand[order], len(cand)

ivf = IVFFlat(nlist=4)
ivf.train_add(embeddings.astype(np.float32))
for nprobe in (1, 2, 4):
    idxs, scanned = ivf.search(q_emb.astype(np.float32), k=3, nprobe=nprobe)
    print(f"nprobe={nprobe}: scanned {scanned}/{len(docs)} vectors, results {idxs.tolist()}")
print("exact results:   ", indices[0].tolist())
```

```output
nprobe=1: scanned 4/10 vectors, results [7, 1, 6]
nprobe=2: scanned 6/10 vectors, results [7, 1, 6]
nprobe=4: scanned 10/10 vectors, results [7, 1, 6]
exact results:    [7, 1, 6]
```

`nprobe` is the accuracy-versus-speed knob: more cells scanned means more vectors compared (cost) and a better chance of matching the exact answer. At `nprobe=nlist` it is exact. On this 10-vector toy even `nprobe=1` already returns the exact top 3, because the query's nearest neighbours happen to fall in one cluster; on millions of vectors the trade-off is real.

```python
faqs = [
    "How do I reset my password?",
    "I forgot my login credentials.",
    "How can I cancel my subscription?",
    "Where do I update my billing information?",
    "The app crashes on startup.",
    "My account is locked after too many attempts.",
    "How do I export my data?",
    "Which payment methods do you accept?",
]

faq_embs = model.embed(faqs).detach().numpy()

query = "I cannot log into my account."
q_emb = model.embed([query]).detach().numpy()

scores = (q_emb @ faq_embs.T)[0]
top2 = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:2]
for idx in top2:
    print(f"[{scores[idx]:.3f}] {faqs[idx]}")
```

```output
[0.987] How do I reset my password?
[0.985] My account is locked after too many attempts.
```

The contrastive training pulled account/login vocabulary together, so the login-related FAQs rank highest. This is exactly why embedding retrieval handles "can't log in" vs "forgot credentials" better than exact keyword match, with the caveat that our toy model only knows the words in its training topics.
