## Three ways to estimate many related means

You run 30 stores. One opened two days ago and sold 40 units a day; another has 300 days of history at
50. Is the new store weak? **No pooling** takes each store's average at face value. **Complete pooling**
uses the chain-wide average for everyone. **Partial pooling** pulls each store toward the chain average,
*harder the less data it has*. A hierarchical model decides how hard to pull.

Group $j$ has a true mean $\theta_j$ (its long-run average) and an observed mean $\bar y_j$ with
standard error $\sigma_j$ (within-store noise over $\sqrt{n_j}$). The true means come from a population
with mean $\mu$ and spread $\tau$:

$$
\bar y_j \mid \theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_j, \sigma_j^2), \qquad \theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2).
$$

### Deriving the shrinkage formula

This shows that partial pooling is a precision-weighted average of data and population mean. Multiply
the two Gaussians in $\theta_j$ and complete the square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\tfrac{(\bar y_j - \theta_j)^2}{\sigma_j^2} + \tfrac{(\theta_j - \mu)^2}{\tau^2}\Big]
&= -\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sigma_j^2} + \tfrac{1}{\tau^2}\Big)\big(\theta_j - \hat\theta_j\big)^2 + \text{const},\\
\hat\theta_j = \frac{\bar y_j/\sigma_j^2 + \mu/\tau^2}{1/\sigma_j^2 + 1/\tau^2}
&= \underbrace{B_j}_{\text{shrinkage}}\,\mu + (1 - B_j)\,\bar y_j,
\qquad B_j = \frac{\sigma_j^2}{\sigma_j^2 + \tau^2},
\qquad V_j = \frac{1}{1/\sigma_j^2 + 1/\tau^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each source is weighted by its precision (one over its variance). $B_j$ is the fraction of the way you
move toward $\mu$: near 1 for a noisy group ($\sigma_j \gg \tau$), near 0 for a precise one. No pooling
is $\tau \to \infty$, complete pooling is $\tau = 0$. Below, $\mu$ and $\tau$ are known (an oracle), so
you see the formula alone.

```python
import logging, warnings
import numpy as np
import pymc as pm
import arviz as az
from scipy import stats, optimize
logging.getLogger("pymc").setLevel(logging.ERROR)
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
J = 30
mu_true, tau_true, sd_within = 50.0, 5.0, 20.0
n_j = rng.choice([2, 4, 10, 30, 100, 300], J)            # days of history per store
theta = rng.normal(mu_true, tau_true, J)                  # true long-run daily sales
sig_j = sd_within / np.sqrt(n_j)                          # standard error of each store's mean
ybar = rng.normal(theta, sig_j)

no_pool = ybar
complete = np.full(J, np.average(ybar, weights=1 / sig_j ** 2))
B = sig_j ** 2 / (sig_j ** 2 + tau_true ** 2)
partial = B * mu_true + (1 - B) * ybar

print(f"{'days':>5} {'truth':>7} {'raw mean':>9} {'B_j':>5} {'partial':>8}")
for j in np.argsort(n_j)[::5]:
    print(f"{n_j[j]:>5} {theta[j]:>7.1f} {ybar[j]:>9.1f} {B[j]:>5.2f} {partial[j]:>8.1f}")
rmse = lambda est, tru=theta: np.sqrt(np.mean((est - tru) ** 2))
print(f"\nRMSE  no pooling {rmse(no_pool):.2f} | complete pooling {rmse(complete):.2f} | "
      f"partial pooling {rmse(partial):.2f}")
```

## Stein's paradox: shrinkage wins even when it should not

Take $k$ unrelated quantities (a batting average, the price of tea, rainfall in Dhaka), each measured
once with unit noise, $y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_i, 1)$. The obvious estimate is $\hat\theta_i = y_i$.
Stein (1956) showed that this is *inadmissible* for $k \ge 3$: some estimator has lower total squared
error for **every** $\theta$, not just on average over a prior. James and Stein (1961) wrote one down.
In its positive-part form ($(\cdot)_+$ clips at zero), shrinking toward the grand mean $\bar y$:

$$
\hat\theta^{\mathrm{JS}}_i = \bar y + \Big(1 - \frac{k - 3}{S}\Big)_{+}(y_i - \bar y),
\qquad S = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (y_i - \bar y)^2 ,
$$

### James–Stein is empirical Bayes

This shows that $(k-3)/S$ estimates the shrinkage $B$. If $\theta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2)$, then
$y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1 + \tau^2)$ and $S/(1+\tau^2) \sim \chi^2_{k-1}$. With
$\mathbb{E}[1/\chi^2_{\nu}] = 1/(\nu - 2)$:

$$
\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{k-3}{S}\Big] = \frac{k-3}{(1+\tau^2)(k-3)} = \frac{1}{1 + \tau^2} = B .
$$

James–Stein plugs an unbiased estimate of $B$ into the oracle formula: the other groups tell you how
much to trust each one. That is empirical Bayes, as in the Gamma-Poisson exercise (ch. 2). Below are
four fixed truths, some nothing like a normal population.

```python
def james_stein(y):
    k, yb = len(y), y.mean()
    S = np.sum((y - yb) ** 2)
    return yb + max(0.0, 1 - (k - 3) / S) * (y - yb)

k, R = 20, 5000
truths = {"normal, tau=1": rng.normal(0, 1, k),
          "normal, tau=4": rng.normal(0, 4, k),
          "uniform [-3, 3]": np.linspace(-3, 3, k),
          "19 zeros + one 10": np.r_[np.zeros(k - 1), 10.0]}
print(f"{'fixed truth (k=20)':>20} {'no pool':>8} {'complete':>9} {'James-Stein':>12}")
for name, th in truths.items():
    err = np.zeros(3)
    for _ in range(R):
        y = th + rng.standard_normal(k)
        err += [np.sum((y - th) ** 2), np.sum((y.mean() - th) ** 2), np.sum((james_stein(y) - th) ** 2)]
    print(f"{name:>20} {err[0] / R:>8.2f} {err[1] / R:>9.2f} {err[2] / R:>12.2f}")
```

```output
  fixed truth (k=20)  no pool  complete  James-Stein
       normal, tau=1    20.00     20.52        11.80
       normal, tau=4    20.10    260.80        19.04
     uniform [-3, 3]    20.04     67.32        16.41
   19 zeros + one 10    20.08     95.98        17.42
```

Each row is the total squared error averaged over 5000 noisy replications. No pooling always costs $k =
20$. Complete pooling nearly ties when the truths are close (20.52) and is catastrophic when they spread
out (260.80). James–Stein is below 20 in **every** row, including the lone outlier at 10. That is
Stein's paradox. The gain is large when the means are similar (11.80, a 41% cut) and fades as they
spread (19.04), because a large $S$ means little shrinkage. The guarantee is about *total* error: one
extreme unit can still be shrunk badly.

With unequal $\sigma_j$ the single factor no longer applies, but the logic does: estimate $(\mu, \tau)$
by maximising the marginal likelihood $\bar y_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_j^2 + \tau^2)$ and plug
them into $B_j$. Here is the store design again, on 500 fresh datasets:

```python
def eb_partial(yb, s):
    nll = lambda p: -stats.norm.logpdf(yb, p[0], np.sqrt(s ** 2 + np.exp(2 * p[1]))).sum()
    m, lt = optimize.minimize(nll, [yb.mean(), np.log(yb.std() + 1e-3)], method="Nelder-Mead").x
    Bj = s ** 2 / (s ** 2 + np.exp(2 * lt))
    return Bj * m + (1 - Bj) * yb, np.exp(lt)

res, tau_hats = [], []
for rep in range(500):
    r_ = np.random.default_rng(1000 + rep)
    sj = sd_within / np.sqrt(r_.choice([2, 4, 10, 30, 100, 300], J))
    th = r_.normal(mu_true, tau_true, J)
    yb = r_.normal(th, sj)
    pp, th_hat = eb_partial(yb, sj)
    tau_hats.append(th_hat)
    res.append([rmse(yb, th), rmse(np.full(J, np.average(yb, weights=1 / sj ** 2)), th), rmse(pp, th)])
res = np.array(res)
print(f"mean RMSE over 500 datasets: no pool {res[:, 0].mean():.2f} | complete {res[:, 1].mean():.2f} "
      f"| EB partial {res[:, 2].mean():.2f}")
print(f"partial pooling beats no pooling in {np.mean(res[:, 2] < res[:, 0]):.1%} of datasets, "
      f"complete pooling in {np.mean(res[:, 2] < res[:, 1]):.1%}")
print(f"estimated tau: median {np.median(tau_hats):.2f} | share estimated as < 0.5: "
      f"{np.mean(np.array(tau_hats) < 0.5):.1%}  (truth 5.0)")
```

```output
mean RMSE over 500 datasets: no pool 7.57 | complete 5.07 | EB partial 3.56
partial pooling beats no pooling in 99.6% of datasets, complete pooling in 99.8%
estimated tau: median 4.68 | share estimated as < 0.5: 0.2%  (truth 5.0)
```

Empirical-Bayes partial pooling averages RMSE 3.56 against 5.07 and 7.57, and wins in 99.6% of datasets.
With 30 groups, $\tau$ is estimated well (median 4.68, truth 5.0) and collapses to near zero only 0.2%
of the time. With 8 groups that collapse is the normal case.

## Eight Schools: the exact posterior of the group-level spread

Empirical Bayes picks one $\tau$ and pretends it is known. With a handful of groups, $\tau$ is very
uncertain, and that uncertainty should reach every group's estimate. The standard test case is **Eight
Schools** (Rubin, 1981): an SAT coaching programme in eight high schools, each reporting an estimated
effect $y_j$ (SAT points) with standard error $\sigma_j$. Some effects look large (28), but all are
noisy (standard errors 9 to 18). Everything is Gaussian, so you can integrate out $\theta_j$ and $\mu$
in closed form and put $\tau$ on a 1-D grid (BDA3 §5.4). No MCMC needed, and this exact answer is what
the samplers will be held to.

### The marginal posterior of tau

This shows that $p(\tau \mid y)$ is a product of 1-D Gaussian terms. Integrating out $\theta_j$ gives
$y_j \mid \mu, \tau \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, v_j)$, $v_j = \sigma_j^2 + \tau^2$. Treat the prior $\mu \sim
\mathcal{N}(m_0, s_0^2)$ as a pseudo-school $j = 0$ ($y_0 = m_0$, $v_0 = s_0^2$); the $\mu$ integral is
then Gaussian around a precision-weighted mean:

$$
\hat\mu = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^{J} y_j / v_j}{\sum_{j=0}^{J} 1/v_j}, \qquad V_\mu = \Big(\sum_{j=0}^{J} 1/v_j\Big)^{-1},
$$

$$
p(\tau \mid y) \propto p(\tau)\, V_\mu^{1/2} \prod_{j=0}^{J} v_j^{-1/2}
\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(y_j - \hat\mu)^2}{2 v_j}\Big).
$$

Then $\mu \mid \tau, y \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat\mu, V_\mu)$ and $\theta_j \mid \mu, \tau, y$ is the
shrinkage formula, so drawing $\tau$, then $\mu$, then $\theta$ gives exact independent draws.

```python
y8 = np.array([28., 8, -3, 7, -1, 1, 18, 12])               # Rubin (1981): effects and standard errors
s8 = np.array([15., 10, 16, 11, 9, 11, 10, 18])
m0, s0 = 0.0, 5.0                                          # mu ~ N(0, 5), tau ~ HalfCauchy(5)

def log_marg_tau(tau, y, s, m0=m0, s0=s0):
    yy = np.r_[m0, y]
    v = np.vstack([np.full_like(tau, s0 ** 2), (s ** 2)[:, None] + tau ** 2])   # (J+1, grid)
    prec = 1 / v
    V_mu = 1 / prec.sum(0)
    mu_hat = (yy[:, None] * prec).sum(0) * V_mu
    return 0.5 * np.log(V_mu) - 0.5 * np.log(v).sum(0) - 0.5 * ((yy[:, None] - mu_hat) ** 2 * prec).sum(0), mu_hat, V_mu

def grid_posterior(log_prior, y=y8, s=s8, tau_grid=np.linspace(1e-4, 60, 60_000)):
    lm, mu_hat, V_mu = log_marg_tau(tau_grid, y, s)
    lp = lm + log_prior(tau_grid)
    p = np.exp(lp - lp.max())
    return tau_grid, p / p.sum(), mu_hat, V_mu

def exact_draws(tg, p, mu_hat, V_mu, y, s, n=100_000, seed=0):
    r_ = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    i = r_.choice(len(tg), n, p=p)
    tau_d, mu_d = tg[i], r_.normal(mu_hat[i], np.sqrt(V_mu[i]))
    prec = 1 / s ** 2 + 1 / tau_d[:, None] ** 2
    th_d = r_.normal((y / s ** 2 + mu_d[:, None] / tau_d[:, None] ** 2) / prec, 1 / np.sqrt(prec))
    return tau_d, mu_d, th_d

tg, p_tau, mu_hat, V_mu = grid_posterior(lambda t: stats.halfcauchy.logpdf(t, scale=5))
tau_x, mu_x, th_x = exact_draws(tg, p_tau, mu_hat, V_mu, y8, s8)
cdf = np.cumsum(p_tau)
print(f"exact p(tau | y): mean {np.sum(tg * p_tau):.2f} | median {tg[np.searchsorted(cdf, .5)]:.2f} | "
      f"95% quantile {tg[np.searchsorted(cdf, .95)]:.2f} | P(tau < 1) {cdf[np.searchsorted(tg, 1)]:.3f}")
print(f"{'school':>7} {'y_j':>5} {'sigma_j':>8} {'E[theta_j|y]':>13} {'sd':>5}")
for j in range(8):
    print(f"{'ABCDEFGH'[j]:>7} {y8[j]:>5.0f} {s8[j]:>8.0f} {th_x[:, j].mean():>13.2f} {th_x[:, j].std():>5.2f}")
```

## Priors on the group-level scale

With few groups, the prior on $\tau$ does real work, and the grid makes checking it cheap. Gelman (2006)
listed the pitfalls. The once-standard "vague" $\tau^2 \sim \mathrm{InvGamma}(\epsilon, \epsilon)$ has
density in $\tau$ like $\tau^{-2\epsilon - 1} e^{-\epsilon/\tau^2}$, a spike near zero placed by the
arbitrary $\epsilon$, and that is exactly where the data are uninformative. A **uniform** prior on
$\tau$ is fine with many groups but leaves a heavy tail with three. **Half-Cauchy** (Gelman) and
**half-normal** (Stan's advice) are flat near zero and decay in the tail.

```python
def ig_on_tau(eps):                                        # tau^2 ~ InvGamma(eps, eps), as a density on tau
    return lambda t: stats.invgamma.logpdf(t ** 2, eps, scale=eps) + np.log(2 * t)

priors = {"uniform(0, inf)": lambda t: np.zeros_like(t),
          "half-Cauchy(5)": lambda t: stats.halfcauchy.logpdf(t, scale=5),
          "half-normal(5)": lambda t: stats.halfnorm.logpdf(t, scale=5),
          "half-normal(1)": lambda t: stats.halfnorm.logpdf(t, scale=1),
          "IG(1, 1) on tau^2": ig_on_tau(1.0),
          "IG(.01, .01) on tau^2": ig_on_tau(0.01),
          "IG(.001,.001) on tau^2": ig_on_tau(0.001)}
tg_wide = np.linspace(1e-4, 300, 150_000)
print(f"{'prior on tau':>23} | {'8 schools: median':>17} {'95%':>6} {'E[theta_A]':>10} | "
      f"{'3 schools: median':>17} {'95%':>6}")
for name, lpr in priors.items():
    row = []
    for yy, ss in ((y8, s8), (y8[:3], s8[:3])):
        t_, p_, mh, vm = grid_posterior(lpr, yy, ss, tg_wide)
        c_ = np.cumsum(p_)
        row += [t_[np.searchsorted(c_, .5)], t_[np.searchsorted(c_, .95)]]
        if len(yy) == 8:
            row.append(exact_draws(t_, p_, mh, vm, yy, ss, n=40_000)[2][:, 0].mean())
    print(f"{name:>23} | {row[0]:>17.2f} {row[1]:>6.1f} {row[2]:>10.2f} | {row[3]:>17.2f} {row[4]:>6.1f}")
```

Half-Cauchy(5) and half-normal(5) agree (median $\tau \approx 2.8$, School A near 6). Uniform gives more

## Centered parameterization: the funnel returns

The natural way to write the model for NUTS, the **centered** parameterization, says $\theta_j \sim
\mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau)$ directly. Its posterior is Neal's funnel (ch. 6): small $\tau$ forces every
$\theta_j$ into a narrow neck around $\mu$, large $\tau$ lets them spread, and no single step size
serves both. The **non-centered** version samples offsets $z_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and *computes*
$\theta_j = \mu + \tau z_j$. With weak data, the posterior in $(\mu, \tau, z)$ is close to a round blob.

```python
def eight_schools(y, s, centered, seed=1, target_accept=0.9):
    with pm.Model():
        mu = pm.Normal("mu", m0, s0)
        tau = pm.HalfCauchy("tau", 5)
        if centered:
            th = pm.Normal("theta", mu, tau, shape=len(y))
        else:
            z = pm.Normal("z", 0, 1, shape=len(y))
            th = pm.Deterministic("theta", mu + tau * z)
        pm.Normal("y", th, s, observed=y)
        return pm.sample(1000, tune=1000, chains=4, cores=1, random_seed=seed, progressbar=False,
                         quiet=True, target_accept=target_accept)

def report(name, idata):
    tau = idata.posterior["tau"].values.ravel()
    ess = az.ess(idata, var_names=["mu", "tau"])
    print(f"{name:>14} divergences {int(idata.sample_stats['diverging'].values.sum()):>4} | "
          f"ESS mu {float(ess['mu'].values):>5.0f} tau {float(ess['tau'].values):>5.0f} | "
          f"E[tau] {tau.mean():5.2f} | P(tau<1) {np.mean(tau < 1):.3f} | E[log tau] {np.log(tau).mean():+.2f}")

print(f"{'exact (grid)':>14} {'':>16} | {'':>21} | E[tau] {tau_x.mean():5.2f} | "
      f"P(tau<1) {np.mean(tau_x < 1):.3f} | E[log tau] {np.log(tau_x).mean():+.2f}")
report("centered", eight_schools(y8, s8, centered=True))
report("non-centered", eight_schools(y8, s8, centered=False))
```

```output
  exact (grid)                  |                       | E[tau]  3.59 | P(tau<1) 0.201 | E[log tau] +0.80
      centered divergences   84 | ESS mu   522 tau   162 | E[tau]  3.92 | P(tau<1) 0.110 | E[log tau] +1.07
  non-centered divergences    0 | ESS mu  3716 tau  2460 | E[tau]  3.49 | P(tau<1) 0.209 | E[log tau] +0.76
```

The centered model has 84 divergences and is *biased*: $P(\tau < 1)$ is 0.110 against the exact 0.200,
and $\mathbb{E}[\log\tau]$ is $+1.07$ against $+0.80$. It cannot enter the funnel's neck, so it
under-represents exactly the small-$\tau$ region where pooling is strongest, and its $\tau$ ESS is 162.
Raising `target_accept` does not cure it, because the curvature in the neck grows without bound as $\tau
\to 0$. The non-centered model has no divergences and 15 times the ESS (2460), and it matches the grid
(0.209, $+0.76$). It is the same change of variables you applied by hand in ch. 6.

## When the centered form is better

Non-centering helps when data are *weak* relative to $\tau$. When each group has lots of data, the
likelihood pins $\theta_j$, so in the non-centered form $\mu + \tau z_j \approx \bar y_j$ couples $z_j$
tightly to $(\mu, \tau)$, and the funnel reappears the other way round. The rule of thumb (Betancourt
and Girolami, 2015): center groups with $\sigma_j$ small compared to $\tau$, non-center the rest. Below,
every standard error is divided by 10 (100 times more students per school).

```python
rng_s = np.random.default_rng(3)
s_strong = s8 / 10
y_strong = rng_s.normal(rng_s.normal(5, 8, 8), s_strong)
report("centered", eight_schools(y_strong, s_strong, centered=True))
report("non-centered", eight_schools(y_strong, s_strong, centered=False))
```

```output
      centered divergences    0 | ESS mu  7583 tau  7189 | E[tau] 10.75 | P(tau<1) 0.000 | E[log tau] +2.34
  non-centered divergences    0 | ESS mu   689 tau   689 | E[tau] 10.84 | P(tau<1) 0.000 | E[log tau] +2.35
```

The pattern reverses. $\tau$ is pinned near 10.8 and the centered model has an ESS of about 7,500 for
both $\mu$ and $\tau$. The non-centered model gives the same answer with an ESS of 689, about 11 times
worse. It has no divergences, so low ESS is the only warning. The right parameterization depends on how
strongly the data identify each group, and you can choose it group by group.

## Varying intercepts and varying slopes

Groups differ in more than their average. Regress test score on hours of study in 25 schools. Schools
differ in baseline (intercept) *and* in what an extra hour buys (slope), and the two are often
correlated: high-baseline schools gain less per hour near the ceiling. Each school gets a pair $(a_j,
b_j)$ from a common bivariate normal, so schools share information about both coefficients and how they
co-vary:

$$
\text{score}_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(a_{j[i]} + b_{j[i]}\,x_i,\ \sigma\big),\qquad
\begin{pmatrix} a_j \\ b_j \end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix} \mu_a \\ \mu_b \end{pmatrix},
\ \mathrm{diag}(\tau)\,\Omega\,\mathrm{diag}(\tau)\right).
$$

Here $x_i$ is centered study hours, $\tau = (\tau_a, \tau_b)$ are the group-level standard deviations
and $\Omega$ is their correlation matrix, with an **LKJ** prior $p(\Omega) \propto \det(\Omega)^{\eta -
1}$ ($\eta = 1$ is uniform, $\eta > 1$ favours weak correlation). It is non-centered: $(a_j, b_j) = \mu
+ L z_j$ with $L$ the covariance's Cholesky factor.

```python
rng_v, Jv = np.random.default_rng(10), 25
n_stu = rng_v.choice([3, 5, 8, 15, 30, 60], Jv)
mu_ab, tau_ab, rho_true = np.array([60.0, 4.0]), np.array([8.0, 2.0]), -0.6
Sig_true = np.diag(tau_ab) @ np.array([[1, rho_true], [rho_true, 1]]) @ np.diag(tau_ab)
ab_true = rng_v.multivariate_normal(mu_ab, Sig_true, Jv)
school = np.repeat(np.arange(Jv), n_stu)
hours = rng_v.uniform(-3, 3, len(school))                 # centered hours of study
score = ab_true[school, 0] + ab_true[school, 1] * hours + rng_v.normal(0, 6, len(school))

with pm.Model() as varying:
    mu = pm.Normal("mu", [60, 0], [20, 5])
    chol, corr, sds = pm.LKJCholeskyCov("chol", n=2, eta=2, sd_dist=pm.HalfNormal.dist(10, shape=2),
                                        compute_corr=True)
    z = pm.Normal("z", 0, 1, shape=(Jv, 2))
    ab = pm.Deterministic("ab", mu + z @ chol.T)
    pm.Deterministic("tau_ab", sds); pm.Deterministic("rho", corr[0, 1])
    sigma = pm.HalfNormal("sigma", 10)
    pm.Normal("score", ab[school, 0] + ab[school, 1] * hours, sigma, observed=score)
    idv = pm.sample(1000, tune=1000, chains=4, cores=1, random_seed=4, progressbar=False,
                    quiet=True, target_accept=0.9, nuts_sampler="numpyro")   # JAX backend: same NUTS, faster here

summ = az.summary(idv, var_names=["mu", "tau_ab", "rho", "sigma"])
print(f"{len(score)} students in {Jv} schools | divergences {int(idv.sample_stats['diverging'].values.sum())}")
truth = dict(zip(summ.index, [*mu_ab, *tau_ab, rho_true, 6.0]))
for name, row in summ.iterrows():
    print(f"{name:>10}: mean {row['mean']:>6.2f} | sd {row['sd']:>5.2f} | ESS {row['ess_bulk']:>5.0f} "
          f"| r_hat {row['r_hat']:.2f} | truth {truth[name]:>5.1f}")

ab_post = idv.posterior["ab"].values.reshape(-1, Jv, 2).mean(0)
ab_raw = np.array([np.polyfit(hours[school == j], score[school == j], 1)[::-1] for j in range(Jv)])
for c, nm in ((0, "intercept"), (1, "slope")):
    print(f"{nm:>9} RMSE: per-school OLS {rmse(ab_raw[:, c], ab_true[:, c]):.2f} | hierarchical {rmse(ab_post[:, c], ab_true[:, c]):.2f}")
```

     mu[0]: mean  62.54 | sd  1.75 | ESS   344 | r_hat 1.01 | truth  60.0
     mu[1]: mean   3.07 | sd  0.35 | ESS   680 | r_hat 1.00 | truth   4.0

## Predicting for an existing group versus a new one

For a school in the data you use its own posterior $(a_j, b_j)$. For an unseen school you only know it
comes from the population. So for each posterior draw you sample a fresh $(a_\text{new}, b_\text{new})
\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$, which adds the between-school spread to the uncertainty. Below is the
predicted score of a student who studies 2 hours more than average.

```python
post = idv.posterior
mu_d, tau_d = post["mu"].values.reshape(-1, 2), post["tau_ab"].values.reshape(-1, 2)
rho_d, sig_d = post["rho"].values.ravel(), post["sigma"].values.ravel()
ab_d = post["ab"].values.reshape(-1, Jv, 2)
rp, x_new = np.random.default_rng(7), 2.0

def pred_interval(a, b):
    yy = a + b * x_new + rp.normal(0, sig_d)
    lo, hi = np.quantile(yy, [0.05, 0.95])
    return yy.mean(), lo, hi

z1, z2 = rp.standard_normal((2, len(mu_d)))                 # 2x2 Cholesky written out
ab_new = np.c_[mu_d[:, 0] + tau_d[:, 0] * z1,
               mu_d[:, 1] + tau_d[:, 1] * (rho_d * z1 + np.sqrt(1 - rho_d ** 2) * z2)]
big, small = np.argmax(n_stu), np.argmin(n_stu)
print(f"{'school':>22} {'students':>8} {'mean':>6} {'90% interval':>17} {'width':>6} {'true mean':>10}")
for label, j in (("existing, largest", big), ("existing, smallest", small)):
    m, lo, hi = pred_interval(ab_d[:, j, 0], ab_d[:, j, 1])
    print(f"{label:>22} {n_stu[j]:>8} {m:>6.1f} [{lo:6.1f}, {hi:6.1f}] {hi - lo:>6.1f} "
          f"{ab_true[j, 0] + ab_true[j, 1] * x_new:>10.1f}")
m, lo, hi = pred_interval(ab_new[:, 0], ab_new[:, 1])
print(f"{'new school':>22} {0:>8} {m:>6.1f} [{lo:6.1f}, {hi:6.1f}] {hi - lo:>6.1f} "
      f"{mu_ab[0] + mu_ab[1] * x_new:>10.1f}")
```

```output
                school students   mean      90% interval  width  true mean
     existing, largest       60   74.3 [  64.2,   84.4]   20.2       75.3
    existing, smallest        3   61.1 [  49.8,   72.1]   22.3       65.7
            new school        0   68.6 [  53.9,   83.1]   29.2       68.0
```

The largest school's interval (width 20.2) is almost all student-level noise ($\sigma \approx 6$). The
3-student school is only slightly wider (22.3), but its center (61.1) sits 4.6 below its true 65.7,
which is the price of shrinkage on very little data. A new school's interval is 29.2 wide, because it
adds the between-school spread, and its center, 68.6, matches the population truth of 68.0. Predicting a
new store or product from the population mean with only the noise term gives an interval about a third
too narrow, a common forecasting mistake.

## Hierarchical logistic regression: conversion rates per group

Nothing requires Gaussian data. For 40 campaigns with very different traffic, each campaign's conversion
rate has logit $\alpha + s\,z_g$, $z_g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, with a shared typical log-odds $\alpha$
and spread $s$. A campaign with 0 conversions in 20 visits is no longer estimated at exactly zero.

```python
from scipy.special import expit
rng_l, G = np.random.default_rng(5), 40
visits = rng_l.choice([20, 50, 200, 1000, 5000], G)
p_true = expit(rng_l.normal(-3.0, 0.5, G))
conv = rng_l.binomial(visits, p_true)

with pm.Model():
    a, s_ = pm.Normal("a", -2, 1.5), pm.HalfNormal("s", 1)
    z = pm.Normal("z", 0, 1, shape=G)
    p = pm.Deterministic("p", pm.math.invlogit(a + s_ * z))
    pm.Binomial("k", visits, p, observed=conv)
    idl = pm.sample(1000, tune=1000, chains=4, cores=1, random_seed=6, progressbar=False, quiet=True)

p_hat = idl.posterior["p"].values.reshape(-1, G)
raw = conv / visits
pooled = np.full(G, conv.sum() / visits.sum())
print(f"divergences {int(idl.sample_stats['diverging'].values.sum())} | "
      f"a {idl.posterior['a'].values.mean():.2f} (truth -3.0) | s {idl.posterior['s'].values.mean():.2f} (truth 0.5)")
print(f"{'visits':>7} {'conv':>5} {'raw':>7} {'partial':>8} {'90% interval':>17} {'truth':>7}")
for g in np.argsort(visits)[[0, 1, 2, 20, 39]]:
    lo, hi = np.quantile(p_hat[:, g], [0.05, 0.95])
    print(f"{visits[g]:>7} {conv[g]:>5} {raw[g]:>7.4f} {p_hat[:, g].mean():>8.4f} [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}] {p_true[g]:>7.4f}")
for label, mask in (("all", visits > 0), ("visits <= 50", visits <= 50)):
    print(f"RMSE {label:>13}: raw {rmse(raw[mask], p_true[mask]):.4f} | pooled {rmse(pooled[mask], p_true[mask]):.4f}"
          f" | partial {rmse(p_hat.mean(0)[mask], p_true[mask]):.4f}")
```

```output
divergences 0 | a -3.16 (truth -3.0) | s 0.55 (truth 0.5)
 visits  conv     raw  partial      90% interval   truth
     20     1  0.0500   0.0459 [0.0183, 0.0882]  0.0460
     20     0  0.0000   0.0369 [0.0133, 0.0722]  0.0286
     20     0  0.0000   0.0365 [0.0136, 0.0716]  0.0392
    200     9  0.0450   0.0444 [0.0269, 0.0671]  0.0317
   5000   117  0.0234   0.0237 [0.0203, 0.0273]  0.0278
RMSE           all: raw 0.0220 | pooled 0.0246 | partial 0.0117
RMSE  visits <= 50: raw 0.0314 | pooled 0.0161 | partial 0.0149
```

The two 0-of-20 campaigns get about 0.037, with 90% intervals that cover their true rates (0.029,
0.039). The 5000-visit campaign keeps its raw rate (0.0234 to 0.0237). Partial pooling halves the raw
RMSE (0.0117 against 0.0220). Complete pooling helps tiny campaigns but is worst overall (0.0246). This
generalises the Beta-Binomial A/B test (ch. 2) to many arms, and the same model fits per-segment churn
or per-SKU defect rates.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
k, tau, R = 10, 1.0, 4000                      # try k = 4 or 50, tau = 0.2 or 5
theta = rng.normal(0, tau, k)                  # or any fixed vector you like
Y = theta + rng.standard_normal((R, k))        # R replications, one per row
yb = Y.mean(1, keepdims=True)
S = ((Y - yb) ** 2).sum(1, keepdims=True)
est = {"no pooling": Y,
       "complete pooling": np.broadcast_to(yb, Y.shape),
       "James-Stein": yb + np.maximum(0, 1 - (k - 3) / S) * (Y - yb),
       "oracle Bayes": (1 - 1 / (1 + tau ** 2)) * Y}       # knows mu = 0 and tau
for name, e in est.items():
    print(f"{name:>17}: expected total squared error {((e - theta) ** 2).sum(1).mean():6.2f}")
```

```output
       no pooling: expected total squared error  10.09
 complete pooling: expected total squared error   8.85
      James-Stein: expected total squared error   6.34
     oracle Bayes: expected total squared error   4.76
```

James–Stein cuts no pooling's 10.09 to 6.34. The oracle, which knows $B$, reaches 4.76, and the gap is
the price of estimating $B$ from 10 numbers. Try `tau = 5` (James–Stein drifts back to no pooling), `k =
4` (the noisy $(k-3)/S$ eats most of the gain), or a `theta` of nine zeros and one large value:
James–Stein still beats no pooling, but the oracle, whose prior is now wrong, need not.
