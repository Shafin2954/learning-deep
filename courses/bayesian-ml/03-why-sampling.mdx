## A posterior you cannot write down

Chapter 2 only worked because the prior and likelihood were conjugate. Change one modelling choice and
the closed form disappears. The standard example is logistic regression. You have a binary outcome
(did the trial user convert?) and features, with $y_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\sigma(x_i^\top\beta))$
and a weakly informative prior $\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2.5^2)$. The unnormalised log posterior
is easy to evaluate:

$$
\log \tilde p(\beta) = \log p(y, \beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[y_i\,\eta_i - \log\left(1 + e^{\eta_i}\right)\right]
- \frac{\lVert\beta\rVert^2}{2 \cdot 2.5^2} + \text{const}, \qquad \eta_i = x_i^\top\beta
$$

No Gaussian prior is conjugate to the logistic likelihood. The posterior is
$p(\beta \mid y) = \tilde p(\beta) / Z$ with $Z = p(y) = \int \tilde p(\beta)\,d\beta$, and that
integral has no closed form. This chapter uses one small, synthetic dataset as its running example:
40 users, an intercept and two standardised features, so $d = 3$.

```python
import numpy as np
import time
from scipy import stats, special, optimize

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, d, prior_sd = 40, 3, 2.5
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), rng.normal(size=(n, 2))])
beta_true = np.array([-0.5, 1.5, -1.0])
y = (rng.random(n) < special.expit(X @ beta_true)).astype(float)

def log_joint(B, X=X, y=y, s=prior_sd):
    """log p(y, beta) for one beta (d,) or a batch (M, d). Vectorised, no normaliser needed."""
    eta = B @ X.T
    loglik = (y * eta - np.logaddexp(0, eta)).sum(-1)
    return loglik - 0.5 * (B ** 2).sum(-1) / s ** 2 - X.shape[1] * np.log(s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))

print(f"n = {n}, positives = {int(y.sum())}, d = {d}")
for b in (np.zeros(3), beta_true, np.array([0.0, 3.0, -3.0])):
    print(f"log p(y, beta={np.array2string(b, precision=1)}) = {log_joint(b):9.3f}")
```

Each line is one evaluation of the numerator. Differences between them are meaningful: the
posterior density at the true $\beta$ is $e^{\Delta}$ times the density at the origin. The absolute
level is not meaningful until you know $\log Z$.

### Every posterior quantity is a ratio of integrals

Anything you want from the posterior has the same shape:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f(\beta) \mid y] = \frac{\int f(\beta)\,\tilde p(\beta)\,d\beta}{\int \tilde p(\beta)\,d\beta}
$$

A mean takes $f(\beta) = \beta$, a tail probability takes $f = \mathbb{1}[\beta_1 > 1]$, and a
predictive takes $f = p(\tilde y \mid \beta)$. Both the numerator and the denominator are
$d$-dimensional integrals. The rest of the chapter is about the four ways to attack them: put a grid
on them, replace the integrand by a Gaussian, average over random points, or (from ch. 4) build a
Markov chain whose draws do the averaging for you.

## The grid and the $G^d$ wall

The brute-force answer from chapter 1 still works in three dimensions. Put $G$ nodes on each axis of
a box, evaluate $\tilde p$ at all $G^d$ nodes, and replace each integral by a sum times the cell
volume $h^d$. Below the box is $[-5, 5]^3$, chosen generously, and $G$ grows down the rows. The $G = 128$
grid becomes the reference for the next few sections.

```python
def grid_posterior(G, lo=-5.0, hi=5.0, chunk=250_000):
    axis = np.linspace(lo, hi, G)
    h = axis[1] - axis[0]
    pts = np.stack(np.meshgrid(*[axis] * d, indexing="ij"), -1).reshape(-1, d)
    lj = np.concatenate([log_joint(pts[i:i + chunk]) for i in range(0, len(pts), chunk)])
    m = lj.max()
    w = np.exp(lj - m)
    logZ = m + np.log(w.sum()) + d * np.log(h)
    w /= w.sum()
    mean = w @ pts
    return logZ, mean, np.sqrt(w @ (pts - mean) ** 2), w, pts

print(f"{'G':>4} {'evals':>10} {'seconds':>8} {'log Z':>9} {'posterior mean':>26} {'posterior sd':>22}")
for G in (10, 20, 40, 80, 128):
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    logZ_g, mean_g, sd_g, w_g, pts_g = grid_posterior(G)
    sec = time.perf_counter() - t0
    print(f"{G:>4} {G**d:>10,} {sec:>8.3f} {logZ_g:>9.4f} {np.array2string(mean_g, precision=3):>26} "
          f"{np.array2string(sd_g, precision=3):>22}")
ref_grid = dict(logZ=logZ_g, mean=mean_g, sd=sd_g)
per_eval = sec / 128 ** d
print(f"\ncost per evaluation ~ {per_eval * 1e9:.0f} ns. With only G = 30 nodes per axis:")
for dd in (3, 5, 8, 10, 15, 20):
    secs = 30 ** dd * per_eval
    print(f"  d = {dd:>2}: {30 ** dd:9.2e} evaluations ~ {secs:9.2e} s = {secs / 3.15e7:9.2e} years")
```

Three parameters is the comfortable case: by $G = 20$ (8,000 evaluations) the mean, sd and

coarse 30 nodes per axis, the cost is a month at $d = 8$, decades at $d = 10$, a billion years
at $d = 15$, and a million times the age of the universe at $d = 20$. A logistic regression with twenty features is not an exotic model. The grid also
wastes almost all of its work: most of the box carries negligible posterior mass, and the
fraction of wasted cells grows with $d$ (the typical-set section below makes this precise).

## The Laplace approximation

If you cannot integrate $\tilde p$, replace it with something you can. Near its mode, a smooth log
posterior looks like a quadratic, and the exponential of a quadratic is a Gaussian whose integral
you know exactly.

### Derivation

Let $\hat\beta = \arg\max \log\tilde p(\beta)$ and $A = -\nabla^2 \log\tilde p(\hat\beta)$ (the
negative Hessian, positive definite at a strict maximum). The gradient vanishes at the mode, so a
second-order Taylor expansion gives

$$
\log\tilde p(\beta) \approx \log\tilde p(\hat\beta) - \tfrac{1}{2}(\beta - \hat\beta)^\top A\,(\beta - \hat\beta)
$$

Exponentiating and using $\int e^{-\frac12 u^\top A u}du = (2\pi)^{d/2}\lvert A\rvert^{-1/2}$:

$$
p(\beta \mid y) \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\hat\beta,\; A^{-1}\right), \qquad
\boxed{\log Z \approx \log\tilde p(\hat\beta) + \frac{d}{2}\log 2\pi - \frac{1}{2}\log\lvert A\rvert}
$$

For logistic regression the derivatives are explicit. With $p_i = \sigma(\eta_i)$ and
$W = \mathrm{diag}(p_i(1 - p_i))$:

$$
\nabla\log\tilde p = X^\top(y - p) - \beta/s^2, \qquad
\nabla^2\log\tilde p = -X^\top W X - I/s^2
$$

The Hessian is negative definite everywhere, so the log posterior is strictly concave and Newton's
method $\beta \leftarrow \beta - (\nabla^2)^{-1}\nabla$ converges fast. It is the same iteration as
IRLS in a GLM, plus the prior term.

```python
def laplace(X=X, y=y, s=prior_sd, iters=12, verbose=False):
    b = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for it in range(iters):
        p = special.expit(X @ b)
        g = X.T @ (y - p) - b / s ** 2
        H = -(X.T * (p * (1 - p))) @ X - np.eye(X.shape[1]) / s ** 2
        if verbose:
            print(f"  iter {it}: log p(y,b) = {log_joint(b, X, y, s):9.5f}  |grad| = {np.linalg.norm(g):.2e}")
        if np.linalg.norm(g) < 1e-10:
            break
        b = b - np.linalg.solve(H, g)
    cov = np.linalg.inv(-H)
    logZ = log_joint(b, X, y, s) + 0.5 * X.shape[1] * np.log(2 * np.pi) + 0.5 * np.linalg.slogdet(cov)[1]
    return b, cov, logZ

b_map, cov_lap, logZ_lap = laplace(verbose=True)
sd_lap = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_lap))
print(f"\n{'':>14} {'mean / mode':>26} {'sd':>22} {'log Z':>9}")
print(f"{'Laplace':>14} {np.array2string(b_map, precision=3):>26} {np.array2string(sd_lap, precision=3):>22} {logZ_lap:9.4f}")
print(f"{'grid G=128':>14} {np.array2string(ref_grid['mean'], precision=3):>26} "
      f"{np.array2string(ref_grid['sd'], precision=3):>22} {ref_grid['logZ']:9.4f}")
```

Newton reaches machine precision in six steps, each costing one pass over the data. The
Gaussian approximation is in the right place but systematically off. The Laplace "mean" is the posterior *mode*, and
logistic posteriors are skewed away from zero (the likelihood flattens as $|\eta|$ grows), so the
true mean of every coefficient sits further out than the mode: 2.34 against 2.02 for the slope,
about 0.4 posterior sds. The sds are 5 to 6% too small for the same reason. The evidence is off by
0.07 nats, which is small for most model comparisons but not negligible when two models are close. The Bernstein-von Mises theorem says the posterior becomes Gaussian as $n \to \infty$,
with Laplace errors of relative order $1/n$. With 40 observations and 3 parameters you are in the
regime where it is good, not perfect.

### Where Laplace breaks

A Gaussian centred on the mode cannot represent three things: heavy skew, a hard boundary, and more
than one mode. Each case below is one-dimensional, so the exact answer comes from a fine grid.

```python
def exact_1d(logp, g):
    w = np.exp(logp - logp.max()); h = g[1] - g[0]
    logZ = logp.max() + np.log(w.sum() * h); w /= w.sum()
    m = w @ g
    return w, m, np.sqrt(w @ (g - m) ** 2), logZ

def laplace_1d(logp_fn, x0):
    mode = optimize.minimize_scalar(lambda t: -logp_fn(t), bracket=(x0 - 0.1, x0 + 0.1)).x
    eps = 1e-4
    a = -(logp_fn(mode + eps) - 2 * logp_fn(mode) + logp_fn(mode - eps)) / eps ** 2
    return mode, 1 / np.sqrt(a), logp_fn(mode) + 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi / a)

# (a) skew: nearly separable logistic data, slope only, weak N(0, 10^2) prior
xs = np.linspace(-2, 2, 10); ys = np.array([0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1.0])
lp_a = lambda t: (ys * np.multiply.outer(t, xs) - np.logaddexp(0, np.multiply.outer(t, xs))).sum(-1) + stats.norm.logpdf(t, 0, 10)
g_a = np.linspace(-5, 60, 200_001)
# (b) boundary: 1 conversion in 30, flat prior on theta in (0, 1)
lp_b = lambda t: stats.binom.logpmf(1, 30, np.clip(t, 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12))
g_b = np.linspace(1e-7, 1 - 1e-7, 200_001)
# (c) two modes: y_i ~ N(theta^2, 1); the sign of theta is not identified
y_c = rng.normal(2.0 ** 2, 1, 15)
lp_c = lambda t: stats.norm.logpdf(y_c[:, None], np.square(np.atleast_1d(t))[None, :], 1).sum(0).squeeze() + stats.norm.logpdf(t, 0, 3)
g_c = np.linspace(-6, 6, 200_001)

cases = (("(a) skewed slope", lp_a, g_a, 2.0, "P(slope > 10)", lambda t: t > 10, lambda m, sd: stats.norm.sf(10, m, sd)),
         ("(b) bounded rate", lp_b, g_b, 0.05, "P(theta < 0) ", lambda t: t < 0, lambda m, sd: stats.norm.cdf(0, m, sd)),
         ("(c) bimodal     ", lp_c, g_c, 1.5, "P(theta < 0) ", lambda t: t < 0, lambda m, sd: stats.norm.cdf(0, m, sd)))
for name, lp, g, x0, label, event, lap_tail in cases:
    w, m, s, lz = exact_1d(lp(g), g)
    mode, s_l, lz_l = laplace_1d(lp, x0)
    print(f"{name}: mean exact {m:6.3f} vs Laplace {mode:6.3f} | sd {s:6.3f} vs {s_l:6.3f} | "
          f"{label} {w[event(g)].sum():.4f} vs {lap_tail(mode, s_l):.4f} | log Z {lz:7.3f} vs {lz_l:7.3f}")
```

Each failure is a different shape problem. In (a) the data are almost separable, so the likelihood
keeps rising toward large slopes and only the weak prior stops it. The posterior has a long right
tail that a symmetric Gaussian cannot follow: the mean, the sd and the tail probability are all
badly understated, and the Laplace tail probability is 0.0000 where the truth is 0.028. In (b) the
mode is $1/30 = 0.033$ but the mean is $2/32 = 0.063$, and the Gaussian puts 15% of its probability on negative conversion rates. The usual
fix is to apply Laplace to an unconstrained transform (the log-odds here), which is what Stan and
PyMC do automatically for constrained parameters (ch. 8). In (c) Laplace finds one of two mirror-image
modes. It reports a mean of 2.08 where the truth is 0, an sd of 0.06 where the truth is 2.08, no
mass at $\theta < 0$ where there is half of it, and misses $\log Z$ by 0.695, almost exactly
$\log 2 = 0.693$: the mass of the mode it never saw. Optimising cannot discover what it does not climb.

## Monte Carlo integration

The way out of the $G^d$ wall is to stop placing points deterministically. If
$\theta^{(1)}, \dots, \theta^{(N)}$ are independent draws from $p$, then

$$
\hat I_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N} f\left(\theta^{(s)}\right) \quad\text{estimates}\quad I = \mathbb{E}_p[f(\theta)]
$$

### The error does not depend on dimension

$\hat I_N$ is unbiased because each term has expectation $I$. The terms are independent, so

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat I_N) = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{N}, \qquad \sigma_f^2 = \mathrm{Var}_p[f(\theta)],
\qquad \sqrt{N}\left(\hat I_N - I\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_f^2)
$$

The dimension $d$ appears nowhere. It can only enter through $\sigma_f$, which is a property of the
function, not of the number of axes. Compare a product grid with $N$ points: it has
$N^{1/d}$ points per axis, and the midpoint rule's error, $O(h^2) = O(N^{-2/d})$, decays more slowly
than $N^{-1/2}$ as soon as $d > 4$. The test below estimates
$P\left(\sum_j x_j / \sqrt{d} > 1\right)$ for $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$. The answer is
$1 - \Phi(1) = 0.15866$ in every dimension, and $\sigma_f$ is the same Bernoulli sd in every dimension.
Both methods get the same budget of $N = 2^{20}$ points.

```python
truth = stats.norm.sf(1)
N_budget = 2 ** 20

def grid_tail_prob(dd, G, box=5.0):
    edges = np.linspace(-box, box, G + 1)
    c, h = (edges[:-1] + edges[1:]) / 2, edges[1] - edges[0]
    w = stats.norm.pdf(c); w /= w.sum()
    dist = np.array([1.0])                     # a product grid's coordinate-sum lies on a lattice,
    for _ in range(dd):                        # so the G^d-term sum is d convolutions of the 1-D weights
        dist = np.convolve(dist, w)
    s = dd * c[0] + h * np.arange(len(dist))
    return dist[s / np.sqrt(dd) > 1].sum()

print(f"truth {truth:.5f} | MC standard error at N = 2^20: {np.sqrt(truth * (1 - truth) / N_budget):.5f}")
print(f"{'d':>3} {'G per axis':>11} {'grid error':>11} {'MC error':>10}")
for dd in (1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20):
    G = round(N_budget ** (1 / dd))
    x = rng.standard_normal((N_budget, dd), dtype=np.float32)
    mc = np.mean(x.sum(1) / np.sqrt(dd) > 1)
    print(f"{dd:>3} {G:>11} {grid_tail_prob(dd, G) - truth:>+11.5f} {mc - truth:>+10.5f}")

print("\nMC RMSE over 200 repeats, d = 10:")
for N in (100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000):
    est = np.array([np.mean(rng.standard_normal((N, 10)).sum(1) / np.sqrt(10) > 1) for _ in range(200)])
    rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((est - truth) ** 2))
    print(f"  N = {N:>7,}: RMSE {rmse:.5f} | sigma_f / sqrt(N) = {np.sqrt(truth * (1 - truth) / N):.5f} | RMSE x sqrt(N) = {rmse * np.sqrt(N):.3f}")
```

### You need draws, not the normaliser

Monte Carlo needs *draws* from the posterior. It does not need $Z$: the average of $f$ over draws
is already the ratio of integrals from the first section, normaliser included. So the problem
changes from "integrate $\tilde p$" to "generate draws from a density known only up to a
constant". The next two methods do this with independent draws. MCMC (ch. 4 to 6) gives up
independence to get scalability.

## Rejection sampling

Pick a proposal $q$ you can sample and a constant $M$ with $\tilde p(\theta) \le M q(\theta)$
everywhere (an **envelope**). Draw $\theta \sim q$ and accept it with probability
$\tilde p(\theta) / (M q(\theta))$.

### Why the accepted draws are exact

For any set $\mathcal{A}$, the probability that a proposal lands in $\mathcal{A}$ *and* is accepted is

$$
P(\theta \in \mathcal{A},\ \text{accept}) = \int_{\mathcal{A}} q(\theta)\,\frac{\tilde p(\theta)}{M q(\theta)}\,d\theta
= \frac{1}{M}\int_{\mathcal{A}} \tilde p(\theta)\,d\theta
$$

Setting $\mathcal{A}$ to the whole space gives $P(\text{accept}) = Z/M$. Dividing,
$P(\theta \in \mathcal{A} \mid \text{accept}) = \int_{\mathcal{A}}\tilde p / Z$, which is exactly
the posterior. The acceptance rate itself estimates $Z$.

For a Bayesian model, the prior is a natural proposal. Then $\tilde p / q$ is the likelihood, so
$M = L(\hat\beta_{\text{MLE}})$, the maximum of the likelihood, works as the envelope constant:

```python
neg_ll = lambda b: -(y * (X @ b) - np.logaddexp(0, X @ b)).sum()
log_Lmax = -optimize.minimize(neg_ll, np.zeros(d), method="BFGS").fun
N_prop = 400_000
props = rng.normal(0, prior_sd, (N_prop, d))
log_lik = (y * (props @ X.T) - np.logaddexp(0, props @ X.T)).sum(1)
accepted = props[np.log(rng.random(N_prop)) < log_lik - log_Lmax]
acc = len(accepted) / N_prop
print(f"acceptance {acc:.5f} | theory Z/M = exp(log Z - log Lmax) = {np.exp(ref_grid['logZ'] - log_Lmax):.5f}")
print(f"log Z from acceptance rate {np.log(acc) + log_Lmax:.4f} | grid {ref_grid['logZ']:.4f}")
print(f"{len(accepted)} exact draws: mean {np.array2string(accepted.mean(0), precision=3)} | "
      f"sd {np.array2string(accepted.std(0), precision=3)}")
```

The acceptance rate matches $Z/M$ to two significant figures, and turning it around recovers
$\log Z$ to within 0.004. The 2,300 accepted draws reproduce the grid mean and sd, skew included,
within about two Monte Carlo standard errors (about 0.016 for the slope's mean), because they are
exact posterior draws. But only one proposal in 175 survives. The
acceptance rate is the evidence divided by the maximum likelihood, the ratio of the posterior's
volume to the prior's. More data makes the posterior narrower relative to the prior, so the rate
falls as $n$ grows.

### The acceptance rate in high dimension

The rate also falls exponentially with dimension, even when the proposal is almost perfect. Take
the target $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ and a proposal $\mathcal{N}(0, 1.2^2 I_d)$, only 20% wider in each
direction. The tightest envelope is attained at the origin, where $p/q = 1.2^d$. So the acceptance
rate is $1.2^{-d}$.

## Importance sampling

Rejection sampling throws information away: a rejected draw still told you the value of $\tilde p$
there. Importance sampling keeps every draw and weights it instead.

### Derivation: unbiased evidence, self-normalised expectations

For any proposal $q$ with $q > 0$ wherever $\tilde p > 0$, define the weight
$w(\theta) = \tilde p(\theta) / q(\theta)$. Then

$$
Z = \int \tilde p(\theta)\,d\theta = \mathbb{E}_q[w(\theta)] \approx \hat Z = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{s} w_s,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}_p[f] = \frac{\mathbb{E}_q[w f]}{\mathbb{E}_q[w]} \approx \sum_{s}\bar w_s f(\theta_s),
\quad \bar w_s = \frac{w_s}{\sum_r w_r}
$$

$\hat Z$ is unbiased. The second estimator is **self-normalised importance sampling** (SNIS). It is
a ratio, so it has $O(1/N)$ bias, but it only needs $\tilde p$, which is all you have. The variance
of $\hat Z$ is $\frac{1}{N}\left(\mathbb{E}_q[w^2] - Z^2\right)$, and

$$
\mathbb{E}_q[w^2] = \int \frac{\tilde p(\theta)^2}{q(\theta)}\,d\theta
$$

is finite only if $q$ has tails at least as heavy as $p$, roughly. A proposal that is too narrow
gives infinite variance, which means an estimator whose error does not shrink like $1/\sqrt N$ and
that looks fine in most runs.

### Effective sample size

If the weights were fixed and the $f(\theta_s)$ were independent with variance $\sigma^2$,
$\mathrm{Var}(\sum_s \bar w_s f_s) = \sigma^2\sum_s \bar w_s^2$. Matching this to $\sigma^2 / N_{\text{eff}}$
gives Kish's effective sample size:

$$
\mathrm{ESS} = \frac{1}{\sum_s \bar w_s^2} = \frac{\left(\sum_s w_s\right)^2}{\sum_s w_s^2}
$$

It equals $N$ for equal weights and 1 when a single draw holds all the weight. It is a heuristic (it
ignores $f$), but it is the first number to look at. The code compares four proposals on the
logistic posterior, all built from the Laplace fit except the last one.

```python
def snis(log_w, draws):
    lw = log_w - log_w.max()
    w = np.exp(lw)
    logZ_hat = log_w.max() + np.log(w.mean())
    wb = w / w.sum()
    mean = wb @ draws
    sd = np.sqrt(wb @ (draws - mean) ** 2)
    return logZ_hat, mean, sd, 1 / np.sum(wb ** 2), wb.max()
```

### Tail mismatch: when the variance is infinite

The narrow-proposal problem can be computed exactly. With target $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and proposal
$\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$,

$$
\mathbb{E}_q[w^2] = \int \frac{\phi(x)^2}{\phi(x/\sigma)/\sigma}\,dx = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sqrt{2\sigma^2 - 1}}
\quad (\sigma^2 > \tfrac{1}{2}), \qquad \text{infinite for } \sigma^2 \le \tfrac{1}{2}
$$

The asymptotic ESS fraction is $1/\mathbb{E}_q[w^2] = \sqrt{2\sigma^2 - 1}/\sigma^2$. In $d$
independent dimensions it is raised to the power $d$, which is the same exponential collapse that
rejection sampling showed. The code estimates $\mathbb{E}_p[x^2] = 1$ by SNIS, 300 times per
proposal.

```python
def ess_frac(sig):
```

## Diagnosing importance weights with Pareto $\hat k$

ESS is computed from the very weights whose tail you are worried about, so it cannot detect a tail it
has not sampled. Vehtari et al.'s **Pareto-smoothed importance sampling** (PSIS) looks at the shape
of the weight distribution instead. It fits a generalised Pareto distribution to the largest weights.
The fitted shape $\hat k$ estimates how many moments of $w$ exist: moments of order below $1/k$ are
finite. So $\hat k < 0.5$ means finite variance. Between 0.5 and 0.7 the estimate is still usable with
smoothing, and $\hat k > 0.7$ means the estimate cannot be trusted. PSIS also replaces the largest
weights with quantiles of the fitted Pareto, which reduces the variance. The sketch below does only
the diagnostic, with a plain maximum-likelihood fit (the reference implementation uses a regularised
estimator).

```python
def pareto_khat(log_w):
    w = np.sort(np.exp(log_w - log_w.max()))
    M = int(min(0.2 * len(w), 3 * np.sqrt(len(w))))      # tail size used by PSIS
    tail, u = w[-M:], w[-M - 1]
    k, _, _ = stats.genpareto.fit(tail - u, floc=0)
    return k

for name, lw in is_logw.items():
    print(f"logistic, {name:>23}: khat = {pareto_khat(lw):+.2f}")
for sig in (0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 1.5):
    x = rng.normal(0, sig, 100_000)
    k = pareto_khat(stats.norm.logpdf(x) - stats.norm.logpdf(x, 0, sig))
    theory = f"{1 - sig ** 2:+.2f}" if sig < 1 else "-2 (bounded weights)"
    print(f"N(0,1) target, sigma_q = {sig}: khat = {k:+.2f} | theory {theory}")
```

safely negative. For the Gaussian experiment you can derive the truth. With $\sigma < 1$,
$w \propto e^{x^2(1/\sigma^2 - 1)/2}$ and $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ give
$P(w > t) \sim t^{-1/(1 - \sigma^2)}$, so $k = 1 - \sigma^2$. That crosses 0.5 exactly at
$\sigma^2 = 1/2$, the infinite-variance boundary derived above. The estimates track it, biased low
for the heaviest tail (0.51 against 0.64): a tail you have barely sampled is hard to measure, which
is why the reference implementation regularises the fit. Chapter 9 uses PSIS for its most important job, leave-one-out cross-validation
from a single posterior fit (PSIS-LOO). There each data point's "proposal" is the full posterior,
and $\hat k$ flags the observations that are too influential for the shortcut.

## Concentration of measure and the typical set

Every method so far has broken down in high dimension in the same way: a fixed, local inefficiency
compounds multiplicatively across coordinates. The underlying cause is geometric, and it decides how
MCMC has to work.

### Mass is density times volume

For $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ the density is largest at the mode. Mass, however, is density
times volume, and the volume of a shell at radius $r$ grows like $r^{d-1}$. The radius therefore
has density

$$
p(r) \propto r^{d-1} e^{-r^2/2}, \qquad
\frac{d}{dr}\left[(d-1)\log r - \frac{r^2}{2}\right] = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; r^{*} = \sqrt{d-1}
$$

and $r^2 = \lVert\theta\rVert^2 \sim \chi^2_d$ has mean $d$ and variance $2d$. By the delta method,
$\mathrm{sd}(r) \approx \sqrt{2d}/(2\sqrt d) = 1/\sqrt{2}$, whatever $d$ is. The mass lives in a shell of
radius about $\sqrt d$ and constant thickness. That shell is the **typical set**. The mode is not
in it, and neither is anything near it.


close to the Gaussian value $d/2 = 1.5$.

This explains the failures above. A grid and an optimiser both spend their effort near the mode.
Rejection sampling and importance sampling have to hit a thin shell with a proposal whose own shell
is slightly misplaced, and in $d$ dimensions "slightly" compounds. A sampler that scales has to
*move along* the typical set once it has found it. That is what a Markov chain does (ch. 4). A
random-walk chain moves through the shell diffusively, with step sizes that must shrink as $d$
grows. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (ch. 6) follows the shell using gradient information, which is
why it is the default in Stan and PyMC.

```python
def rwm(lj, x0, cov, n_steps, rng):

    for t in range(n_steps):
        prop = x + L @ rng.standard_normal(len(x0))
        lp = lj(prop)
        if np.log(rng.random()) < lp - lx:
            x, lx, acc = prop, lp, acc + 1
        out[t] = x
    return out, acc / n_steps
```

why its sd error (4%) is larger than SNIS's. Its cost,
however, grows only polynomially with $d$. That trade is why the rest of this course is about
Markov chains.

| Method | What it needs | Gives $\log Z$? | Cost as $d$ grows | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grid | $\tilde p$ on a box | yes | $G^d$, hopeless past $d \approx 6$ | the wall |
| Laplace | mode, Hessian | yes (approx.) | $O(d^3)$ | skew, bounds, multimodality |
| Importance sampling | proposal $q$ near $p$ | yes, unbiased | ESS decays like $c^{d}$ | narrow-tailed $q$, hidden bias |
| MCMC (ch. 4 to 6) | $\tilde p$ (and its gradient for HMC) | no | polynomial in $d$ | slow mixing, needs diagnostics (ch. 7) |

```python
    beta = rng.normal(0, 1, dim)
    y = (rng.random(n) < special.expit(X @ beta)).astype(float)
    lj = lambda B: (y * (B @ X.T) - np.logaddexp(0, B @ X.T)).sum(-1) - 0.5 * (B ** 2).sum(-1) / s ** 2
    b = np.zeros(dim)
    for _ in range(25):                           # Newton to the mode
        p = special.expit(X @ b)
        H = -(X.T * (p * (1 - p))) @ X - np.eye(dim) / s ** 2
        b = b - np.linalg.solve(H, X.T @ (y - p) - b / s ** 2)
    cov = np.linalg.inv(-H)
    out = []
    for name, q in (("Gaussian", stats.multivariate_normal(b, cov)),
                    ("t4", stats.multivariate_t(b, cov, df=4)),
                    ("narrow", stats.multivariate_normal(b, cov / 2))):
        th = q.rvs(10_000, random_state=rng)
        lw = lj(th) - q.logpdf(th)
        w = np.exp(lw - lw.max())
        out.append(w.sum() ** 2 / (w ** 2).sum() / len(w))
    return out

print(f"{'d':>3} {'ESS/N Gaussian':>15} {'ESS/N t4':>9} {'ESS/N narrow':>13}")
for dim in (2, 5, 10, 20, 40):
    g, t, nr = run(dim)
    print(f"{dim:>3} {g:>15.3f} {t:>9.3f} {nr:>13.3f}")
```

Importance sampling from the Laplace fit is about as good as an independent-draw method gets,
and with 200 observations it still collapses: ESS/N falls from 0.98 at $d = 2$ to 0.03 at $d = 20$
and 0.002 at $d = 40$. No proposal family survives. Even the heavy-tailed t loses at $d = 40$,
because in high dimension a multivariate t spreads its radius over a wide range and wastes most draws
off the typical shell. Raise $n$ and the posterior becomes more Gaussian (Bernstein-von Mises), so
the collapse moves to higher $d$. Lower it and the skew makes things worse. The "narrow" column
is the one to distrust: even where its ESS is non-zero, its estimates are biased in the tails.

With the default prior, the evidence prefers the model with $x_2$ by $e^{0.80} \approx 2.2$ to 1,
weak evidence, as you would expect from 40 observations of a moderate effect. Multiply the prior sd
by 10 and the Bayes factor flips to $e^{-1.10} \approx 1/3$, against $x_2$, although the posterior
mode moves only from $-0.96$ to $-1.08$. The posterior barely depends on the prior, but the evidence
depends on it directly. In the Laplace formula, $\log Z$ contains $\log p(\hat\beta)$, and a
prior spread over a 10 times wider range assigns roughly 10 times less density to the region the
data support. That costs up to $\log 10 = 2.3$ nats per extra parameter (1.9 here), an Occam
penalty that grows without bound as the prior widens (Lindley's paradox). So Bayes factors require
proper, deliberately chosen priors (ch. 9). SNIS and the grid agree to within the 0.0014 standard
error. Laplace is 0.035 and 0.057 nats off: small next to these Bayes factors, but large enough to
matter when two models are nearly tied.
