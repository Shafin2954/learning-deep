## What can go wrong, and why one chain cannot tell you

Every MCMC estimate assumes the chain has reached its stationary distribution and explored it well
enough for averages to be accurate. Four failures break that. **Non-convergence**: the chain is
still drifting away from a bad start. **Poor mixing**: it is in the right place but so slow that
2000 draws are worth 20. **Multimodality**: it explores one mode and never visits the other.
**Heavy tails**: it mixes, but means and variances are unstable or do not exist. Below are five random-walk samplers (ch. 4), 4 chains each, started from the overdispersed points
$-6, -2, 2, 6$. One is healthy and four are broken. The question is whether one chain could tell you.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, signal
import arviz as az

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

def rwm1(logp, x0, n, step, rng):                      # 1-D random-walk Metropolis, all chains in lockstep
    x = np.array(x0, float); lp = logp(x); out = np.empty((len(x), n))
    for t in range(n):
        prop = x + step * rng.standard_normal(len(x)); lpp = logp(prop)
        acc = np.log(rng.random(len(x))) < lpp - lp
        x, lp = np.where(acc, prop, x), np.where(acc, lpp, lp)
        out[:, t] = x
    return out

starts, N = np.array([-6.0, -2.0, 2.0, 6.0]), 2000
std_normal = lambda x: -0.5 * x ** 2
samplers = {                                            # each value: (chains, draws) after 1000 warmup
    "healthy":  rwm1(std_normal, starts, N + 1000, 2.4, rng)[:, 1000:],
    "sticky":   rwm1(std_normal, starts, N + 1000, 0.05, rng)[:, 1000:],          # tiny step
    "bimodal":  rwm1(lambda x: np.logaddexp(-0.5 * (x + 5) ** 2, -0.5 * (x - 5) ** 2),
                     starts, N + 1000, 1.0, rng)[:, 1000:],                       # modes at -5, +5
    "drifting": rwm1(std_normal, starts - 12, N, 0.06, rng),                      # bad init, no warmup
    "heavy":    rwm1(lambda x: -np.log1p(x ** 2), starts, N + 1000, 3.0, rng)[:, 1000:],  # Cauchy target
}
x1 = samplers["bimodal"][3]                             # one chain of the bimodal sampler, alone
print(f"bimodal chain 4 alone: mean {x1.mean():+.2f} | sd {x1.std():.2f} | "
      f"first-half mean {x1[:N//2].mean():+.2f} | second-half mean {x1[N//2:].mean():+.2f}")
print("truth: mean +0.00 | sd 5.10")
for name, x in samplers.items():
    print(f"{name:9} chain means {np.array2string(x.mean(1), precision=2, sign='+'):34} "
          f"chain sds {np.array2string(x.std(1), precision=2)}")
```

Chain 4 of the bimodal sampler is a perfect-looking sample from $\mathcal{N}(5, 1)$: stable halves,
the right local spread. No statistic computed from that chain alone can reveal a mode it never
visited. Chains started wider than the posterior can, because they disagree, and that disagreement
is what $\hat R$ measures. The chain means hint at the other failures too: sticky means spread over
1.5 units on a target of sd 1, drifting chains still between $-7.5$ and $-1.2$, and heavy-tailed
chain sds from 2.8 to 10.2, which is what a target with no finite variance does to a sample sd.

## Classic R-hat: between-chain against within-chain variance

### Deriving var+ and R-hat

Gelman and Rubin (1992) compare a within-chain variance estimate, too small before convergence,
with a pooled one, too large under overdispersed starts. Take $M$ chains of $N$ draws $\theta_{nm}$, with chain means $\bar\theta_{\cdot m}$, grand mean
$\bar\theta_{\cdot\cdot}$ and chain variances $s_m^2$:

$$
B = \frac{N}{M-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\big(\bar\theta_{\cdot m} - \bar\theta_{\cdot\cdot}\big)^2,
\qquad
W = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} s_m^2 .
$$

$B$ is scaled by $N$ because $\mathrm{Var}(\bar\theta_{\cdot m}) \approx \sigma^2/N$ for
independent draws, so $B$ estimates $\sigma^2$ when all chains share one distribution. The pooled
estimate of the marginal posterior variance and the potential scale reduction are

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^{+} = \frac{N-1}{N}\,W + \frac{1}{N}\,B,
\qquad
\hat R = \sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^{+}}{W}} = \sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N} + \frac{B}{N\,W}} .
$$

$\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^{+}$ is unbiased under stationarity and an overestimate under overdispersed
starts, while $W$ underestimates before convergence, so $\hat R \to 1$ from above. **Split
$\hat R$** (BDA3) cuts every chain in half and treats the halves as $2M$ chains. A drifting chain
then has two halves that disagree, which raises $B$ even if all chains drift the same way.

```python
def split(x):                                           # (M, N) -> (2M, N // 2): first and last halves
    h = x.shape[1] // 2
    return np.vstack([x[:, :h], x[:, -h:]])

def rhat_basic(x):
    M, n = x.shape
    B = n * x.mean(1).var(ddof=1)
    W = x.var(1, ddof=1).mean()
    return np.sqrt((n - 1) / n + B / (n * W))

same_drift = samplers["drifting"] - samplers["drifting"].mean(1, keepdims=True)    # equalise chain means
for name, x in (*samplers.items(), ("drift, equal means", same_drift)):
    print(f"{name:19} classic R-hat {rhat_basic(x):6.3f} (arviz {az.rhat(x, method='identity'):6.3f}) | "
          f"split R-hat {rhat_basic(split(x)):6.3f} (arviz {az.rhat(x, method='split'):6.3f})")
```

that do not exist. The next section shows why that verdict cannot be trusted.

## Rank normalisation and folding

Classic $\hat R$ compares means and variances, which leaves two blind spots (Vehtari, Gelman,
Simpson, Carpenter and Bürkner 2021). With heavy tails the variances are dominated by a few extreme
draws, or do not exist, so $B/W$ is noise. And chains with the *same mean but different spread*
have $B \approx 0$ and pass. The fix is **rank normalisation**: pool all $S = MN$ draws, replace
each by its rank $r$, and map ranks to normal scores,

$$
z_{nm} = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{r_{nm} - 3/8}{S + 1/4}\right),
$$

with Blom's $3/8$ offset. The $z$ values are finite and approximately normal whatever the target,
and the map is monotone, so a mixing failure in $\theta$ remains one in $z$. Split $\hat R$ on $z$ is
**bulk** $\hat R$. For scale, **fold** the draws, $\zeta = |\theta - \mathrm{median}(\theta)|$, then
rank-normalise and apply split $\hat R$: **folded** $\hat R$ measures disagreement in spread, that
is, in the tails. The reported statistic is $\max(\hat R_{\text{bulk}}, \hat R_{\text{folded}})$.

```python
def znorm(x):                                           # rank-normalise jointly across all chains
    r = stats.rankdata(x, method="average").reshape(x.shape)
    return stats.norm.ppf((r - 3 / 8) / (x.size + 1 / 4))

def rhat_folded(x):
    s = split(x)
    return rhat_basic(znorm(np.abs(s - np.median(s))))

def rhat_rank(x):
    return max(rhat_basic(znorm(split(x))), rhat_folded(x))

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
cases = {
    "iid Cauchy, 4 identical chains": lambda: rng.standard_cauchy((4, 1000)),
    "Cauchy, chain 1 shifted by +1":  lambda: rng.standard_cauchy((4, 1000)) + np.array([[1], [0], [0], [0]]),
    "Normal, chain 1 has sd 3":       lambda: rng.standard_normal((4, 1000)) * np.array([[3], [1], [1], [1]]),
}
print(f"{'200 replicates each':32} {'statistic':>9} {'median':>7} {'90% range':>16} {'P(>1.01)':>9}")
for name, gen in cases.items():
    reps = [gen() for _ in range(200)]
    for stat, f in (("split", lambda x: rhat_basic(split(x))), ("rank", rhat_rank)):
        r = np.array([f(x) for x in reps])
        lo, hi = np.quantile(r, [0.05, 0.95])
        print(f"{name:32} {stat:>9} {np.median(r):7.3f}   [{lo:6.3f}, {hi:6.3f}] {np.mean(r > 1.01):9.2f}")
x_test = cases["Normal, chain 1 has sd 3"]()
print(f"arviz check: mine {rhat_rank(x_test):.6f} | az.rhat {az.rhat(x_test):.6f} | "
      f"folded mine {rhat_folded(x_test):.6f} | az folded {az.rhat(x_test, method='folded'):.6f}")
```

With four identical Cauchy chains both statistics are calm, so classic $\hat R$ raises no false
alarms here. Its problem is blindness. Shift one Cauchy chain by a whole unit, a real failure, and
split $\hat R$ stays at 1.000 in every replicate, because a single extreme draw dominates both $B$
and $W$. Rank $\hat R$ is above 1.01 in all 200. With one Normal chain three times wider than the
others the means agree, split $\hat R$ is blind again, and the folded half of rank $\hat R$ reports
about 1.15 every time. The implementation agrees with `az.rhat` to six decimals.

## Effective sample size across chains

### Autocorrelation and the multi-chain estimator

$\hat R$ asks whether chains agree; ESS asks how much information they carry. Chapter 4 built a
single-chain ESS. For a stationary chain with lag-$t$ autocorrelation $\rho_t$, the mean of $S$ draws has variance
$\sigma^2 \tau / S$ with $\tau = 1 + 2\sum_{t \ge 1}\rho_t$, so $\mathrm{ESS} = S/\tau$. A chain's
own autocovariance $\hat\gamma_{t,m}$ is measured around its own mean, so it looks fine even if the
chains disagree. Vehtari et al. combine the chains through $\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^{+}$:

$$
\hat\rho_t = 1 - \frac{W - \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\hat\gamma_{t,m}}{\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^{+}} .
$$

When the chains agree, $\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^{+} \approx W$ and this is the average
autocorrelation. When they disagree, the between-chain part of $\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^{+}$ pushes
every $\hat\rho_t$ toward 1 and ESS collapses, so ESS also detects non-convergence.

### Geyer's initial monotone sequence

The tail of $\hat\rho_t$ is noise, and summing all of it gives $\tau \approx 0$. Geyer (1992)
showed that for a reversible chain the pair sums $P_k = \rho_{2k} + \rho_{2k+1}$ are positive and
decreasing. So the estimator stops at the first $\hat P_k \le 0$ (initial positive sequence) and
replaces each $\hat P_k$ by $\min_{j \le k}\hat P_j$ (initial monotone sequence):

$$
\hat\tau = -1 + 2\sum_{k=0}^{K}\min_{j \le k}\hat P_j, \qquad \mathrm{ESS} = \frac{MN}{\hat\tau}.
$$

**Bulk-ESS** applies this to rank-normalised split chains. **Tail-ESS** is the smaller ESS of the
indicators $I(\theta \le q_{0.05})$ and $I(\theta \le q_{0.95})$, which govern the 5% and 95%
quantile estimates and hence the 90% interval.

```python
def autocov(x):                                         # per-chain autocovariance by FFT, all lags
    n = x.shape[1]
    f = np.fft.rfft(x - x.mean(1, keepdims=True), 2 * n)
    return np.fft.irfft(f * np.conj(f), 2 * n)[:, :n] / n

def ess_core(x):                                        # x: (chains, draws), already split/transformed
    M, n = x.shape
    if np.ptp(x) == 0: return float(x.size)
    acov = autocov(x)
    W = acov[:, 0].mean() * n / (n - 1)
    var_plus = W * (n - 1) / n + (x.mean(1).var(ddof=1) if M > 1 else 0.0)
    rho = 1 - (W - acov.mean(0)) / var_plus
    rho[0] = 1.0
    P = (rho[:-1:2] + rho[1::2])[: (n - 3) // 2 + 1]    # pair sums P_k = rho_2k + rho_2k+1
    neg = np.flatnonzero(P <= 0)                        # initial positive sequence: stop at first P_k <= 0
    K = max(1, neg[0]) if len(neg) else len(P) - 1
    P = np.minimum.accumulate(P[:K])                    # initial monotone sequence
    tau = -1 + 2 * P.sum() + max(rho[2 * K], 0)         # + the leftover even lag, as Stan and ArviZ do
    return x.size / max(tau, 1 / np.log10(x.size))

ess_mean = lambda x: ess_core(split(x))
ess_bulk = lambda x: ess_core(znorm(split(x)))
ess_q = lambda x, p: ess_core(split((x <= np.quantile(x, p)).astype(float)))
ess_tail = lambda x: min(ess_q(x, 0.05), ess_q(x, 0.95))

def ar1(phi, M, n, rng):                                # stationary AR(1), unit marginal variance
    e = rng.standard_normal((M, n)) * np.sqrt(1 - phi ** 2); e[:, 0] = rng.standard_normal(M)
    return signal.lfilter([1.0], [1.0, -phi], e, axis=1)

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
print(f"{'4 x 5000 AR(1)':16} {'theory':>7} {'mean':>7} {'bulk':>7} {'tail':>7}   max |mine - arviz|")
for phi in (0.0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99):
    x = ar1(phi, 4, 5000, rng)
    mine = [ess_mean(x), ess_bulk(x), ess_tail(x)]
    ref = [az.ess(x, method="mean"), az.ess(x), az.ess(x, method="tail", prob=(0.05, 0.95))]
    print(f"phi = {phi:<10} {20000 * (1 - phi) / (1 + phi):7.0f} " + " ".join(f"{v:7.0f}" for v in mine)
          + f"   {max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(mine, ref)):.1e}")
```

```output

phi = 0.5           6667    6508    6504   11231   1.8e-12
```

chain that reaches the tails only in rare, long excursions has tail-ESS below bulk-ESS (the
heavy-tailed sampler below: 134 against 224), which is why both are reported.

## Monte Carlo standard error for means and quantiles

ESS becomes an error bar. For the mean, $\mathrm{MCSE}(\bar\theta) = \hat\sigma/\sqrt{\mathrm{ESS}_{\text{mean}}}$.
For a quantile $q_p$ there is no $\sigma$ to divide. The number of draws below $q_p$ behaves like a
binomial count from $\mathrm{ESS}_p$ trials, so the probability level is approximately
$\mathrm{Beta}(\mathrm{ESS}_p\, p + 1,\ \mathrm{ESS}_p(1-p) + 1)$. Take its 15.9% and 84.1% points
($\pm 1$ sd), read off the matching order statistics, and halve the gap. The check below compares
the average MCSE over 500 independent 4-chain runs with the actual spread of the estimates.

```python
def mcse_mean(x):
    return x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(ess_mean(x))

def mcse_quantile(x, p):
    e = ess_q(x, p)
    a, b = stats.beta.ppf([0.1586553, 0.8413447], e * p + 1, e * (1 - p) + 1)
    s = np.sort(x.ravel()); S = s.size
    return (s[int(np.ceil(min(b * S - 1, S - 1)))] - s[int(np.floor(max(a * S - 1, 0)))]) / 2

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
reps = [ar1(0.8, 4, 1000, rng) for _ in range(500)]
for label, est, mc in (("mean", np.mean, mcse_mean),
                       ("5% quantile", lambda x: np.quantile(x, 0.05), lambda x: mcse_quantile(x, 0.05))):
    e = np.array([est(x) for x in reps]); m = np.array([mc(x) for x in reps])
    print(f"{label:12} actual sd of estimate {e.std():.4f} | average MCSE {m.mean():.4f}")
x = reps[0]
print(f"arviz check: mcse mean {mcse_mean(x):.6f} vs {az.mcse(x):.6f} | "
      f"mcse q05 {mcse_quantile(x, 0.05):.6f} vs {az.mcse(x, method='quantile', prob=0.05):.6f}")
```

```output
mean         actual sd of estimate 0.0423 | average MCSE 0.0479
5% quantile  actual sd of estimate 0.0659 | average MCSE 0.0680
arviz check: mcse mean 0.045150 vs 0.045150 | mcse q05 0.053300 vs 0.053300
```

The quantile MCSE is within 3% of the truth and the mean MCSE about 13% conservative (ESS is itself
noisy), so one run predicts how far a rerun would move the estimate. The 5% quantile carries 1.5
times the error of the mean. Report a mean of 0.0312 with MCSE 0.02 as $0.03 \pm 0.02$.

## Why R-hat < 1.01 and ESS > 400

Write $s_b^2 = B/N$ for the variance of the chain means. For large $N$,

$$
\hat R^2 \approx 1 + \frac{s_b^2}{W}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{s_b}{\sqrt W} \approx \sqrt{\hat R^2 - 1}.
$$

The old threshold $\hat R < 1.1$ lets chain means scatter by $\sqrt{0.21} \approx 0.46$ posterior
sds, enough to hide an unconverged mode. The threshold $1.01$ allows $0.14$ sds. But a threshold
is only usable if it exceeds the sampling noise of $\hat R$ for chains that *have* converged, and
that noise depends on ESS.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
print(f"{'4 iid chains of n':18} {'total ESS':>9} {'95th pct R-hat':>15} {'P(R-hat > 1.01)':>16} {'MCSE / sd':>10}")
for n in (25, 50, 100, 400, 1000):
    r = np.array([rhat_rank(rng.standard_normal((4, n))) for _ in range(300)])
    print(f"{n:18} {4 * n:9} {np.quantile(r, 0.95):15.3f} {np.mean(r > 1.01):16.2f} {1 / np.sqrt(4 * n):10.3f}")
```

```output

                25       100           1.063             0.49      0.100
                50       200           1.024             0.34      0.071
               100       400           1.015             0.12      0.050
               400      1600           1.003             0.00      0.025
```

At a total ESS of 100, perfectly converged chains exceed 1.01 about half the time. At 400 the
false-alarm rate is 12%, and at 1600 zero. That is where ESS $> 400$ (about 100 per chain) comes
from: the smallest ESS at which the noise of rank $\hat R$ is about the size of the threshold. It
also makes the autocorrelation estimates stable and the MCSE of a mean $\sigma/20$, 5% of a
posterior sd. Apply it to bulk *and* tail ESS of *every* parameter. The worst one decides.

## Trace forensics without pictures

Trace plots do not scale to 300 parameters and cannot be checked in a CI log. Two numbers carry
most of what the eye takes from them. **Running means** per chain, in pooled-sd units at 10%,
25%, 50% and 100% of the run, show drift as a trend and stickiness as disagreement. **Rank
histograms**: rank all draws jointly and count each chain's draws in 20 equal rank bins. If the
chains sample the same distribution, every histogram is uniform. Summarise each by its chi-square
against uniform per degree of freedom. Independent draws give about 1, and autocorrelation inflates
it by roughly the autocorrelation time, so a converged chain shows a small single-digit value. A
chain sampling a different distribution deviates systematically, so its statistic grows in
proportion to $N$. Do not read a p-value off $\chi^2_{19}$: the draws are not independent.

```python
def forensics(x, bins=20):
    M, n = x.shape
    run = [(x[:, :int(f * n)].mean(1) - x.mean()) / x.std() for f in (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0)]
    r = stats.rankdata(x).reshape(x.shape)
    counts = np.array([np.histogram(r[m], bins=bins, range=(0.5, x.size + 0.5))[0] for m in range(M)])
    return np.array(run).T, ((counts - n / bins) ** 2 / (n / bins)).sum(1) / (bins - 1)

print("running means at 10/25/50/100% of the run (pooled-sd units) | rank-histogram chi2/df")
for name in ("drifting", "heavy"):
    run, c = forensics(samplers[name])
    for m in range(4):
        print(f"{name:8} chain {m + 1}: {np.array2string(run[m], precision=2, sign='+', floatmode='fixed')} | {c[m]:6.1f}")
```

## HMC-specific diagnostics: divergences, E-BFMI, tree depth

HMC (ch. 6) has three diagnostics of its own. A **divergence** is a trajectory whose energy error
blows up (here, above 1000): the step size is too large for the local curvature, the region is
under-sampled, and even a handful mean biased estimates. **E-BFMI** (Betancourt 2016) asks whether
momentum resampling can move the chain across energy levels. Each iteration changes the energy
$E$ only through fresh momentum. If the marginal energy distribution is much wider than those
changes, the chain random-walks in energy:

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{E\text{-}BFMI}} = \frac{\sum_{n=2}^{N}(E_n - E_{n-1})^2 / (N-1)}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}(E_n - \bar E)^2 / (N-1)} ,
$$

and values below 0.3 mean the tails will be under-explored. Below, a compact HMC with energy
logging on Neal's funnel (ch. 6), centred and non-centred.

```python
def funnel(x):                                          # v ~ N(0, 3), x_i | v ~ N(0, e^{v/2}), 9 x's
    v, z = x[:, 0], x[:, 1:]
    s2, e = np.sum(z * z, 1), np.exp(-v)
    return -v ** 2 / 18 - 0.5 * s2 * e - 4.5 * v, np.column_stack([-v / 9 + 0.5 * s2 * e - 4.5, -z * e[:, None]])

funnel_nc = lambda x: (-0.5 * np.sum(x * x, 1), -x)     # non-centred: everything N(0, 1), v = 3 x_0

def hmc(f, x0, n, eps, L, rng):
    x = x0.copy(); C, d = x.shape; lp, g = f(x)
    out, E, div = np.empty((C, n, d)), np.empty((C, n)), np.zeros((C, n), bool)
    for t in range(n):
        p = rng.standard_normal((C, d)); H0 = -lp + 0.5 * np.sum(p * p, 1)
        e = eps * rng.uniform(0.8, 1.2)                 # jittered step size
        q, pq = x.copy(), p + 0.5 * e * g               # leapfrog: half step, full steps, half step
        with np.errstate(all="ignore"):
            for l in range(L):
                q = q + e * pq; lq, gq = f(q)
                pq = pq + (e if l < L - 1 else 0.5 * e) * gq
            dH = np.nan_to_num(-lq + 0.5 * np.sum(pq * pq, 1) - H0, nan=np.inf)
        acc = np.log(rng.random(C)) < -dH
        x, lp, g = np.where(acc[:, None], q, x), np.where(acc, lq, lp), np.where(acc[:, None], gq, g)
        out[:, t], E[:, t], div[:, t] = x, H0, dH > 1000
    return out, E, div

ebfmi = lambda E: np.mean(np.diff(E, axis=1) ** 2, 1) / E.var(1, ddof=1)

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
for name, f, scale in (("centred", funnel, 1), ("non-centred", funnel_nc, 3)):
    out, E, div = hmc(f, rng.standard_normal((4, 10)), 3000, 0.5, 6, rng)
    v, E, div = out[:, 500:, 0] * scale, E[:, 500:], div[:, 500:]
    print(f"{name:11} divergent {div.mean():6.2%} | E-BFMI {np.array2string(ebfmi(E), precision=2)} "
          f"(az {np.array2string(np.asarray(az.bfmi(E)), precision=2)}) | R-hat(v) {rhat_rank(v):.3f} | "
          f"bulk-ESS {ess_bulk(v):5.0f} | tail-ESS {ess_tail(v):5.0f} | sd(v) {v.std():.2f} (truth 3)")
```

```output
centred     divergent  0.45% | E-BFMI [0.08 0.04 0.1  0.12] (az [0.08 0.04 0.1  0.12]) | R-hat(v) 1.115 | bulk-ESS    28 | tail-ESS    21 | sd(v) 2.49 (truth 3)
non-centred divergent  0.00% | E-BFMI [1.03 0.98 0.97 0.97] (az [1.03 0.98 0.97 0.97]) | R-hat(v) 1.003 | bulk-ESS 40000 | tail-ESS  1871 | sd(v) 3.09 (truth 3)
```

The centred funnel trips every alarm: divergences in 0.45% of transitions, E-BFMI of 0.04 to 0.12
in all chains, $\hat R = 1.115$, ESS in the twenties, and a biased sd$(v)$ of 2.49 against 3 because
the neck is under-visited. The non-centred version of the *same model* has no divergences, E-BFMI
near 1 and $\hat R = 1.003$. Its bulk-ESS is capped at 40,000 from 10,000 draws because the
trajectory length makes successive draws of $v$ anti-correlated. These diagnostics point to a fix
in geometry (reparameterise, ch. 10), not to running longer. `az.bfmi` returns the same numbers.

### Tree-depth saturation

NUTS (ch. 6) doubles its trajectory until a U-turn, up to a maximum depth (10 in Stan and PyMC,
1023 leapfrog steps). Hitting the cap is not a bias, it is waste: the step size is set by the
narrowest direction and the U-turn by the widest, so their ratio sets the step count. The sketch
counts leapfrog steps until $p \cdot (q - q_0) < 0$ on 2-D Gaussians of growing anisotropy.

```python
def steps_to_uturn(sds, eps=0.5, rng=np.random.default_rng(6), reps=200):
    prec, out = 1 / np.asarray(sds) ** 2, []
    for _ in range(reps):
        q0 = rng.standard_normal(2) * sds; q, p = q0.copy(), rng.standard_normal(2)
        for k in range(1, 100_000):
            p = p - 0.5 * eps * prec * q; q = q + eps * p; p = p - 0.5 * eps * prec * q
            if p @ (q - q0) < 0: out.append(k); break
    return np.median(out)

for sds in ((1, 1), (1, 10), (1, 100), (1, 1000)):
    k = steps_to_uturn(sds)
    print(f"sds {str(sds):10} median steps to U-turn {k:7.0f} -> tree depth {int(np.ceil(np.log2(k + 1))):2d}"
          + ("  SATURATES depth 10" if k > 1023 else ""))
```

```output
sds (1, 1)     median steps to U-turn       6 -> tree depth  3
sds (1, 10)    median steps to U-turn      19 -> tree depth  5
sds (1, 100)   median steps to U-turn     113 -> tree depth  7
sds (1, 1000)  median steps to U-turn    1748 -> tree depth 11  SATURATES depth 10
```

Steps grow roughly in proportion to the scale ratio, and between ratios of 100 and 1000 they cross
the depth-10 cap. Saturated trees are silent: estimates stay unbiased, but each draw costs 1023
gradients and stops short of a full orbit, so ESS per gradient collapses. The fix is a warmup-adapted
mass matrix (ch. 6) or rescaled parameters, not a higher cap.

## Thinning is (mostly) pointless

Keeping every $k$-th draw cannot add information. The thinned chain's ESS is always below the full
chain's (Geyer 1992; MacEachern and Berliner 1994), and the sampling cost is unchanged, so ESS per
second of sampling only falls. Thinning pays only when *each stored draw* has its own downstream
cost: a posterior-predictive simulation per draw, memory, disk. The cost model below charges one
unit per sampler iteration and $c$ units per stored draw.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
x = ar1(0.95, 4, 50_000, rng)                           # tau about 39
print(f"{'thin k':>6} {'stored':>7} {'bulk-ESS':>9} {'ESS/iter':>9} {'ESS/cost, c=0':>14} {'ESS/cost, c=50':>15}")
for k in (1, 2, 5, 10, 40, 100):
    xs = x[:, ::k]; e = ess_bulk(xs); iters = x.size
    print(f"{k:6} {xs.size:7} {e:9.0f} {e / iters:9.4f} {e / iters:14.4f} {e / (iters + 50 * xs.size):15.5f}")
```

```output
thin k  stored  bulk-ESS  ESS/iter  ESS/cost, c=0  ESS/cost, c=50
     1  200000      4969    0.0248         0.0248         0.00049
     2  100000      4978    0.0249         0.0249         0.00096
     5   40000      4971    0.0249         0.0249         0.00226

    40    5000      3712    0.0186         0.0186         0.00825
```

With no downstream cost thinning only loses ESS: almost nothing up to $k = 10$ (neighbours are
near-duplicates when $\tau \approx 39$), a quarter at $k = 40$, 60% at $k = 100$. If each stored
draw feeds a computation 50 times as expensive as a sampler step, thinning to $k = 40$, about
$\tau$, gives 17 times the ESS per unit cost. Thin for storage and downstream compute, never "to
reduce autocorrelation": ESS already accounts for it.

## Exercise

A Gibbs sampler (ch. 5) for a bivariate Normal with correlation $\rho$ updates
$\theta_1 \mid \theta_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\rho\theta_2, 1-\rho^2)$, then $\theta_2 \mid \theta_1$

1. Run 4 chains of 2000 iterations from overdispersed starts $(\pm 5, \pm 5)$ with $\rho = 0.995$, discarding the first 500.
2. Compute rank $\hat R$, bulk-ESS, tail-ESS, and the MCSE of the mean and of the 95% quantile of $\theta_1$ with your functions, and check them against ArviZ.
3. Use $\tau = (1+\rho^2)/(1-\rho^2)$ to predict the iterations per chain needed for bulk-ESS $> 400$, run that many, and verify both thresholds.

```python
def gibbs_bvn(rho, n, starts, rng):
    t1, t2 = starts[:, 0].copy(), starts[:, 1].copy(); out = np.empty((len(t1), n))
    s = np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2)
    for t in range(n):
        t1 = rho * t2 + s * rng.standard_normal(len(t1))
        t2 = rho * t1 + s * rng.standard_normal(len(t1))
        out[:, t] = t1
    return out

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
rho, st = 0.995, np.array([[-5.0, -5.0], [-5.0, 5.0], [5.0, -5.0], [5.0, 5.0]])
x = gibbs_bvn(rho, 2000, st, rng)[:, 500:]
```
