## Inference as optimization

MCMC answers "what does the posterior look like?" by wandering around it. Variational inference
(VI) answers a different question: "which member of a simple family, say Gaussians, is closest to
the posterior?" That turns integration into optimization. You pick a family $\mathcal{Q}$ of
densities $q_\lambda(z)$ with parameters $\lambda$ (a mean and a scale, for instance), and you solve

$$
q^\star = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\big),
\qquad \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log q(z) - \log p(z \mid x)\right]
$$

Here $z$ is everything unknown (parameters and latent variables) and $x$ is the data. The price is
a bias you cannot reduce with more compute: if the posterior is not in $\mathcal{Q}$, the best
$q$ is still wrong. The reward is speed, a deterministic answer, minibatch training, and a lower
bound on $\log p(x)$ for free. This chapter follows Blei, Kucukelbir & McAuliffe (2017) and
Murphy (PML2, ch. 10).

### The ELBO, derived two ways

This derivation shows that you can minimise the KL without ever knowing the normaliser $p(x)$. First
by Jensen's inequality. For any $q$ that covers the posterior's support, $\log$ is concave, so

$$
\log p(x) = \log \int q(z)\,\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\,dz \;\ge\; \int q(z) \log \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\,dz
= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)]}_{\mathrm{ELBO}(q)}
$$

Second, exactly. Write $\log p(z \mid x) = \log p(x, z) - \log p(x)$ inside the KL:

$$
\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p(\cdot \mid x)) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] + \log p(x)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\log p(x) = \mathrm{ELBO}(q) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p(\cdot \mid x)\big)}
$$

$\log p(x)$ does not depend on $q$, so maximising the ELBO is the same as minimising the KL, and the
gap in Jensen's inequality is exactly that KL. The ELBO needs only the unnormalised joint
$p(x, z)$, the same thing MCMC needs. A second reading is useful too:
$\mathrm{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x \mid z)] - \mathrm{KL}(q(z)\,\|\,p(z))$, which is fit to the data minus distance
from the prior. The check below uses a model where everything is closed form: $x_i \sim
\mathcal{N}(\theta, 1)$, $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, 20 observations.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, special, optimize

# x_i ~ N(theta, 1), theta ~ N(0, 1): exact posterior and evidence are known
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
x = rng.normal(1.3, 1.0, 20); n = len(x)
v_post = 1 / (1 + n); m_post = v_post * x.sum()
log_px = stats.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(n), np.eye(n) + np.ones((n, n))).logpdf(x)

def elbo_gauss(m, s):
    e_loglik = np.sum(-0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * ((x - m) ** 2 + s ** 2))
    e_logprior = -0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * (m ** 2 + s ** 2)
    entropy = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e * s ** 2)
    return e_loglik + e_logprior + entropy

def kl_gauss(m1, s1, m2, s2):
    return np.log(s2 / s1) + (s1 ** 2 + (m1 - m2) ** 2) / (2 * s2 ** 2) - 0.5

print(f"exact posterior N({m_post:.4f}, {np.sqrt(v_post):.4f}^2), log p(x) = {log_px:.4f}\n")
print(f"{'q = N(m, s^2)':>22} {'ELBO':>10} {'KL(q||p)':>9} {'ELBO + KL':>10}")
for m, s in ((0.0, 1.0), (1.0, 0.5), (m_post, 0.4), (m_post + 0.1, np.sqrt(v_post)), (m_post, np.sqrt(v_post))):
    L, K = elbo_gauss(m, s), kl_gauss(m, s, m_post, np.sqrt(v_post))
    print(f"   N({m:.3f}, {s:.3f}^2) {L:10.4f} {K:9.4f} {L + K:10.4f}")
```

Every row sums to the same number, $\log p(x) = -26.98$, whatever $q$ you plug in. A bad $q$
(the prior, first row) has a low ELBO because almost 30 nats of the evidence are lost in the KL. As
$q$ approaches the exact posterior, the KL drains to zero and the ELBO rises to touch $\log p(x)$.
Being off by 0.1 in the mean, about half a posterior sd, costs 0.1 nats. That identity is the
whole of VI: climb the ELBO, and you are descending the KL you cannot compute.

## Reverse KL hunts modes, forward KL covers mass

The direction of the KL decides what kind of mistake $q$ makes. Think of a target with two
bumps and a Gaussian that can only make one. $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$ (VI's "reverse" KL) averages
$\log q/p$ under $q$. Wherever $p \approx 0$ and $q > 0$ the integrand blows up, so $q$ is forced to
be zero wherever $p$ is: it is **zero-forcing**, and in practice it sits inside one bump.
$\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$ (the "forward" KL, what maximum likelihood and expectation propagation
minimise) averages under $p$, so it punishes $q \approx 0$ where $p > 0$. It is **mass-covering**: $q$
stretches over both bumps, even if that puts mass in the gap between them.

For a Gaussian $q$ the forward-KL optimum is moment matching. Setting the gradient of
$-\mathbb{E}_p[\log q]$ to zero gives $\mu = \mathbb{E}_p[z]$ and $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}_p[z]$.
The reverse KL has no such closed form and, on a multimodal target, several local optima. Below,
both are minimised numerically on a fine grid, from several starting points.

```python
g = np.linspace(-10, 10, 40_001); h = g[1] - g[0]
log_p = np.logaddexp(np.log(0.6) + stats.norm.logpdf(g, -2.0, 0.7), np.log(0.4) + stats.norm.logpdf(g, 2.5, 0.9))
p = np.exp(log_p)

def kl_rev(par):                                   # KL(q || p) = E_q[log q - log p]
    q_log = stats.norm.logpdf(g, par[0], np.exp(par[1]))
    return np.sum(np.exp(q_log) * (q_log - log_p)) * h
def kl_fwd(par):                                   # KL(p || q) = E_p[log p - log q]
    return np.sum(p * (log_p - stats.norm.logpdf(g, par[0], np.exp(par[1])))) * h

mp = np.sum(g * p) * h; sp = np.sqrt(np.sum((g - mp) ** 2 * p) * h)
print(f"target: mixture 0.6 N(-2, 0.7^2) + 0.4 N(2.5, 0.9^2), mean {mp:.3f}, sd {sp:.3f}\n")
print(f"{'objective':>16} {'init mu':>8} {'mu':>7} {'sd':>6} {'KL(q||p)':>9} {'KL(p||q)':>9} {'q mass in gap |x-0.25|<1':>25}")
for name, f, inits in (("reverse KL(q||p)", kl_rev, (-3.0, 1.0, 3.0)), ("forward KL(p||q)", kl_fwd, (-3.0, 3.0))):
    for mu0 in inits:
        r = optimize.minimize(f, [mu0, 0.0], method="BFGS")
        mu, sd = r.x[0], np.exp(r.x[1])
        gap = stats.norm.cdf(1.25, mu, sd) - stats.norm.cdf(-0.75, mu, sd)
        print(f"{name:>16} {mu0:>8.1f} {mu:>7.3f} {sd:>6.3f} {kl_rev(r.x):>9.3f} {kl_fwd(r.x):>9.3f} {gap:>25.3f}")
p_gap = np.sum(p[np.abs(g - 0.25) < 1]) * h
print(f"\ntarget mass in the same gap: {p_gap:.3f}")
```

The reverse KL has three local optima. Started on the left, $q$ locks onto the heavier mode
(sd 0.72, almost the component's 0.7) and puts only 4% of its mass in the gap, which is about
what the target has there (5.5%). Started on the right, it finds the smaller mode, a worse optimum
(KL 0.90 against 0.51). Started in the middle, it finds a third local optimum, a wide Gaussian
(sd 2.08) that straddles both modes. All three understate the target's spread of 2.34 or misplace
its centre. The forward KL has one optimum, $\mu = -0.20$ and sd 2.34, the target's exact moments.
It spreads mass over both modes, but 33% of it sits in a gap that holds 5.5% of the target. So
neither fit is "right". Reverse KL gives you a confident, local description and underestimates
uncertainty. Forward KL gives honest moments but puts its density where there is none. Standard VI
is reverse KL, so keep the first failure mode in mind for the rest of the chapter.

## Mean-field families and coordinate ascent

The simplest useful family is a product of independent factors, one per block of unknowns:
$q(z) = \prod_{j} q_j(z_j)$. It is called **mean-field**, after the physics approximation that
replaces the interactions with other particles by their average. You give up correlations between
blocks. In return each factor has a closed-form optimal update, provided the model is
conditionally conjugate, which makes coordinate ascent possible.

### The optimal factor is the exponentiated expected log joint

This derivation shows what the best $q_j$ is when all other factors are held fixed. Write
$\mathbb{E}_{-j}$ for the expectation over every factor except $q_j$. Keep only the terms of the ELBO that
depend on $q_j$:

$$
\mathrm{ELBO}(q_j) = \int q_j(z_j)\,\mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p(x, z)]\,dz_j - \int q_j(z_j)\log q_j(z_j)\,dz_j + \text{const}
$$

Define $\log \tilde q_j(z_j) = \mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p(x, z)] + \text{const}$ (normalised). Then
the expression is $-\mathrm{KL}(q_j\,\|\,\tilde q_j) + \text{const}$, maximised by

$$
\boxed{q_j^\star(z_j) \propto \exp\left\{\mathbb{E}_{-j}\left[\log p(z_j \mid z_{-j}, x)\right]\right\}}
$$

(the full conditional and the joint differ by terms that do not involve $z_j$). It is the Gibbs
sampler's full conditional (ch. 5), with the other variables averaged out of the log density rather
than sampled. If the complete conditional is in an exponential family, $q_j^\star$ is in the same
family, with its natural parameter replaced by its expectation under $q_{-j}$. **CAVI**
(coordinate ascent VI) cycles through $j$ and applies the update. Each step maximises the ELBO
exactly in one coordinate, so the ELBO can never decrease. That makes a monotone ELBO trace the
standard unit test for a CAVI implementation.

### Example (a): a Normal with unknown mean and precision

This is the classic warm-up (Bishop PRML 10.1.3). The model is $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1/\tau)$ with the
Normal-Gamma prior $\mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 1/(\lambda_0\tau))$ and
$\tau \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)$. The exact posterior is Normal-Gamma (ch. 2), so you can grade
the approximation. Applying the boxed update to $q(\mu)\,q(\tau)$ gives

$$
q(\mu) = \mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + N\bar x}{\lambda_0 + N},\; \frac{1}{(\lambda_0 + N)\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]}\right),
\qquad
q(\tau) = \mathrm{Gamma}\left(a_0 + \tfrac{N + 1}{2},\; b_0 + \tfrac12\mathbb{E}_\mu\Big[\textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big]\right)
$$

Each factor needs one moment of the other: $q(\mu)$ needs $\mathbb{E}[\tau]$, and $q(\tau)$ needs
$\mathbb{E}[\mu]$ and $\mathrm{Var}[\mu]$. The ELBO is computed in closed form from Gamma and Gaussian
expectations ($\mathbb{E}[\log\tau] = \psi(a) - \log b$, with $\psi$ the digamma function).

```python
from scipy.special import digamma, gammaln

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
xs = rng.normal(4.0, 3.0, 12); N = len(xs); xbar = xs.mean()
mu0, lam0, a0, b0 = 0.0, 0.1, 1.0, 1.0           # mu | tau ~ N(mu0, 1/(lam0 tau)), tau ~ Gamma(a0, b0)

def elbo_ng(muN, lamN, aN, bN):
    Et, Elt = aN / bN, digamma(aN) - np.log(bN)
    Esq = np.sum((xs - muN) ** 2) + N / lamN       # E sum (x_i - mu)^2
    Esq0 = (muN - mu0) ** 2 + 1 / lamN
    e_lik = 0.5 * N * (Elt - np.log(2 * np.pi)) - 0.5 * Et * Esq
    e_mu = 0.5 * (np.log(lam0) + Elt - np.log(2 * np.pi)) - 0.5 * lam0 * Et * Esq0
    e_tau = a0 * np.log(b0) - gammaln(a0) + (a0 - 1) * Elt - b0 * Et
    h_mu = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e / lamN)
    h_tau = aN - np.log(bN) + gammaln(aN) + (1 - aN) * digamma(aN)
    return e_lik + e_mu + e_tau + h_mu + h_tau

aN, bN = a0 + (N + 1) / 2, 1.0 * (a0 + (N + 1) / 2)  # init q(tau) with E[tau] = 1 (data sd is ~3)
muN = (lam0 * mu0 + N * xbar) / (lam0 + N)           # fixed: does not depend on q(tau)
trace = []
for it in range(8):
    lamN = (lam0 + N) * aN / bN                                   # update q(mu)
    bN = b0 + 0.5 * (np.sum((xs - muN) ** 2) + N / lamN + lam0 * ((muN - mu0) ** 2 + 1 / lamN))  # update q(tau)
    trace.append(elbo_ng(muN, lamN, aN, bN))
    print(f"sweep {it}: E[tau] = {aN / bN:.5f}  sd(mu) = {lamN ** -0.5:.5f}  ELBO = {trace[-1]:.6f}")
print("ELBO increments all >= 0:", bool(np.all(np.diff(trace) >= -1e-12)))

# exact Normal-Gamma posterior
lam_n, a_n = lam0 + N, a0 + N / 2
b_n = b0 + 0.5 * np.sum((xs - xbar) ** 2) + lam0 * N * (xbar - mu0) ** 2 / (2 * lam_n)
log_ev = gammaln(a_n) - gammaln(a0) + a0 * np.log(b0) - a_n * np.log(b_n) + 0.5 * np.log(lam0 / lam_n) - 0.5 * N * np.log(2 * np.pi)
mu_marg = stats.t(2 * a_n, muN, np.sqrt(b_n / (a_n * lam_n)))
print(f"\n{'':>10} {'E[mu]':>8} {'sd(mu)':>8} {'E[tau]':>8} {'sd(tau)':>8} {'log p(x)':>9}")
print(f"{'exact':>10} {mu_marg.mean():8.4f} {mu_marg.std():8.4f} {a_n / b_n:8.4f} {np.sqrt(a_n) / b_n:8.4f} {log_ev:9.4f}")
print(f"{'CAVI':>10} {muN:8.4f} {lamN ** -0.5:8.4f} {aN / bN:8.4f} {np.sqrt(aN) / bN:8.4f} {trace[-1]:9.4f} (ELBO)")
```

CAVI starts from $\mathbb{E}[\tau] = 1$, which assumes a data sd of 1 when the real one is near 3. It converges

### Example (b): a Bayesian Gaussian mixture

This is the running example of Blei et al. (2017). Its latent cluster labels make the posterior
hard for MCMC (label switching, ch. 5). The model is
$\mu_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, $c_i \sim \mathrm{Categorical}(1/K)$ and
$x_i \mid c_i, \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{c_i}, 1)$. The mean-field family is
$q(\mu_k) = \mathcal{N}(m_k, s_k^2)$ and $q(c_i) = \mathrm{Categorical}(\varphi_i)$. The boxed update gives

$$
\varphi_{ik} \propto \exp\left\{\mathbb{E}[\mu_k]\,x_i - \tfrac12\mathbb{E}[\mu_k^2]\right\},
\qquad
m_k = \frac{\sum_i \varphi_{ik}x_i}{1/\sigma^2 + \sum_i\varphi_{ik}},
\qquad
s_k^2 = \frac{1}{1/\sigma^2 + \sum_i\varphi_{ik}}
$$

This is soft k-means with a Bayesian twist. The responsibilities $\varphi$ play EM's E-step, but
each $\mu_k$ carries a variance, and its mean is shrunk towards the prior. Below are 300 points from three
clusters (true means $-3$, $0.5$, $4$), with eight random restarts.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
K, sigma2 = 3, 10.0 ** 2                          # mu_k ~ N(0, sigma^2), c_i ~ Uniform(K), x_i ~ N(mu_{c_i}, 1)
true_mu = np.array([-3.0, 0.5, 4.0])
xg = rng.normal(true_mu[rng.integers(0, K, 300)], 1.0)

def gmm_elbo(phi, m, s2):
    e_prior = np.sum(-0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * sigma2) - (m ** 2 + s2) / (2 * sigma2))
    e_lik = np.sum(phi * (-0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * (xg[:, None] ** 2 - 2 * xg[:, None] * m + m ** 2 + s2)))
    e_c = -len(xg) * np.log(K)
    h_c = -np.sum(phi * np.log(phi + 1e-300))
    h_mu = np.sum(0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e * s2))
    return e_prior + e_lik + e_c + h_c + h_mu

def cavi_gmm(seed, sweeps=200, tol=1e-10):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    m, s2 = r.normal(0, 6, K), np.ones(K)
    trace = []
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        logits = xg[:, None] * m - 0.5 * (m ** 2 + s2)          # q(c_i) update
        phi = np.exp(logits - logits.max(1, keepdims=True)); phi /= phi.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        s2 = 1 / (1 / sigma2 + phi.sum(0))                      # q(mu_k) update
        m = s2 * (phi * xg[:, None]).sum(0)
        trace.append(gmm_elbo(phi, m, s2))
        if len(trace) > 1 and trace[-1] - trace[-2] < tol:
            break
    return np.array(trace), m, s2, phi

print(f"{'restart':>7} {'sweeps':>6} {'final ELBO':>11} {'ELBO monotone':>14} {'used':>5}  sorted E[mu_k]")
for seed in range(8):
    tr, m, s2, phi = cavi_gmm(seed)
    o = np.argsort(m)
    print(f"{seed:>7} {len(tr):>6} {tr[-1]:>11.3f} {str(bool(np.diff(tr).min() >= 0)):>14} {int((phi.sum(0) > 1).sum()):>5}  " + "  ".join(f"{np.round(v, 3) + 0.0:+.3f}" for v in m[o]))
tr, m, s2, phi = cavi_gmm(0)
print(f"\nrestart 0, first ELBO values: {np.array2string(tr[:5], precision=2)}")
print(f"posterior sd of each mu_k: {np.array2string(np.sqrt(s2[np.argsort(m)]), precision=3)}")
```

All eight runs climb the ELBO monotonically, as the theory promises. They do not all reach the
same place. Five restarts find the three clusters (ELBO $-713.5$, means within 0.16 of the truth,

collapsed to a single component in three sweeps. CAVI is coordinate ascent on a non-convex
objective. Monotone means "never worse", not "global". The standard remedy is the one used
here: run several restarts and keep the highest ELBO, which is a fair comparison because each ELBO is a
bound on the same $\log p(x)$.

## Mean-field underestimates posterior variance

Mean-field VI is systematically overconfident. You can see why with a correlated 2-D Gaussian. If
$z_1$ and $z_2$ move together, a product of independent factors has to choose a width for each, and the reverse KL,
which forbids mass where $p$ is small, picks the narrow width of the conditional
rather than the wide width of the marginal.

### Deriving the mean-field solution for a Gaussian target

This derivation shows that each mean-field factor gets the conditional variance, not the marginal one. Take $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$
with precision $\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1}$. In $\log p$, the terms involving $z_j$ are
$-\tfrac12\Lambda_{jj}z_j^2 + z_j\big(\Lambda_{jj}\mu_j - \sum_{k\ne j}\Lambda_{jk}(z_k - \mu_k)\big)$.
Averaging over $q_{-j}$ replaces $z_k$ by $m_k$, so

$$
q_j^\star(z_j) = \mathcal{N}\left(\mu_j - \Lambda_{jj}^{-1}\sum_{k \ne j}\Lambda_{jk}(m_k - \mu_k),\; \frac{1}{\Lambda_{jj}}\right)
$$

The fixed point of the means is $m = \mu$, so the means are exact. The variance is
$1/\Lambda_{jj}$, the conditional variance $\mathrm{Var}(z_j \mid z_{-j})$, which is never larger
than the marginal $\Sigma_{jj}$. For two unit-variance variables with correlation $\rho$,
$\Lambda_{11} = 1/(1-\rho^2)$, so

$$
\frac{\mathrm{sd}_q(z_1)}{\mathrm{sd}_p(z_1)} = \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}
$$

```python
mu_t = np.array([1.0, -1.0])
```

## Black-box VI: two gradient estimators

CAVI needs conditional conjugacy and a hand-derived update for each model. Black-box VI
(Ranganath et al., 2014) needs only $\log p(x, z)$. You estimate $\nabla_\lambda\mathrm{ELBO}$ with Monte Carlo
draws from $q_\lambda$ and run stochastic gradient ascent. The difficulty is that $\lambda$ appears in
the distribution you are averaging over. The two estimators below handle that in different ways.

### Score-function (REINFORCE) gradient

This derivation moves the gradient onto $\log q$ so that the draws can come from any $q$. Write
$f(z) = \log p(x, z) - \log q_\lambda(z)$. Then $\nabla_\lambda q = q\,\nabla_\lambda\log q$, and
$\mathbb{E}_q[\nabla_\lambda \log q] = \nabla_\lambda\int q = 0$, so

$$
\nabla_\lambda\mathrm{ELBO} = \int \nabla_\lambda q_\lambda(z)\,f(z)\,dz + \mathbb{E}_q[\nabla_\lambda f]
= \mathbb{E}_{q_\lambda}\big[\nabla_\lambda\log q_\lambda(z)\,f(z)\big]
$$

because $\nabla_\lambda f = -\nabla_\lambda\log q$ has mean zero. This works for any $q$ you can
sample and differentiate, including discrete ones, and needs no gradient of the model. Since
$\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log q] = 0$, you can subtract any constant $b$ from $f$ (a **baseline**, or
control variate) without adding bias.

### Reparameterization gradient

This derivation moves the randomness out of $q$ so that the gradient can go through the model. Write
$z = T_\lambda(\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon$ from a fixed distribution, for example
$z = m + s \odot \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Then the expectation is over
$\varepsilon$, which does not depend on $\lambda$:

$$
\nabla_\lambda\mathrm{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\nabla_z\log p(x, z)\big|_{z = T_\lambda(\varepsilon)}\,\nabla_\lambda T_\lambda(\varepsilon)\right] + \nabla_\lambda\mathbb{H}[q_\lambda]
$$

For the diagonal Gaussian, the entropy is $\sum_j\log s_j + \text{const}$. That gives
$\nabla_{m}= \mathbb{E}[\nabla_z\log p]$ and $\nabla_{\log s_j} = \mathbb{E}[(\nabla_z\log p)_j\,\varepsilon_j s_j] + 1$.
The estimator uses the model's gradient, so it knows which direction is uphill. The score function only
learns that from the correlation between $f$ and $\nabla\log q$. The comparison below uses the Bayesian logistic regression
of ch. 3 (40 users, intercept and two features, prior sd 2.5), with $q$ placed slightly off the
optimum, and 200,000 single-draw gradient estimates of each kind.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)                    # the running dataset of ch. 3

          f"{g_sf_b[:, j].var():>12.1f} {g_rp[:, j].var():>9.3f} {g_sf[:, j].var() / g_rp[:, j].var():>8.0f}")
```

## ADVI: automatic VI in unconstrained space

ADVI (Kucukelbir et al., 2017) is what Stan, PyMC and NumPyro run when you ask them for VI. It
does three things. First, it maps every constrained parameter to $\mathbb{R}^d$, for example
$\sigma = e^{\zeta}$ or a probability through the logit, and adds the log-Jacobian to the target:
$\log p(x, T^{-1}(\zeta)) + \log\lvert\det J_{T^{-1}}(\zeta)\rvert$. A Gaussian on $\mathbb{R}^d$
then always respects the constraints. Second, it fits either a **mean-field** Gaussian
$\mathcal{N}(m, \mathrm{diag}(s^2))$ or a **full-rank** Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(m, LL^\top)$, with $L$
lower-triangular and a positive diagonal. Third, it climbs the ELBO with reparameterization gradients and
automatic differentiation.

The logistic regression coefficients are already unconstrained, so the Jacobian is 1 here. The
implementation below parameterises the diagonal of $L$ through its log and uses 16 draws per step.
It runs Adam with a decaying step size and averages the iterates over the second half of the run
(Polyak averaging), which removes most of the optimiser noise. The reference is the 1M-draw
importance sampler of ch. 3.

```python
import torch
torch.set_num_threads(1)

# reference posterior: SNIS with a Student-t proposal around the Laplace fit (ch. 3), 1M draws
t_ref = stats.multivariate_t(b_map, 1.5 * cov_lap, df=4)
th_ref = t_ref.rvs(1_000_000, random_state=np.random.default_rng(1))
lw_ref = log_joint(th_ref) - t_ref.logpdf(th_ref)
w_ref = np.exp(lw_ref - lw_ref.max()); w_ref /= w_ref.sum()
m_ref = w_ref @ th_ref
cov_ref = (th_ref - m_ref).T @ ((th_ref - m_ref) * w_ref[:, None])
s_ref = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_ref))
log_Z_ref = lw_ref.max() + np.log(np.mean(np.exp(lw_ref - lw_ref.max())))

Xt, yt = torch.tensor(X), torch.tensor(y)
def log_joint_t(B, Xb=Xt, yb=yt, scale=1.0):        # B: (S, d); scale = n / batch for minibatches
    eta = B @ Xb.T
    return scale * (yb * eta - torch.nn.functional.softplus(eta)).sum(-1) - 0.5 * (B ** 2).sum(-1) / prior_sd ** 2

def advi(full_rank, steps=4000, S=16, lr=0.05, decay=500, init_log_sd=0.0, seed=0, lj=log_joint_t, dim=d):
    torch.manual_seed(seed)
    mu = torch.zeros(dim, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
    L_raw = (init_log_sd * torch.eye(dim, dtype=torch.float64)).requires_grad_(True)   # diag holds log sd
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([mu, L_raw], lr=lr)
    sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(opt, lambda t: 1 / (1 + t / decay))
    mask = torch.tril(torch.ones(dim, dim, dtype=torch.float64), -1) if full_rank else torch.zeros(dim, dim, dtype=torch.float64)
    avg_mu, avg_C = torch.zeros(dim, dtype=torch.float64), torch.zeros(dim, dim, dtype=torch.float64)
    for t in range(steps):
        L = torch.diag(torch.exp(torch.diagonal(L_raw))) + L_raw * mask
        eps = torch.randn(S, dim, dtype=torch.float64)
        Z = mu + eps @ L.T                                  # reparameterised draws
        elbo = lj(Z).mean() + torch.log(torch.diagonal(L)).sum()   # + entropy (up to a constant)
        opt.zero_grad(); (-elbo).backward(); opt.step(); sched.step()
        if t >= steps // 2:                                 # Polyak-average the second half
            with torch.no_grad():
                L = torch.diag(torch.exp(torch.diagonal(L_raw))) + L_raw * mask
                avg_mu, avg_C = avg_mu + mu / (steps - steps // 2), avg_C + L @ L.T / (steps - steps // 2)
    return avg_mu.numpy(), avg_C.numpy()

fits = {"Laplace": (b_map, cov_lap), "ADVI mean-field": advi(False), "ADVI full-rank": advi(True)}
print(f"{'':>16} {'means':>27} {'sds':>23} {'corr(b0,b1)':>12}")
print(f"{'reference':>16} {np.array2string(m_ref, precision=3):>27} {np.array2string(s_ref, precision=3):>23} "
      f"{cov_ref[0, 1] / (s_ref[0] * s_ref[1]):>12.3f}")
for name, (mq, Cq) in fits.items():
    sq = np.sqrt(np.diag(Cq))
    print(f"{name:>16} {np.array2string(mq, precision=3):>27} {np.array2string(sq, precision=3):>23} "
          f"{Cq[0, 1] / (sq[0] * sq[1]):>12.3f}")
print(f"\nmax |mean error| / ref sd and sd ratio range:")
for name, (mq, Cq) in fits.items():
    r = np.sqrt(np.diag(Cq)) / s_ref
    print(f"{name:>16}: {np.max(np.abs(mq - m_ref) / s_ref):.3f} | {r.min():.3f} to {r.max():.3f}")
```

## Stochastic VI with minibatches

The likelihood is a sum over data points, so each step can look at a random minibatch of $B$ out of $n$
points and scale it up:

$$
\widehat{\log p}(x, z) = \frac{n}{B}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}}\log p(x_i \mid z) + \log p(z),
\qquad \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\widehat{\log p}\right] = \log p(x, z)
$$

The estimate is unbiased, so its gradient is too, and Robbins-Monro step sizes
($\sum_t\rho_t = \infty$, $\sum_t\rho_t^2 < \infty$) still give convergence (Hoffman et al., 2013, who
also use natural gradients for conjugate models). Each step costs $B$ likelihood evaluations
instead of $n$. That is what lets VI scale to datasets MCMC cannot touch. The factor $n/B$ is easy to forget, and
forgetting it silently fits the posterior you would get from only $B$ data points. The comparison
below uses 20,000 points and 5 coefficients with mean-field ADVI. For a near-Gaussian posterior,
the exact mean-field sds are $1/\sqrt{\Lambda_{jj}}$ (see above).

```python
rb = np.random.default_rng(8)
```

## Is the approximation any good? PSIS $\hat k$ and the importance-weighted ELBO

The ELBO tells you which of two $q$'s is better. It does not tell you whether either is good enough,
because $\log p(x)$ is unknown. Yao et al. (2018) proposed using $q$ as an importance-sampling
proposal and asking how heavy-tailed the weights $w = p(x, z)/q(z)$ are. That is the PSIS
diagnostic of ch. 3, which ch. 9 applies to LOO. A Pareto $\hat k < 0.5$ means $q$ is close enough
that importance sampling from it is reliable. In $0.5 < \hat k < 0.7$ it is usable with smoothing, and $\hat k > 0.7$
means the approximation should not be trusted and cannot be repaired by reweighting. The same weights
give two more things. Self-normalised IS from $q$ corrects its moments. The **importance-weighted
ELBO** (Burda et al., 2016)

$$
\mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}_{z_{1:K} \sim q}\left[\log\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{p(x, z_k)}{q(z_k)}\right],
\qquad \mathcal{L}_1 = \mathrm{ELBO} \le \mathcal{L}_K \le \mathcal{L}_{K+1} \le \log p(x)
$$

tightens towards $\log p(x)$ as $K$ grows. It is a lower bound for every $K$ (Jensen again, now on the
average of $K$ weights). The estimator below follows the Zhang-Stephens fit that the reference
PSIS implementation uses, applied to 100,000 draws from each fit of the last section.

```python
@np.errstate(over="ignore")
def pareto_khat(log_w):
    """Generalised-Pareto shape of the largest weights, Zhang-Stephens estimator as in PSIS."""
    w = np.sort(np.exp(log_w - log_w.max()))
    M = int(np.ceil(min(0.2 * len(w), 3 * np.sqrt(len(w)))))
    x = w[-M:] - w[-M - 1]
    nm = 30 + int(np.sqrt(M))
    bs = 1 / x[-1] + (1 - np.sqrt(nm / (np.arange(1, nm + 1) - 0.5))) / (3 * x[int(M / 4 + 0.5) - 1])
    ks = -np.mean(np.log1p(-bs[:, None] * x), axis=1)
    L = M * (np.log(bs / ks) + ks - 1)
    b = np.sum(bs / np.sum(np.exp(L[None, :] - L[:, None]), axis=1))
    k = np.mean(np.log1p(-b * x))
    return (M * k + 5) / (M + 10)                   # shrink towards 0.5 for small tails

rq = np.random.default_rng(9)
print(f"{'q':>16} {'khat':>6} {'ESS/S':>6} {'IS-corrected sds':>22} {'IW-ELBO K=1':>12} {'K=10':>8} {'K=100':>8} {'K=1000':>8}")
for name, (mq, Cq) in fits.items():

    w = np.exp(lw - lw.max()); w /= w.sum()
    mc = w @ Zq; sc = np.sqrt(w @ (Zq - mc) ** 2)
    iw = [np.mean(special.logsumexp(lw.reshape(-1, K), axis=1) - np.log(K)) for K in (1, 10, 100, 1000)]
    print(f"{name:>16} {pareto_khat(lw):>6.2f} {1 / np.sum(w ** 2) / len(w):>6.2f} {np.array2string(sc, precision=3):>22} "
          + " ".join(f"{v:>8.3f}" for v in iw))
print(f"{'reference':>16} {'':>6} {'':>6} {np.array2string(s_ref, precision=3):>22}  log Z = {log_Z_ref:.3f}")
```

```output
       reference                  [0.5   0.771 0.548]  log Z = -21.207

bimodal target, q fitted to the left mode: khat = 2.33, draws beyond x = 0.5: 3 of 10,000
```

The $K = 1$ column is the ELBO of each fit. Full-rank ADVI has the highest ELBO, $-21.248$, so its KL to the
posterior is only 0.04 nats, against 0.18 for mean-field. As $K$ grows, every bound climbs to the
reference $\log Z = -21.207$ (Laplace and full-rank reach it at $K = 100$). Importance reweighting
then repairs the moments. From full-rank and Laplace the corrected sds match the reference to three
digits. From mean-field they move most of the way (0.739 against 0.771 for the slope),
held back by an ESS of only 35%. Here $\hat k$ is the
reverse KL's light-tail fingerprint. It is near 0.5 for all three fits, because each Gaussian is
slightly narrower than the skewed posterior in some direction. The estimate is noisy at this
boundary, so treat 0.45 versus 0.55 as the same verdict: usable, with smoothing. The bimodal case is the
one that matters. A $q$ fitted to one mode draws only a handful of points near the second, but their weights are
enormous, and $\hat k = 2.33$ flags the fit as useless for the global picture. If a $q$ never visits a
mode at all, no weight-based diagnostic can see it. So $\hat k$ is necessary, not sufficient, and
multimodality is still best probed with multiple restarts, as with the mixture.

## When to prefer VI and when to prefer MCMC

MCMC is asymptotically exact, and its error falls as you run it longer, with diagnostics (ch. 7)
that tell you when it has failed. VI is biased by its family, and more compute does not remove the bias.
In exchange it is fast, deterministic once converged, and works with minibatches. The decision is about
which error you can afford.

| Situation | Prefer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Final inference for a moderate model, tail probabilities or intervals matter | MCMC (NUTS, ch. 6) | VI underestimates variance and distorts tails |
| Huge $n$ (millions of rows) or streaming data | SVI | minibatch gradients, cost per step $O(B)$ |
| Many latent variables, one per data point (topic models, VAEs) | amortised VI (Generative Models) | an encoder predicts each $q(z_i \mid x_i)$ |
| Model iteration, initialising or tuning MCMC | ADVI or Pathfinder | minutes instead of hours, decent means |
| Multimodal or strongly correlated posteriors | MCMC with several chains, or full-rank VI plus $\hat k$ | mean-field misses correlation, any VI can miss modes |
| Bayesian neural networks (ch. 13) | VI, ensembles, Laplace | MCMC is rarely practical at that scale |

In practice the two are combined: VI to get close quickly, $\hat k$ to decide whether that is enough,
and MCMC (or importance sampling with $q$ as the proposal) when it is not.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
counts = np.array([0, 1, 0, 2, 0, 0, 1])          # Poisson counts; try [0, 0, 0] or 50 counts near 4
prior_mu, prior_sd = 0.0, 2.0                      # z = log rate ~ N(0, 2^2)

def grad_log_joint(z):                             # d/dz [sum(y z - e^z) + log N(z; 0, 2^2)]
    return counts.sum() - len(counts) * np.exp(z) - (z - prior_mu) / prior_sd ** 2

m, log_s, S, steps = 0.0, 0.0, 8, 5000             # q(z) = N(m, s^2), reparameterised z = m + s * eps
avg = np.zeros(2)
for t in range(steps):
    eps = rng.standard_normal(S); s = np.exp(log_s)
    g = grad_log_joint(m + s * eps)
    lr = 0.05 / (1 + t / 500)
    m += lr * g.mean()
    log_s += lr * (np.mean(g * eps * s) + 1.0)     # +1 is the entropy gradient
    if t >= steps // 2:
        avg += np.array([m, np.exp(log_s)]) / (steps - steps // 2)

grid = np.linspace(-12, 6, 200_001)
lp = counts.sum() * grid - len(counts) * np.exp(grid) + stats.norm.logpdf(grid, prior_mu, prior_sd)
w = np.exp(lp - lp.max()); w /= w.sum()
gm = w @ grid; gs = np.sqrt(w @ (grid - gm) ** 2)
print(f"log-rate z:   VI mean {avg[0]:+.3f} sd {avg[1]:.3f} | exact mean {gm:+.3f} sd {gs:.3f}")
lam_vi = stats.lognorm(avg[1], scale=np.exp(avg[0]))
print(f"rate e^z:     VI mean {lam_vi.mean():.3f} | exact mean {w @ np.exp(grid):.3f}")
print(f"P(rate > 1):  VI {lam_vi.sf(1.0):.3f} | exact {w[grid > 0].sum():.3f}")
```

```output
log-rate z:   VI mean -0.634 sd 0.472 | exact mean -0.637 sd 0.501
rate e^z:     VI mean 0.593 | exact mean 0.594
P(rate > 1):  VI 0.089 | exact 0.089
```

The posterior here is one-dimensional and skewed (seven small counts, a log-rate parameter). The
reparameterised Gaussian in log space gets the mean right to 0.003, but its sd is 6% too small
(0.472 against 0.501). That is the reverse KL trimming the long left tail again. Moments of the
rate itself and $P(\text{rate} > 1)$ still come out right, because they depend mostly on the
centre. Try `counts = np.array([0, 0, 0])`: the posterior then piles up against very low rates,
and the gap in the sd grows. Then try 50 counts near 4, where the posterior becomes almost Gaussian and VI is
almost exact. That is Bernstein-von Mises (ch. 3) in action.

## Exercise

Bayesian linear regression with unknown noise has a conjugate Normal-Inverse-Gamma posterior, so you can

1. Derive the CAVI updates for $q(w)\,q(\tau)$ and implement them.

   scale $(b_n/a_n)P^{-1}$, where $P = X^\top X + \alpha I$.
3. Mean-field broke the correlations in the logistic example. Explain why this factorisation does

```python
rr = np.random.default_rng(12)
nr, dr, alpha, a0r, b0r = 30, 4, 1.0, 2.0, 2.0
Xr = rr.normal(size=(nr, dr)); Xr[:, 1] = Xr[:, 0] + 0.3 * rr.normal(size=nr)    # two correlated features
yr = Xr @ np.array([1.0, -0.5, 0.0, 2.0]) + rr.normal(0, 1.5, nr)

P = Xr.T @ Xr + alpha * np.eye(dr)                 # w | tau ~ N(0, (alpha tau)^-1 I), tau ~ Gamma(a0, b0)
Pinv = np.linalg.inv(P)
m_w = Pinv @ Xr.T @ yr                             # same mean under exact and mean-field
a_q, E_tau = a0r + (nr + dr) / 2, 1.0
for sweep in range(50):
    S_w = Pinv / E_tau                                                         # q(w) = N(m_w, S_w)
    E_quad = np.sum((yr - Xr @ m_w) ** 2) + alpha * m_w @ m_w + np.trace(P @ S_w)
    b_q = b0r + 0.5 * E_quad                                                   # q(tau) = Gamma(a_q, b_q)
    E_tau = a_q / b_q

a_n = a0r + nr / 2                                 # exact: w is multivariate t with 2 a_n dof
b_n = b0r + 0.5 * (yr @ yr - m_w @ P @ m_w)
sd_exact = np.sqrt(np.diag(Pinv) * b_n / a_n * (2 * a_n) / (2 * a_n - 2))
print(f"E[tau]: exact {a_n / b_n:.4f} | CAVI {E_tau:.4f}")
print(f"sd(w) exact {np.array2string(sd_exact, precision=4)}")
print(f"sd(w) CAVI  {np.array2string(np.sqrt(np.diag(S_w)), precision=4)}")
print(f"ratio       {np.array2string(np.sqrt(np.diag(S_w)) / sd_exact, precision=4)}")
print(f"corr(w0, w1): exact {Pinv[0, 1] / np.sqrt(Pinv[0, 0] * Pinv[1, 1]):+.4f} | CAVI {S_w[0, 1] / np.sqrt(S_w[0, 0] * S_w[1, 1]):+.4f}")
```

```output
E[tau]: exact 0.5436 | CAVI 0.5436
sd(w) exact [0.6767 0.652  0.3391 0.235 ]
sd(w) CAVI  [0.6565 0.6325 0.3289 0.228 ]
ratio       [0.9701 0.9701 0.9701 0.9701]
corr(w0, w1): exact -0.9110 | CAVI -0.9110
```

Every coefficient's sd is too small by the same factor, 0.9701, while the correlation $-0.911$
between the collinear features survives intact. The factorisation separates $w$ from $\tau$, but
it keeps $w$ as one block, and the update $q(w) = \mathcal{N}(P^{-1}X^\top y,\; P^{-1}/\mathbb{E}[\tau])$ inherits the full
shape of $P^{-1}$. The only thing lost is the coupling between scale and noise level. The exact
marginal is a t whose covariance is $P^{-1}\,b_n/(a_n - 1)$. At the fixed point,
$\mathbb{E}_q[\tau]$ equals the exact $a_n/b_n$: the extra $d/2$ in $a_q$ is cancelled by the
$\mathrm{tr}(P S_w)/2 = d/(2\mathbb{E}[\tau])$ term in $b_q$. So CAVI's covariance is $P^{-1}\,b_n/a_n$, and the ratio of sds is
$\sqrt{(a_n - 1)/a_n} = \sqrt{16/17} = 0.9701$: the Gaussian misses the t's heavier tails. The moral
is to choose the factorisation along the weak dependencies of the posterior and keep the
strongly coupled parameters in one block.
