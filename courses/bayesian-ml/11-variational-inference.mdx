## Inference as optimization

MCMC describes the posterior by wandering around it. Variational inference (VI) asks which member
of a simple family, say Gaussians, is closest to it, which turns integration into optimization. Pick
densities $q_\lambda(z)$ with parameters $\lambda$ (a mean and a scale, say), where $z$ is every
unknown and $x$ the data, and solve

$$
q^\star = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\big),
\qquad \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log q(z) - \log p(z \mid x)\right]
$$

The price is a bias compute cannot remove (if the posterior is not in $\mathcal{Q}$, $q^\star$ is
wrong). The reward is speed, minibatches, and a bound on $\log p(x)$ (Blei, Kucukelbir & McAuliffe,
2017; Murphy PML2, ch. 10).

### The ELBO, derived two ways

This shows that you can minimise the KL without knowing the normaliser $p(x)$. By Jensen's
inequality ($\log$ is concave):

$$
\log p(x) = \log \int q(z)\,\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\,dz \;\ge\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)]}_{\mathrm{ELBO}(q)}
$$

Exactly, by writing $\log p(z \mid x) = \log p(x, z) - \log p(x)$ inside the KL:

$$
\boxed{\log p(x) = \mathrm{ELBO}(q) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p(\cdot \mid x)\big)}
$$

$\log p(x)$ does not depend on $q$, so maximising the ELBO minimises the KL, using only the
unnormalised joint. Rearranged, ELBO $= \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x \mid z)] - \mathrm{KL}(q(z)\,\|\,p(z))$:
data fit minus distance from the prior. Check it on $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1)$,
$\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, special, optimize

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)                   # x_i ~ N(theta, 1), theta ~ N(0, 1)
x = rng.normal(1.3, 1.0, 20); n = len(x)
v_post = 1 / (1 + n); m_post = v_post * x.sum(); s_post = np.sqrt(v_post)
log_px = stats.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(n), np.eye(n) + np.ones((n, n))).logpdf(x)
def elbo_gauss(m, s):                             # E_q log p(x|theta) + E_q log p(theta) + entropy
    return (np.sum(-0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * ((x - m) ** 2 + s ** 2))
            - 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * (m ** 2 + s ** 2) + 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e * s ** 2))
kl_gauss = lambda m1, s1, m2, s2: np.log(s2 / s1) + (s1 ** 2 + (m1 - m2) ** 2) / (2 * s2 ** 2) - 0.5
print(f"exact posterior N({m_post:.4f}, {s_post:.4f}^2), log p(x) = {log_px:.4f}")
print(f"{'q = N(m, s^2)':>18} {'ELBO':>9} {'KL(q||p)':>9} {'ELBO + KL':>10}")
for m, s in ((0.0, 1.0), (1.0, 0.5), (m_post, 0.4), (m_post + 0.1, s_post), (m_post, s_post)):
    L, K = elbo_gauss(m, s), kl_gauss(m, s, m_post, s_post)
    print(f"N({m:.3f}, {s:.3f}^2) {L:9.4f} {K:9.4f} {L + K:10.4f}")
```

climb the ELBO, and you descend a KL you cannot compute.

## Reverse KL hunts modes, forward KL covers mass

The direction of the KL decides what mistake $q$ makes. Picture a target with two bumps and a
Gaussian that can make only one. VI's **reverse** KL, $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$, averages $\log q/p$
under $q$. It explodes wherever $q > 0$ and $p \approx 0$, so $q$ is **zero-forcing** and sits
inside one bump. The **forward** KL, $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$ (maximum likelihood, expectation
propagation), averages under $p$ and punishes $q \approx 0$ where $p > 0$. It is
**mass-covering**. For a Gaussian $q$, setting the gradient of $-\mathbb{E}_p[\log q]$ to zero gives
moment matching: $\mu = \mathbb{E}_p[z]$, $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}_p[z]$. Below, both are minimised
numerically on a grid from several starts.

```python
g = np.linspace(-10, 10, 40_001); h = g[1] - g[0]
log_p = np.logaddexp(np.log(0.6) + stats.norm.logpdf(g, -2.0, 0.7), np.log(0.4) + stats.norm.logpdf(g, 2.5, 0.9))
p = np.exp(log_p)
lq = lambda par: stats.norm.logpdf(g, par[0], np.exp(par[1]))
kl_rev = lambda par: np.sum(np.exp(lq(par)) * (lq(par) - log_p)) * h      # E_q[log q - log p]
kl_fwd = lambda par: np.sum(p * (log_p - lq(par))) * h                    # E_p[log p - log q]
in_gap = lambda mu, sd: stats.norm.cdf(1.25, mu, sd) - stats.norm.cdf(-0.75, mu, sd)
mp = np.sum(g * p) * h
print(f"target mean {mp:.3f}, sd {np.sqrt(np.sum((g - mp) ** 2 * p) * h):.3f}, "
      f"mass in the gap -0.75 < x < 1.25: {np.sum(p[np.abs(g - 0.25) < 1]) * h:.3f}")
print(f"{'objective':>9} {'init':>5} {'mu':>7} {'sd':>6} {'KL(q||p)':>9} {'KL(p||q)':>9} {'q mass in gap':>14}")
for name, f, inits in (("reverse", kl_rev, (-3.0, 1.0, 3.0)), ("forward", kl_fwd, (3.0,))):
    for mu0 in inits:
        r = optimize.minimize(f, [mu0, 0.0], method="BFGS")
        print(f"{name:>9} {mu0:>5.1f} {r.x[0]:>7.3f} {np.exp(r.x[1]):>6.3f} {kl_rev(r.x):>9.3f} "
              f"{kl_fwd(r.x):>9.3f} {in_gap(r.x[0], np.exp(r.x[1])):>14.3f}")
```

## Mean-field families and coordinate ascent

The simplest family is a product of independent factors, $q(z) = \prod_j q_j(z_j)$: **mean-field**,
after the physics trick of averaging interactions. You lose cross-block correlations and, in
conditionally conjugate models, gain closed-form updates.

### The optimal factor is the exponentiated expected log joint

This shows what the best $q_j$ is with the other factors fixed. Write $\mathbb{E}_{-j}$ for the
expectation over all factors but $q_j$, and $\tilde q_j \propto e^{\mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p(x, z)]}$. Then

$$
\int q_j(z_j)\,\mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p(x, z)]\,dz_j - \int q_j\log q_j\,dz_j
= -\mathrm{KL}\big(q_j\,\|\,\tilde q_j\big) + \text{const}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{q_j^\star(z_j) \propto \exp\left\{\mathbb{E}_{-j}\left[\log p(z_j \mid z_{-j}, x)\right]\right\}}
$$

It is the Gibbs full conditional (ch. 5) with the other variables averaged in the log rather than
sampled; in an exponential family, $q_j^\star$ stays in it with the natural parameter replaced by its
expectation. **CAVI** cycles through $j$; each update is an exact coordinate maximum, so the ELBO
never decreases, and a monotone trace is the standard unit test.

### Example (a): a Normal with unknown mean and precision

Take $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1/\tau)$ with $\mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 1/(\lambda_0\tau))$
and $\tau \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)$ (Bishop PRML 10.1.3). The exact posterior is
Normal-Gamma (ch. 2), so you can grade the fit. The boxed update gives

$$
q(\mu) = \mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + N\bar x}{\lambda_0 + N},\; \frac{1}{(\lambda_0 + N)\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]}\right),
\quad
q(\tau) = \mathrm{Gamma}\left(a_0 + \tfrac{N + 1}{2},\; b_0 + \tfrac12\mathbb{E}_\mu\Big[\textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big]\right)
$$

Each factor needs moments of the other. The ELBO is closed form via
$\mathbb{E}[\log\tau] = \psi(a) - \log b$ ($\psi$ is the digamma function).

```python
from scipy.special import digamma, gammaln
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
xs = rng.normal(4.0, 3.0, 12); N = len(xs); xbar = xs.mean()
mu0, lam0, a0, b0 = 0.0, 0.1, 1.0, 1.0           # mu | tau ~ N(mu0, 1/(lam0 tau)), tau ~ Gamma(a0, b0)
def elbo_ng(muN, lamN, aN, bN):
    Et, Elt = aN / bN, digamma(aN) - np.log(bN)
    e_lik = 0.5 * N * (Elt - np.log(2 * np.pi)) - 0.5 * Et * (np.sum((xs - muN) ** 2) + N / lamN)
    e_mu = 0.5 * (np.log(lam0) + Elt - np.log(2 * np.pi)) - 0.5 * lam0 * Et * ((muN - mu0) ** 2 + 1 / lamN)
    e_tau = a0 * np.log(b0) - gammaln(a0) + (a0 - 1) * Elt - b0 * Et
    ent = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e / lamN) + aN - np.log(bN) + gammaln(aN) + (1 - aN) * digamma(aN)
    return e_lik + e_mu + e_tau + ent
muN, aN = (lam0 * mu0 + N * xbar) / (lam0 + N), a0 + (N + 1) / 2   # neither depends on the other factor
bN, trace = aN, []                                                  # init E[tau] = 1; the data sd is ~3
for it in range(4):
    lamN = (lam0 + N) * aN / bN                                                              # q(mu)
    bN = b0 + 0.5 * (np.sum((xs - muN) ** 2) + N / lamN + lam0 * ((muN - mu0) ** 2 + 1 / lamN))  # q(tau)
    trace.append(elbo_ng(muN, lamN, aN, bN))
print("ELBO by sweep:", np.array2string(np.array(trace), precision=6), "| never decreases:", bool(np.all(np.diff(trace) >= -1e-12)))
lam_n, a_n = lam0 + N, a0 + N / 2                  # exact Normal-Gamma posterior (ch. 2)
b_n = b0 + 0.5 * np.sum((xs - xbar) ** 2) + lam0 * N * (xbar - mu0) ** 2 / (2 * lam_n)
log_ev = gammaln(a_n) - gammaln(a0) + a0 * np.log(b0) - a_n * np.log(b_n) + 0.5 * np.log(lam0 / lam_n) - 0.5 * N * np.log(2 * np.pi)
mu_marg = stats.t(2 * a_n, muN, np.sqrt(b_n / (a_n * lam_n)))
print(f"exact: E[mu] {mu_marg.mean():.4f} sd(mu) {mu_marg.std():.4f} E[tau] {a_n / b_n:.4f} log p(x) {log_ev:.4f}")
print(f"CAVI:  E[mu] {muN:.4f} sd(mu) {lamN ** -0.5:.4f} E[tau] {aN / bN:.4f} ELBO     {trace[-1]:.4f}")
```

```output
ELBO by sweep: [-34.05383  -33.557098 -33.556329 -33.556326] | never decreases: True
exact: E[mu] 3.8125 sd(mu) 0.7889 E[tau] 0.1549 log p(x) -33.5210
CAVI:  E[mu] 3.8125 sd(mu) 0.7303 E[tau] 0.1549 ELBO     -33.5563
```

From $\mathbb{E}[\tau] = 1$ (data sd 1 when the truth is near 3), CAVI converges in four sweeps,
rising every time. The mean and $\mathbb{E}[\tau]$ are exact; the ELBO is 0.035 nats (the KL) below
$\log p(x)$, and the error lives in the uncertainty: sd($\mu$) 0.730 against 0.789, 7% small. The
exact posterior couples $\mu$ and $\tau$ (low precision, uncertain mean: a Student-t marginal), which
a factorised $q$ cannot express.

### Example (b): a Bayesian Gaussian mixture

This is Blei et al.'s running example: $\mu_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$,
$c_i \sim \mathrm{Categorical}(1/K)$, $x_i \mid c_i, \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{c_i}, 1)$, with
$q(\mu_k) = \mathcal{N}(m_k, s_k^2)$ and $q(c_i) = \mathrm{Categorical}(\varphi_i)$. The boxed update gives

$$
\varphi_{ik} \propto \exp\left\{\mathbb{E}[\mu_k]\,x_i - \tfrac12\mathbb{E}[\mu_k^2]\right\},
\qquad
m_k = \frac{\sum_i \varphi_{ik}x_i}{1/\sigma^2 + \sum_i\varphi_{ik}},
\qquad
s_k^2 = \frac{1}{1/\sigma^2 + \sum_i\varphi_{ik}}
$$

Soft k-means with uncertainty: each $\mu_k$ has a variance and shrinks to the prior. Data: 300
points, clusters at $-3$, $0.5$, $4$.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
K, sigma2 = 3, 10.0 ** 2                          # mu_k ~ N(0, sigma^2), c_i ~ Uniform(K), x_i ~ N(mu_{c_i}, 1)
xg = rng.normal(np.array([-3.0, 0.5, 4.0])[rng.integers(0, K, 300)], 1.0)
def gmm_elbo(phi, m, s2):
    e_mu = np.sum(-0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * sigma2) - (m ** 2 + s2) / (2 * sigma2))
    e_x = np.sum(phi * (-0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * (xg[:, None] ** 2 - 2 * xg[:, None] * m + m ** 2 + s2)))
    ent = -np.sum(phi * np.log(phi + 1e-300)) + np.sum(0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e * s2))
    return e_mu + e_x - len(xg) * np.log(K) + ent
def cavi_gmm(seed, sweeps=200):
    m, s2, trace = np.random.default_rng(seed).normal(0, 6, K), np.ones(K), []
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        logits = xg[:, None] * m - 0.5 * (m ** 2 + s2)                     # q(c_i)
        phi = np.exp(logits - logits.max(1, keepdims=True)); phi /= phi.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        s2 = 1 / (1 / sigma2 + phi.sum(0)); m = s2 * (phi * xg[:, None]).sum(0)   # q(mu_k)
        trace.append(gmm_elbo(phi, m, s2))
        if len(trace) > 1 and trace[-1] - trace[-2] < 1e-10:
            break
    return np.array(trace), m, s2, phi
print(f"{'restart':>7} {'sweeps':>6} {'final ELBO':>11} {'monotone':>9} {'used':>5}  sorted E[mu_k]")
for seed in range(8):
    tr, m, s2, phi = cavi_gmm(seed)
    print(f"{seed:>7} {len(tr):>6} {tr[-1]:>11.3f} {str(bool(np.diff(tr).min() >= 0)):>9} {int((phi.sum(0) > 1).sum()):>5}  "
          + "  ".join(f"{np.round(v, 3) + 0.0:+.3f}" for v in np.sort(m)))
```

## Mean-field underestimates posterior variance

Mean-field VI is systematically overconfident. If $z_1$ and $z_2$ move together, independent
factors must each pick one width, and the reverse KL (no mass where $p$ is small) picks the narrow
conditional width, not the wide marginal one.

### Deriving the mean-field solution for a Gaussian target

Let $p = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$ with
precision $\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1}$. The terms of $\log p$ in $z_j$ are
$-\tfrac12\Lambda_{jj}z_j^2 + z_j\big(\Lambda_{jj}\mu_j - \sum_{k\ne j}\Lambda_{jk}(z_k - \mu_k)\big)$.
Averaging over $q_{-j}$ replaces $z_k$ by $m_k$:

$$
q_j^\star = \mathcal{N}\left(\mu_j - \Lambda_{jj}^{-1}\textstyle\sum_{k \ne j}\Lambda_{jk}(m_k - \mu_k),\; \Lambda_{jj}^{-1}\right),
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{sd}_q(z_1)}{\mathrm{sd}_p(z_1)} = \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\ \text{ (two unit variances, correlation } \rho)
$$

The means converge to $\mu$; the variance $1/\Lambda_{jj} = \mathrm{Var}(z_j \mid z_{-j}) \le \Sigma_{jj}$.

```python
mu_t = np.array([1.0, -1.0])
print(f"{'rho':>5} {'sweeps':>7} {'MF mean':>10} {'MF sd (true 1)':>15} {'sqrt(1-rho^2)':>14} {'KL(q||p)':>9}")
for rho in (0.0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99):
    Lam = np.linalg.inv(np.array([[1.0, rho], [rho, 1.0]])); m = np.array([4.0, 4.0])
    for sweep in range(1, 5000):
        m_old = m.copy()
        for j, k in ((0, 1), (1, 0)):              # m_j = mu_j - Lam_jj^{-1} Lam_jk (m_k - mu_k)
            m[j] = mu_t[j] - Lam[j, k] * (m[k] - mu_t[k]) / Lam[j, j]
        if np.abs(m - m_old).max() < 1e-8:
            break
    v = 1 / np.diag(Lam)
    kl = 0.5 * (np.sum(np.diag(Lam) * v) - 2 + np.log((1 - rho ** 2) / np.prod(v)))
    print(f"{rho:>5} {sweep:>7} {np.array2string(m, precision=4):>10} {np.sqrt(v[0]):>15.3f} {np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2):>14.3f} {kl:>9.3f}")
```

```output
  0.0       2  [ 1. -1.]           1.000          1.000     0.000
```

Means exact, sds exactly $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$: 2.3 times too narrow at $\rho = 0.9$, seven at $0.99$,

## Black-box VI: two gradient estimators

CAVI needs conjugacy and hand-derived updates. Black-box VI (Ranganath et al., 2014) needs only
$\log p(x, z)$: estimate $\nabla_\lambda\mathrm{ELBO}$ from draws of $q_\lambda$ and ascend. The catch:
$\lambda$ sits inside the distribution you average over.

### Score-function and reparameterization gradients

The **score function** (REINFORCE) moves the gradient onto $\log q$. With $f(z) = \log p(x, z) - \log q_\lambda(z)$,
$\nabla_\lambda q = q\,\nabla_\lambda\log q$ and $\mathbb{E}_q[\nabla_\lambda\log q] = 0$:

$$
\nabla_\lambda\mathrm{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\lambda}\big[\nabla_\lambda\log q_\lambda(z)\,f(z)\big]
$$

It works for any $q$, discrete included, without the model's gradient, and any constant
**baseline** can be subtracted from $f$ without bias. **Reparameterization** instead moves the
randomness out of $q$. Write $z = T_\lambda(\varepsilon) = m + s \odot \varepsilon$
with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, so the expectation no longer depends on $\lambda$:

$$
\nabla_\lambda\mathrm{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\nabla_z\log p(x, z)\big|_{z = T_\lambda(\varepsilon)}\,\nabla_\lambda T_\lambda(\varepsilon)\right] + \nabla_\lambda\mathbb{H}[q_\lambda]
$$

With entropy $\sum_j\log s_j + \text{const}$, $\nabla_m = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_z\log p]$ and
$\nabla_{\log s_j} = \mathbb{E}[(\nabla_z\log p)_j\,\varepsilon_j s_j] + 1$: it uses the model's
gradient, so it knows which way is uphill. Test: ch. 3's Bayesian logistic regression (40 users,
three coefficients), $q$ slightly off the optimum, 200,000 single-draw estimates each.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)                    # the running dataset of ch. 3
n, d, prior_sd = 40, 3, 2.5
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), rng.normal(size=(n, 2))])
y = (rng.random(n) < special.expit(X @ np.array([-0.5, 1.5, -1.0]))).astype(float)
def log_joint(B):
    eta = B @ X.T
    return (y * eta - np.logaddexp(0, eta)).sum(-1) - 0.5 * (B ** 2).sum(-1) / prior_sd ** 2 - d * np.log(prior_sd * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
grad_log_joint = lambda B: (y - special.expit(B @ X.T)) @ X - B / prior_sd ** 2
b_map = np.zeros(d)
for _ in range(25):                               # Newton to the mode; Laplace covariance
    pr = special.expit(X @ b_map)
    H = -(X.T * (pr * (1 - pr))) @ X - np.eye(d) / prior_sd ** 2
    b_map = b_map - np.linalg.solve(H, grad_log_joint(b_map))
cov_lap = np.linalg.inv(-H)
m, s = b_map + 0.3, 0.8 * np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_lap))   # q = N(m, diag(s^2)), lambda = (m, log s)
eps = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal((200_000, d)); Z = m + s * eps
f = log_joint(Z) - stats.norm.logpdf(Z, m, s).sum(-1)
```

## ADVI: automatic VI in unconstrained space

ADVI (Kucukelbir et al., 2017) is what Stan, PyMC and NumPyro run for VI. It maps constrained
parameters to $\mathbb{R}^d$ ($\sigma = e^{\zeta}$, logits) and adds the log-Jacobian,
$\log p(x, T^{-1}(\zeta)) + \log\lvert\det J_{T^{-1}}(\zeta)\rvert$, so a Gaussian respects every
constraint. It fits a **mean-field** $\mathcal{N}(m, \mathrm{diag}(s^2))$ or **full-rank**
$\mathcal{N}(m, LL^\top)$ Gaussian ($L$ lower-triangular, positive diagonal) by reparameterised
autodiff gradients. The logistic coefficients are already unconstrained. Below: 16 draws per step,
decaying Adam, Polyak averaging over the second half; reference is ch. 3's 1M-draw IS.

```python
import torch
torch.set_num_threads(1)
t_ref = stats.multivariate_t(b_map, 1.5 * cov_lap, df=4)      # reference: 1M-draw SNIS of ch. 3
th_ref = t_ref.rvs(1_000_000, random_state=np.random.default_rng(1))
lw_ref = log_joint(th_ref) - t_ref.logpdf(th_ref)
w_ref = np.exp(lw_ref - lw_ref.max()); w_ref /= w_ref.sum()
m_ref = w_ref @ th_ref; cov_ref = (th_ref - m_ref).T @ ((th_ref - m_ref) * w_ref[:, None])
s_ref, log_Z_ref = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_ref)), lw_ref.max() + np.log(np.mean(np.exp(lw_ref - lw_ref.max())))
def log_joint_t(B, Xb=torch.tensor(X), yb=torch.tensor(y), scale=1.0):   # scale = n / B for minibatches
    eta = B @ Xb.T
    return scale * (yb * eta - torch.nn.functional.softplus(eta)).sum(-1) - 0.5 * (B ** 2).sum(-1) / prior_sd ** 2
def advi(full_rank, steps=4000, S=16, lr=0.05, decay=500, init_log_sd=0.0, lj=log_joint_t, dim=d):
    torch.manual_seed(0); kw = dict(dtype=torch.float64)
    mu = torch.zeros(dim, **kw, requires_grad=True)
    L_raw = (init_log_sd * torch.eye(dim, **kw)).requires_grad_(True)       # diagonal holds log sd
    mask = torch.tril(torch.ones(dim, dim, **kw), -1) * float(full_rank)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([mu, L_raw], lr=lr)
    sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(opt, lambda t: 1 / (1 + t / decay))
    avg_mu, avg_C, n_avg = torch.zeros(dim, **kw), torch.zeros(dim, dim, **kw), steps - steps // 2
    for t in range(steps):
        L = torch.diag(torch.exp(torch.diagonal(L_raw))) + L_raw * mask
        Zs = mu + torch.randn(S, dim, **kw) @ L.T                          # reparameterised draws
        elbo = lj(Zs).mean() + torch.diagonal(L_raw).sum()                 # + entropy, up to a constant
        opt.zero_grad(); (-elbo).backward(); opt.step(); sched.step()
        if t >= steps // 2:                                                # Polyak averaging
            with torch.no_grad():
                L = torch.diag(torch.exp(torch.diagonal(L_raw))) + L_raw * mask
                avg_mu += mu / n_avg; avg_C += L @ L.T / n_avg
    return avg_mu.numpy(), avg_C.numpy()
fits = {"Laplace": (b_map, cov_lap), "ADVI mean-field": advi(False), "ADVI full-rank": advi(True)}
print(f"{'':>16} {'means':>23} {'sds':>21} {'corr(b0,b1)':>12} {'max|mean err|/sd':>17} {'sd / ref':>12}")
for name, (mq, Cq) in {"reference": (m_ref, cov_ref), **fits}.items():
    sq = np.sqrt(np.diag(Cq)); r = sq / s_ref
    print(f"{name:>16} {np.array2string(mq, precision=3):>23} {np.array2string(sq, precision=3):>21} {Cq[0, 1] / sq[0] / sq[1]:>12.3f} "
          f"{np.max(np.abs(mq - m_ref) / s_ref):>17.3f} {r.min():>5.3f}-{r.max():.3f}")
```

## Stochastic VI with minibatches

The likelihood is a sum, so each step can scale up a random minibatch $\mathcal{B}$ of $B$ of the
$n$ points:

$$
\widehat{\log p}(x, z) = \frac{n}{B}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}}\log p(x_i \mid z) + \log p(z),
\qquad \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}\big[\widehat{\log p}\big] = \log p(x, z)
$$

It is unbiased, so Robbins-Monro steps ($\sum_t\rho_t = \infty$, $\sum_t\rho_t^2 < \infty$)
converge (Hoffman et al., 2013, add natural gradients). Each step costs $B$ evaluations, not $n$.
Forget $n/B$ and you fit a $B$-point dataset. Test: 20,000 points, 5 coefficients, mean-field; the
exact mean-field sds of a near-Gaussian posterior are $1/\sqrt{\Lambda_{jj}}$.


## Is the approximation good enough? PSIS $\hat k$ and the importance-weighted ELBO

The ELBO ranks two $q$'s but cannot say whether either is good, because $\log p(x)$ is unknown. Yao
et al. (2018) use $q$ as an importance-sampling proposal and look at the tail of
$w = p(x, z)/q(z)$ with the Pareto $\hat k$ of ch. 3 (applied to LOO in ch. 9). Below $0.5$ the fit
is reliable, between $0.5$ and $0.7$ usable with smoothing, and above $0.7$ untrustworthy. The same
weights correct $q$'s moments by self-normalised IS and give the **importance-weighted ELBO**
(Burda et al., 2016), a bound that tightens with $K$ (Jensen on an average of $K$ weights):

$$
\mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}_{z_{1:K} \sim q}\left[\log\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{p(x, z_k)}{q(z_k)}\right],
\qquad \mathrm{ELBO} = \mathcal{L}_1 \le \mathcal{L}_K \le \mathcal{L}_{K+1} \le \log p(x)
$$

```python
@np.errstate(over="ignore")
def pareto_khat(log_w):                             # Zhang-Stephens GPD fit, as in PSIS
    w = np.sort(np.exp(log_w - log_w.max()))
    M = int(np.ceil(min(0.2 * len(w), 3 * np.sqrt(len(w))))); x = w[-M:] - w[-M - 1]; nm = 30 + int(np.sqrt(M))
    bs = 1 / x[-1] + (1 - np.sqrt(nm / (np.arange(1, nm + 1) - 0.5))) / (3 * x[int(M / 4 + 0.5) - 1])
    ks = -np.mean(np.log1p(-bs[:, None] * x), axis=1)
    L = M * (np.log(bs / ks) + ks - 1)
    b = np.sum(bs / np.sum(np.exp(L[None, :] - L[:, None]), axis=1))
    return (M * np.mean(np.log1p(-b * x)) + 5) / (M + 10)     # shrink towards 0.5 for short tails
rq = np.random.default_rng(9)
print(f"{'q':>16} {'khat':>5} {'ESS/S':>6} {'IS-corrected sds':>21} {'IW-ELBO K=1':>12} {'K=10':>8} {'K=100':>8} {'K=1000':>8}")

          + " ".join(f"{v:>8.3f}" for v in iw))
print(f"{'reference':>16} {'':>12} {np.array2string(s_ref, precision=3):>21}  log Z = {log_Z_ref:.3f}")
```

## When to prefer VI and when to prefer MCMC

MCMC is asymptotically exact with failure diagnostics (ch. 7); VI is biased by its family but fast,
deterministic, and minibatchable. Often you combine them: VI gets close, $\hat k$ decides if that is enough.

| Situation | Prefer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Final inference where intervals and tails matter | MCMC (NUTS, ch. 6) | VI underestimates variance |
| Millions of rows, streaming data | SVI | minibatch gradients, $O(B)$ per step |
| One latent per data point (topic models, VAEs) | amortised VI (Generative Models) | an encoder predicts each $q(z_i \mid x_i)$ |
| Fast model iteration, initialising MCMC | ADVI, Pathfinder | minutes, decent means |
| Bayesian neural networks (ch. 13) | VI, ensembles, Laplace | MCMC rarely scales there |
