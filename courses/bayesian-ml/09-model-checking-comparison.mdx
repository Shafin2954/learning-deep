## Two different questions

Think of a weather forecaster. *Checking* asks whether her forecasts look like real weather at all: does
it ever rain as hard as it really does? *Comparison* asks which of two forecasters to trust tomorrow. A
model can pass every check and still predict worse, and a comparison winner can fail a check, since a
comparison only ranks the candidates you supplied. The workflow (ch. 8) needs both.

The running data are 80 days of failed-job counts $y_i$ from a batch cluster. The obvious model is
Poisson with rate $\lambda$ and a $\mathrm{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)$ prior (shape, rate). It is conjugate
(ch. 2), so the posterior is $\mathrm{Gamma}(a_0 + \sum y_i,\; b_0 + n)$ and draws cost nothing.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from scipy.special import logsumexp, gammaln

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
n_c = 80
y_c = stats.nbinom.rvs(1.5, 1.5 / (1.5 + 4.0), size=n_c, random_state=rng)   # truth: NegBin, mean 4
a0, b0 = 2.0, 0.5                                                           # Gamma(shape, rate) prior
S = 4000
lam = rng.gamma(a0 + y_c.sum(), 1 / (b0 + n_c), S)                          # Poisson posterior draws
print(f"n={n_c} | mean {y_c.mean():.2f} | var {y_c.var(ddof=1):.2f} | zeros {np.mean(y_c == 0):.3f} | max {y_c.max()}")
print(f"posterior lambda: mean {lam.mean():.3f}, 95% [{np.quantile(lam, .025):.3f}, {np.quantile(lam, .975):.3f}]")
```

```output
n=80 | mean 3.73 | var 18.00 | zeros 0.200 | max 22
posterior lambda: mean 3.728, 95% [3.321, 4.180]
```

The variance is almost five times the mean, which a Poisson cannot produce: its variance *equals* its
mean. The posterior for $\lambda$ hides that. It is a tight interval around 3.7 and looks perfectly healthy.

## Posterior predictive checks done properly

If the model were right, fake data simulated from it should look like the real data. So you simulate many
fake datasets and hunt for a feature where the real one stands out. Formally, a posterior predictive check
(ch. 1) draws replicated data $y^{\text{rep}} \sim p(y^{\text{rep}}\mid y)$, computes a test statistic
$T$ on each, and reports the posterior predictive p-value

$$
p_B = P\big(T(y^{\text{rep}}) \ge T(y) \,\big|\, y\big) = \iint \mathbb{1}\big[T(y^{\text{rep}}) \ge T(y)\big]\,
p(y^{\text{rep}}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid y)\,dy^{\text{rep}}\,d\theta ,
$$

the fraction of fake datasets whose statistic is at least the real one. The choice of $T$ is everything.
For the Poisson, $\sum y_i$ is sufficient for $\lambda$, so the posterior is built to reproduce the sample
mean, and checking the mean checks the fit against itself. Useful statistics are roughly **orthogonal to
the fitted parameters**: features the model fixes *by assumption*, not by fitting. For a Poisson these are
the dispersion ratio $s^2/\bar y$ (fixed at 1), the fraction of zeros (fixed at $e^{-\lambda}$), and the
maximum.

```python
y_rep_p = rng.poisson(lam[:, None], (S, n_c))
T_stats = {"mean": lambda a: a.mean(axis=-1),
           "var/mean": lambda a: a.var(axis=-1, ddof=1) / np.maximum(a.mean(axis=-1), 1e-9),
           "frac zeros": lambda a: np.mean(a == 0, axis=-1),
           "max": lambda a: a.max(axis=-1)}

def ppc(y_obs, y_rep):
    for name, T in T_stats.items():
        t_obs, t_rep = T(y_obs), T(y_rep)
        print(f"  {name:10} observed {t_obs:6.2f} | replicated 90% [{np.quantile(t_rep, .05):6.2f}, "
              f"{np.quantile(t_rep, .95):6.2f}] | p_B {np.mean(t_rep >= t_obs):.3f}")

print("Poisson:"); ppc(y_c, y_rep_p)
```

```output
Poisson:
  mean       observed   3.73 | replicated 90% [  3.24,   4.24] | p_B 0.497
  var/mean   observed   4.83 | replicated 90% [  0.75,   1.27] | p_B 0.000
  frac zeros observed   0.20 | replicated 90% [  0.00,   0.06] | p_B 0.000
  max        observed  22.00 | replicated 90% [  7.00,  11.00] | p_B 0.000
```

The mean passes with $p_B = 0.497$, which proves nothing. The three assumption-driven statistics all fail
at $p_B = 0.000$. The dispersion ratio of 4.83 is far outside the replicated 0.75 to 1.27. One day in five
has zero failures where the Poisson expects at most 6%, and the maximum of 22 is double the replicated
upper band (11). Every failure points at the same fix: the data need a variance larger than the mean.

### Posterior predictive p-values are conservative

This shows that $p_B$ cannot be read like a classical p-value. The data are used twice, once to fit
$\theta$ and once to judge it, so $p_B$ is not uniform when the model is true. It is pulled toward 0.5,
most strongly for statistics close to what was fitted. Below, 400 datasets are drawn *from the Poisson
itself*. A calibrated p-value would have sd $1/\sqrt{12} = 0.289$ and 10% of its mass outside $[0.05, 0.95]$.

```python
R, S_small = 400, 500
pb = {name: np.empty(R) for name in ("mean", "var/mean", "max")}
for r in range(R):
    y_sim = rng.poisson(4.0, n_c)                                        # the model is TRUE here
    lam_r = rng.gamma(a0 + y_sim.sum(), 1 / (b0 + n_c), S_small)
    rep = rng.poisson(lam_r[:, None], (S_small, n_c))
    for name in pb:
        T = T_stats[name]
        t_rep, t_obs = T(rep), T(y_sim)
        pb[name][r] = np.mean(t_rep > t_obs) + 0.5 * np.mean(t_rep == t_obs)   # mid-p for ties
for name, v in pb.items():
    print(f"T = {name:9} sd(p_B) {v.std():.3f} (uniform 0.289) | "
          f"P(p_B < 0.05 or > 0.95) {np.mean((v < .05) | (v > .95)):.3f} (uniform 0.100)")
```

```output
T = mean      sd(p_B) 0.022 (uniform 0.289) | P(p_B < 0.05 or > 0.95) 0.000 (uniform 0.100)
T = var/mean  sd(p_B) 0.277 (uniform 0.289) | P(p_B < 0.05 or > 0.95) 0.095 (uniform 0.100)
T = max       sd(p_B) 0.263 (uniform 0.289) | P(p_B < 0.05 or > 0.95) 0.068 (uniform 0.100)
```

With a true model, $p_B$ for the mean has sd 0.022 and never leaves $[0.05, 0.95]$: it cannot reject
anything. The dispersion ratio, which the fit does not tune, is nearly calibrated (sd 0.277, 9.5% extreme),
and the discrete maximum sits at 6.8%. So $p_B$ near 0 or 1 is strong evidence of misfit, while $p_B$ near
0.5 is weak evidence of fit. Use PPCs to find *how* a model is wrong, not as tests with a known size.

## Fixing the likelihood: negative binomial

Let each day have its own hidden rate. If busy and quiet days differ, pooled counts vary more than any
single Poisson allows. That is the negative binomial, a Gamma mixture of Poissons:
$y \sim \mathrm{NB}(\mu, \phi)$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\mu + \mu^2/\phi$. Small dispersion $\phi$
means strong overdispersion, and $\phi \to \infty$ recovers the Poisson. Two parameters fit comfortably
on a grid (ch. 1), with $\log\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\log 3, 1)$ and $\log\phi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1.5)$.
Keep the grid's log-likelihood matrix: exact leave-one-out on a grid is just a reweighting.

```python
def nb_logpmf(y, mu, phi):
    return stats.nbinom.logpmf(y, phi, phi / (phi + mu))

lmu_g, lphi_g = np.linspace(np.log(2), np.log(8), 150), np.linspace(np.log(0.2), np.log(20), 150)
LMU, LPHI = [a.ravel() for a in np.meshgrid(lmu_g, lphi_g, indexing="ij")]
ll_grid_nb = nb_logpmf(y_c[:, None], np.exp(LMU), np.exp(LPHI))          # (n, G)
lp_nb = ll_grid_nb.sum(0) + stats.norm.logpdf(LMU, np.log(3), 1) + stats.norm.logpdf(LPHI, 0, 1.5)
w_nb = np.exp(lp_nb - logsumexp(lp_nb))
cell = rng.choice(LMU.size, S, p=w_nb)
d_mu, d_phi = lmu_g[1] - lmu_g[0], lphi_g[1] - lphi_g[0]
mu_s = np.exp(LMU[cell] + rng.uniform(-d_mu / 2, d_mu / 2, S))          # jitter within the cell
phi_s = np.exp(LPHI[cell] + rng.uniform(-d_phi / 2, d_phi / 2, S))
for name, d in (("mu ", mu_s), ("phi", phi_s)):
    print(f"{name} posterior mean {d.mean():.2f}, 95% [{np.quantile(d, .025):.2f}, {np.quantile(d, .975):.2f}]")
y_rep_nb = stats.nbinom.rvs(phi_s[:, None], (phi_s / (phi_s + mu_s))[:, None], size=(S, n_c), random_state=rng)
print("NegBin:"); ppc(y_c, y_rep_nb)
```

```output
mu  posterior mean 3.76, 95% [2.93, 4.80]
phi posterior mean 1.03, 95% [0.66, 1.53]
NegBin:
  mean       observed   3.73 | replicated 90% [  2.75,   4.97] | p_B 0.502
  var/mean   observed   4.83 | replicated 90% [  2.85,   7.47] | p_B 0.406
  frac zeros observed   0.20 | replicated 90% [  0.11,   0.33] | p_B 0.597
  max        observed  22.00 | replicated 90% [ 13.00,  33.00] | p_B 0.377
```

The dispersion is $1.03$ (95% interval 0.66 to 1.53), nowhere near the Poisson limit, and every $p_B$
now lies between 0.38 and 0.60. The interval for the mean widened from $[3.32, 4.18]$ to $[2.93, 4.80]$:
the Poisson was also overconfident about the one quantity it fitted.

## LOO-PIT: a calibration check that does not double-dip

For each day, ask at which percentile the model would have put that day's count *before seeing it*. For an
honest model, those percentiles are uniform. With $F_{-i}$ the leave-one-out predictive CDF, the
probability integral transform $u_i = F_{-i}(y_i)$ is uniform under calibration. Counts need the randomised
$u_i = F_{-i}(y_i - 1) + v_i\,p_{-i}(y_i)$, with tie-breaker $v_i \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$. A U-shape means
intervals too narrow, a hump means too wide. LOO is exact for both models: for the Poisson, $\lambda \mid y_{-i}$
is Gamma, so the predictive is negative binomial. On the NB grid, dividing the weights by $p(y_i\mid\theta)$
and renormalising *is* the posterior given $y_{-i}$.

```python
a_i, b_i = a0 + y_c.sum() - y_c, b0 + n_c - 1                           # Gamma posterior without y_i
cdf_p = lambda t: stats.nbinom.cdf(t, a_i, b_i / (b_i + 1))             # Poisson-Gamma predictive
w_loo = w_nb[None, :] * np.exp(-ll_grid_nb)                             # posterior given y_{-i}, on the grid
w_loo /= w_loo.sum(1, keepdims=True)
cdf_nb = lambda t: np.sum(w_loo * stats.nbinom.cdf(t[:, None], np.exp(LPHI), np.exp(LPHI) / (np.exp(LPHI) + np.exp(LMU))), 1)
v = rng.uniform(size=n_c)
for name, F in (("Poisson", cdf_p), ("NegBin", cdf_nb)):
    lo, hi = F(y_c - 1), F(y_c)
    u = lo + v * (hi - lo)
    hist = np.histogram(u, bins=5, range=(0, 1))[0]
    print(f"{name:8} LOO-PIT counts per fifth {hist} (expect 16 each) | outside [0.05, 0.95]: "
          f"{np.mean((u < .05) | (u > .95)):.3f} (expect 0.10) | KS p {stats.kstest(u, 'uniform').pvalue:.3f}")
```

```output
Poisson  LOO-PIT counts per fifth [35 11  9  7 18] (expect 16 each) | outside [0.05, 0.95]: 0.412 (expect 0.10) | KS p 0.000
NegBin   LOO-PIT counts per fifth [15 17 16 15 17] (expect 16 each) | outside [0.05, 0.95]: 0.113 (expect 0.10) | KS p 0.999
```

The Poisson's LOO-PIT is U-shaped: 35 of 80 days in the bottom fifth, 18 in the top, and 41% outside the
central 90% band where 10% is expected. Its predictive intervals are far too narrow at both ends. The
negative binomial is flat (15 to 17 per bin, 11.3% outside, KS $p$ 0.999). LOO-PIT checks the whole
predictive distribution at once, and the leave-one-out step removes the conservativeness of $p_B$.

## Predictive accuracy as the target

Grade a forecaster by the probability she gave to what actually happened. If she said 30% and it rained,
she scores $\log 0.3$. Summed over many days, that log score is the yardstick for the rest of the chapter.
For new data $\tilde y_i$ from the true process $p_t$, the **expected log pointwise predictive density** is

$$
\mathrm{elpd} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \int p_t(\tilde y_i)\,\log p(\tilde y_i \mid y)\,d\tilde y_i,
\qquad p(\tilde y_i \mid y) = \int p(\tilde y_i\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta .
$$

The log score is the only proper local scoring rule, and it grades the whole predictive distribution, not
a point forecast. You do not know $p_t$. Plugging in the observed data instead gives the **log pointwise
predictive density**, computed from $S$ posterior draws $\theta^{(s)}$:

$$
\mathrm{lppd} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} p(y_i\mid\theta^{(s)}) .
$$

The lppd is optimistic, because each $y_i$ helped choose the $\theta$ that now scores it, and the bias
grows with the effective number of parameters. Every criterion below estimates and removes this optimism.

### The classical corrections, briefly

These are the two older "fit minus complexity" estimates. AIC uses $\log p(y\mid\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}) - k$
with $k$ parameters. DIC uses $\log p(y\mid\bar\theta) - p_{\mathrm{DIC}}$ with posterior mean $\bar\theta$ and
$p_{\mathrm{DIC}} = 2\big(\log p(y\mid\bar\theta) - \mathbb{E}_{\text{post}}\log p(y\mid\theta)\big)$, which
shrinks when the prior regularises. Both plug in one point, which fails under multimodality or constraints.

### WAIC derived from a cumulant expansion

This shows that a point's optimism is, to second order, the posterior variance of its log-likelihood.
Treat $L_i = \log p(y_i\mid\theta)$ as a random variable under the posterior, with mean $\mu_i$ and
variance $\sigma_i^2$. The cumulant expansion is
$\log\mathbb{E}[e^{tL_i}] = t\mu_i + \tfrac{t^2}{2}\sigma_i^2 + O(t^3)$. At $t = 1$ it gives the lppd
term. At $t = -1$ it gives the *leave-one-out* term, because by Bayes' rule

$$
p(y_i\mid y_{-i}) = \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\mid y}\big[1/p(y_i\mid\theta)\big]}
\;\Rightarrow\; \log p(y_i\mid y_{-i}) = -\log\mathbb{E}[e^{-L_i}] \approx \mu_i - \tfrac12\sigma_i^2 .
$$

Subtracting, $\log \mathbb{E}[e^{L_i}] - \log p(y_i\mid y_{-i}) \approx \sigma_i^2$, so

$$
\boxed{\;\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{WAIC}} = \mathrm{lppd} - p_{\mathrm{WAIC}},
\qquad p_{\mathrm{WAIC}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{Var}_{\theta\mid y}\big[\log p(y_i\mid\theta)\big]\;}
$$

Watanabe proved the asymptotic equivalence with LOO, for singular models too. WAIC is fully Bayesian
and pointwise. It fails when one $\sigma_i^2$ is large, which is exactly when the expansion breaks.

## All the criteria against the truth

To test a penalty fairly, generate data where you know the true out-of-sample score, and see which estimate
lands on it. A conjugate Normal-inverse-Gamma regression gives closed forms for posterior draws, the
evidence and the Student-t predictive. So you can refit $n$ times for exact LOO and score a 20,000-point
test set from the known generator. Model $k$ uses the first $k$ of 10 covariates, and only four have
nonzero effects.

```python
def nig_fit(X, y, tau=1.0, a=2.0, b=2.0):
    """w | s2 ~ N(0, s2 tau^2 I), s2 ~ InvGamma(a, b). Returns posterior params and log evidence."""
    n, k = X.shape
    Vn = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X + np.eye(k) / tau**2)
    mn = Vn @ X.T @ y
    an, bn = a + n / 2, b + 0.5 * (y @ y - mn @ (X.T @ X + np.eye(k) / tau**2) @ mn)
    log_ev = (-n / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi) + 0.5 * np.linalg.slogdet(Vn)[1] - k * np.log(tau)
              + a * np.log(b) - an * np.log(bn) + gammaln(an) - gammaln(a))
    return dict(Vn=Vn, mn=mn, an=an, bn=bn, log_ev=log_ev)

def nig_pred_logpdf(f, Xnew, ynew):                       # Student-t posterior predictive
    scale = np.sqrt(f["bn"] / f["an"] * (1 + np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Xnew, f["Vn"], Xnew)))
    return stats.t.logpdf(ynew, 2 * f["an"], Xnew @ f["mn"], scale)

def nig_draws(f, S, rng):
    s2 = 1 / rng.gamma(f["an"], 1 / f["bn"], S)
    z = rng.standard_normal((S, len(f["mn"]))) @ np.linalg.cholesky(f["Vn"]).T
    return f["mn"] + np.sqrt(s2)[:, None] * z, s2

def exact_loo(X, y, **kw):
    idx = np.arange(len(y))
    return np.array([nig_pred_logpdf(nig_fit(X[idx != i], y[idx != i], **kw), X[i:i+1], y[i:i+1])[0] for i in idx])

n_r, w_true = 40, np.array([1.0, 0.6, 0.3, 0.15, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0])
X_te = rng.standard_normal((20_000, 10))
y_te = X_te @ w_true + rng.standard_normal(20_000)
ks, R_ds, S_r = (1, 2, 4, 7, 10), 40, 2000
crit = {c: np.zeros((R_ds, len(ks))) for c in ("lppd", "AIC", "DIC", "WAIC", "LOO", "true")}
for r in range(R_ds):                                       # 40 independent training sets
    X_r = rng.standard_normal((n_r, 10))
    y_r = X_r @ w_true + rng.standard_normal(n_r)
    for j, k in enumerate(ks):
        X = X_r[:, :k]
        f = nig_fit(X, y_r)
        W, s2 = nig_draws(f, S_r, rng)
        ll = stats.norm.logpdf(y_r, W @ X.T, np.sqrt(s2)[:, None])          # (S, n) pointwise log-lik
        lppd_i = logsumexp(ll, 0) - np.log(S_r)
        beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y_r, rcond=None)[0]
        ll_bar = stats.norm.logpdf(y_r, X @ W.mean(0), np.sqrt(s2.mean())).sum()
        crit["lppd"][r, j] = lppd_i.sum()
        crit["AIC"][r, j] = stats.norm.logpdf(y_r, X @ beta, np.sqrt(np.mean((y_r - X @ beta) ** 2))).sum() - (k + 1)
        crit["DIC"][r, j] = ll_bar - 2 * (ll_bar - ll.sum(1).mean())
        crit["WAIC"][r, j] = lppd_i.sum() - ll.var(0, ddof=1).sum()
        crit["LOO"][r, j] = exact_loo(X, y_r).sum()
        crit["true"][r, j] = n_r * nig_pred_logpdf(f, X_te[:, :k], y_te).mean()   # elpd on fresh data
print("averages over 40 training sets (elpd scale, higher is better)")
print(f"{'k':>2}" + "".join(f"{c:>8}" for c in crit) + f"{'sd(LOO-true)':>14}")
for j, k in enumerate(ks):
    print(f"{k:>2}" + "".join(f"{v[:, j].mean():8.2f}" for v in crit.values())
          + f"{(crit['LOO'][:, j] - crit['true'][:, j]).std():14.2f}")
print(f"LOO picks the true-elpd-best k in {np.mean(crit['LOO'].argmax(1) == crit['true'].argmax(1)):.2f} of datasets")
```

```output
averages over 40 training sets (elpd scale, higher is better)
 k    lppd     AIC     DIC    WAIC     LOO    true  sd(LOO-true)
 1  -63.44  -65.32  -65.21  -65.28  -65.30  -65.19          4.28
 2  -58.12  -60.89  -60.75  -60.85  -60.89  -60.10          3.45
 4  -55.51  -60.11  -59.90  -60.20  -60.35  -59.17          3.83
 7  -54.33  -61.59  -61.30  -62.01  -62.44  -60.72          4.68
10  -52.88  -62.72  -62.31  -63.54  -64.57  -62.76          6.10

```

The lppd is always too high, by 1.75 nats at $k=1$ and 9.88 at $k=10$: about one nat per parameter,
counting the noise variance. AIC, DIC, WAIC and exact LOO remove most of the gap, and on average all pick
$k=4$, as the truth does. At $k=10$ AIC and DIC are on target, while WAIC and LOO are pessimistic by 0.8 and
1.8 nats. The last column is the sobering one: on a *single* dataset LOO misses the true elpd by 3.5 to 6
nats, more than the gaps between models, so it picks the truly best $k$ only 35% of the time. That noise
must enter the comparison (below). Later sections reuse the last training set, `X_r, y_r`.

## Importance-sampling LOO and Pareto smoothing

Refitting $n$ times is cheap only for conjugate models. The shortcut is to reweight the draws of the one
posterior you have, so that point $i$ counts as never seen. Draws that fit $y_i$ badly get *more* weight,
because without $y_i$ they would have been more plausible. The posterior is the proposal and
$p(\theta\mid y_{-i})$ the target, so the importance ratios are $r_i^{(s)} \propto 1/p(y_i\mid\theta^{(s)})$ (ch. 3):

$$
p(y_i\mid y_{-i}) \approx \frac{\sum_s r_i^{(s)}\,p(y_i\mid\theta^{(s)})}{\sum_s r_i^{(s)}}
= \frac{1}{\frac{1}{S}\sum_s 1/p(y_i\mid\theta^{(s)})} .
$$

That harmonic mean can have infinite variance. When $y_i$ is influential, the full posterior is narrower
than the leave-$i$-out one, which is the light-tailed proposal of ch. 3. **PSIS** (Vehtari, Gelman and
Gabry 2017) fits a generalised Pareto distribution to the largest $M = \min(S/5,\, 3\sqrt{S})$ ratios,
replaces them with quantiles of the fit, truncates at the raw maximum, and reports the shape $\hat k$.
Here is the reference algorithm: the Zhang–Stephens estimator, with a weak prior shrinking $\hat k$ toward
0.5, checked on ch. 3's Gaussian test where the true shape is $1 - \sigma_q^2$.

```python
def gpd_fit(x):                  # Zhang & Stephens (2009) GPD fit to sorted exceedances, PSIS prior on k
    n = len(x)
    m = 30 + int(np.sqrt(n))
    b = 1 - np.sqrt(m / (np.arange(1, m + 1) - 0.5))
    b = b / (3 * x[int(n / 4 + 0.5) - 1]) + 1 / x[-1]
    k = np.log1p(-b[:, None] * x).mean(1)
    L = n * (np.log(-b / k) - k - 1)                       # profile log-likelihood for each b
    wts = 1 / np.exp(L[None, :] - L[:, None]).sum(1)
    b_post = np.sum(b * wts) / wts.sum()
    k_post = np.log1p(-b_post * x).mean()
    return (n * k_post + 10 * 0.5) / (n + 10), -k_post / b_post   # prior: 10 pseudo-obs at k = 0.5; sigma

def psis(log_r):                 # Pareto-smoothed normalised log weights and khat for one observation
    lw = log_r - log_r.max()
    M = int(np.ceil(min(0.2 * len(lw), 3 * np.sqrt(len(lw)))))
    order = np.argsort(lw)
    tail_idx, cutoff = order[-M:], lw[order[-M - 1]]
    k, sigma = gpd_fit(np.exp(lw[tail_idx]) - np.exp(cutoff))
    p = (np.arange(M) + 0.5) / M
    smoothed = np.log(np.exp(cutoff) + sigma / k * np.expm1(-k * np.log1p(-p)))
    lw[tail_idx] = np.minimum(smoothed, 0.0)                # never exceed the largest raw weight
    return lw - logsumexp(lw), k

for sig in (0.8, 0.7, 0.5):                                 # ch. 3's test: N(0,1) target, N(0, sig^2) proposal
    x = rng.normal(0, sig, S)
    log_r = stats.norm.logpdf(x) - stats.norm.logpdf(x, 0, sig)
    lw, k = psis(log_r)
    raw = np.exp(log_r - logsumexp(log_r))
    print(f"sigma_q {sig}: khat {k:.2f} (theory {1 - sig**2:.2f}) | E[x^2] raw IS {raw @ x**2:.3f}, "
          f"PSIS {np.exp(lw) @ x**2:.3f} (truth 1)")

def loo_estimates(ll):           # ll: (S, n) -> pointwise raw-IS LOO, PSIS-LOO, khat
    is_loo = -(logsumexp(-ll, 0) - np.log(ll.shape[0]))
    res = [psis(-ll[:, i]) for i in range(ll.shape[1])]
    psis_loo = np.array([logsumexp(lw + ll[:, i]) for i, (lw, _) in enumerate(res)])
    return is_loo, psis_loo, np.array([k for _, k in res])
```

```output
sigma_q 0.7: khat 0.30 (theory 0.51) | E[x^2] raw IS 0.901, PSIS 0.899 (truth 1)
```

## Differences, standard errors, and ties

Two students who sit the same exam are compared question by question, because a hard question is hard for
both. Both models score the same $y_i$, so take $d_i = \widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{A,i} - \widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{B,i}$:

$$
\widehat{\Delta} = \sum_i d_i, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\widehat\Delta) = \sqrt{n\,\mathrm{Var}(d_i)} .
$$

The paired SE is usually far smaller than either model's own. Rules of thumb (Sivula et al. 2022): $|\Delta| < 4$
is a tie whatever the SE says, and the SE is trustworthy only when $n$ is not tiny and neither model is badly
misspecified. Beyond that, a difference within about two SEs is noise.

```python
def compare(name, a, b):
    d = a - b
    se = np.sqrt(len(d) * d.var(ddof=1))
    print(f"{name:32} diff {d.sum():+7.2f} | SE {se:5.2f} | diff/SE {d.sum() / se:+5.1f} | "
          f"SE(A) alone {np.sqrt(len(a) * a.var(ddof=1)):5.2f}")

```

## Bayes factors versus LOO: prior sensitivity

Give a coefficient an absurdly vague prior, say $\pm 1000$: before any data, the model spreads its bets so
thin that it predicted the actual data poorly. The evidence $p(y\mid M) = \int p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta\mid M)\,d\theta$
scores exactly that **prior** predictive performance, while LOO scores **posterior** predictive performance,
where the vague prior has washed out. One extra coefficient with prior sd $\tau$ adds an Occam factor near
$\sigma_{\text{post}}/\tau$, so each tenfold widening costs $\log 10 \approx 2.3$ nats: Lindley's paradox (ch. 3).

```python
print(f"{'tau':>6} {'log BF(k=4 vs 3)':>17} {'LOO diff':>9}")
for tau in (0.5, 1, 10, 100, 1000):
    log_bf = nig_fit(X_r[:, :4], y_r, tau=tau)["log_ev"] - nig_fit(X_r[:, :3], y_r, tau=tau)["log_ev"]
    d = exact_loo(X_r[:, :4], y_r, tau=tau).sum() - exact_loo(X_r[:, :3], y_r, tau=tau).sum()
    print(f"{tau:>6g} {log_bf:17.2f} {d:9.2f}")
```

```output
   tau  log BF(k=4 vs 3)  LOO diff
   0.5             -1.26     -1.09

    10             -4.24     -1.27
```

From $\tau = 10$ on, the log Bayes factor falls by $\log 10 = 2.30$ per decade ($-4.24$, $-6.54$, $-8.85$).
A vague enough prior makes the evidence reject $k = 4$ as strongly as you like, with the data unchanged.
The LOO difference moves from $-1.09$ to $-1.27$ and freezes. Bayes factors suit priors that are genuine
claims about effect sizes. With default priors they measure the prior as much as the model.

## Stacking predictive distributions

If three forecasters each watch a different part of the sky, the best forecast mixes them, even when one is
individually best. **Bayesian model averaging** weights $M_k$ by $p(M_k\mid y) \propto p(y\mid M_k)\,p(M_k)$,
which is right when one candidate is true ($\mathcal{M}$-closed). When none is ($\mathcal{M}$-open), BMA
still converges to the single KL-closest model. *Pseudo-BMA*, $w_k \propto \exp(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo},k})$,
is just as winner-take-all. **Stacking** (Yao et al. 2018) maximises the mixture's LOO log score over the simplex $\Delta_K$:

$$
\hat w = \arg\max_{w \in \Delta_K} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\, p(y_i\mid y_{-i}, M_k).
$$

It is the concave log-likelihood of a mixture with *fixed* components $p_{ik} = p(y_i\mid y_{-i}, M_k)$, so
EM, $w_k \leftarrow \frac1n\sum_i w_k p_{ik} / \sum_j w_j p_{ij}$, finds the global optimum. Below, the truth
has three similar covariates and each candidate sees only one.

```python
n_s, rng_s = 300, np.random.default_rng(18)
X_s, X_st = rng_s.standard_normal((n_s, 3)), rng_s.standard_normal((20_000, 3))
y_s = X_s @ [0.8, 0.7, 0.6] + rng_s.standard_normal(n_s)
y_st = X_st @ [0.8, 0.7, 0.6] + rng_s.standard_normal(20_000)
P_loo = np.column_stack([np.exp(exact_loo(X_s[:, [k]], y_s)) for k in range(3)])      # (n, K)
P_test = np.column_stack([np.exp(nig_pred_logpdf(nig_fit(X_s[:, [k]], y_s), X_st[:, [k]], y_st)) for k in range(3)])
log_ev = np.array([nig_fit(X_s[:, [k]], y_s)["log_ev"] for k in range(3)])
w_stack = np.full(3, 1 / 3)
for _ in range(2000):                                       # EM for fixed-component mixture weights
    w_stack = (w_stack * P_loo / (P_loo @ w_stack)[:, None]).mean(0)
elpd_k = np.log(P_loo).sum(0)
weights = {"BMA (evidence)": np.exp(log_ev - logsumexp(log_ev)),
           "pseudo-BMA (LOO)": np.exp(elpd_k - logsumexp(elpd_k)), "stacking": w_stack}
print(f"elpd_loo per model {np.round(elpd_k, 2)} | log evidence {np.round(log_ev, 2)}")
for name, w in weights.items():
    print(f"{name:17} weights {np.round(w, 3)} | test log score per obs {np.log(P_test @ w).mean():.4f}")
```

```output
elpd_loo per model [-526.43 -541.76 -545.95] | log evidence [-530.02 -545.42 -549.7 ]
BMA (evidence)    weights [1. 0. 0.] | test log score per obs -1.7202
pseudo-BMA (LOO)  weights [1. 0. 0.] | test log score per obs -1.7202
stacking          weights [0.623 0.281 0.096] | test log score per obs -1.6957
```

Model 1 leads by 15 nats, so BMA and pseudo-BMA give it all the weight and score like it alone ($-1.7202$
per test point). Stacking keeps all three (0.623, 0.281, 0.096) and scores $-1.6957$, about 7 nats better
over 300 points, a gap that grows with $n$ as BMA concentrates harder. The mixture is wider than any single
predictive, which suits data none of them explains alone. Stacking weights rank combinations for
prediction, not models for truth.
