## Two different questions

Think of a weather forecaster. *Checking* asks whether her forecasts look like real weather at all: does
it ever rain as hard as it really does? *Comparison* asks which of two forecasters you should listen to
tomorrow. The two questions are different. A model can pass every check you think of and still predict
worse, and the winner of a comparison can still fail a check, because a comparison only ranks the
candidates you supplied. The workflow (ch. 8) needs both: checking tells you what to fix, and comparison
tells you whether the fix paid off.

The running data are 80 days of failed-job counts $y_i$ from a batch cluster. The obvious model is
Poisson with rate $\lambda$ and a $\mathrm{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)$ prior (shape, rate). It is conjugate (ch.
2), so the posterior is $\mathrm{Gamma}(a_0 + \sum y_i,\; b_0 + n)$ and posterior draws cost nothing.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from scipy.special import logsumexp, gammaln

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
n_c = 80
y_c = stats.nbinom.rvs(1.5, 1.5 / (1.5 + 4.0), size=n_c, random_state=rng)   # truth: NegBin, mean 4
a0, b0 = 2.0, 0.5                                                           # Gamma(shape, rate) prior
S = 4000
lam = rng.gamma(a0 + y_c.sum(), 1 / (b0 + n_c), S)                          # Poisson posterior draws
print(f"n={n_c} | mean {y_c.mean():.2f} | var {y_c.var(ddof=1):.2f} | zeros {np.mean(y_c == 0):.3f} | max {y_c.max()}")
print(f"posterior lambda: mean {lam.mean():.3f}, 95% [{np.quantile(lam, .025):.3f}, {np.quantile(lam, .975):.3f}]")
```

```output
n=80 | mean 3.73 | var 18.00 | zeros 0.200 | max 22
posterior lambda: mean 3.728, 95% [3.321, 4.180]
```

The variance is almost five times the mean, which a Poisson cannot produce (its variance *equals* its
mean). The posterior for $\lambda$ does not show any of that. It is a tight interval around 3.7, and the
fit looks perfectly healthy from the parameters alone.

## Posterior predictive checks done properly

The idea: if the model were right, fake data simulated from it should look like the real data. So you
simulate many fake datasets and look for a feature where the real data stand out. Formally, a posterior
predictive check (ch. 1) simulates replicated data $y^{\text{rep}} \sim p(y^{\text{rep}}\mid y)$ and
compares a test statistic $T(y^{\text{rep}})$ with $T(y)$. The posterior predictive p-value is

$$
p_B = P\big(T(y^{\text{rep}}) \ge T(y) \,\big|\, y\big) = \iint \mathbb{1}\big[T(y^{\text{rep}}) \ge T(y)\big]\,
p(y^{\text{rep}}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid y)\,dy^{\text{rep}}\,d\theta .
$$

In words: $p_B$ is the fraction of fake datasets whose statistic is at least as large as the real one.
The choice of $T$ is everything. For the Poisson, $\sum y_i$ is sufficient for $\lambda$, so the
posterior is built to reproduce the sample mean. Checking the mean is checking the fit against itself.
Useful statistics are roughly **orthogonal to the fitted parameters**: features the model pins down *as a
consequence* of its assumptions rather than by fitting them. For a Poisson, that means the dispersion
ratio $s^2/\bar y$ (fixed at 1 by assumption), the fraction of zeros (fixed at $e^{-\lambda}$), and the
maximum.

```python
y_rep_p = rng.poisson(lam[:, None], (S, n_c))
T_stats = {"mean": lambda a: a.mean(axis=-1),
           "var/mean": lambda a: a.var(axis=-1, ddof=1) / np.maximum(a.mean(axis=-1), 1e-9),
           "frac zeros": lambda a: np.mean(a == 0, axis=-1),
           "max": lambda a: a.max(axis=-1)}

def ppc(y_obs, y_rep):
    for name, T in T_stats.items():
        t_obs, t_rep = T(y_obs), T(y_rep)
        print(f"  {name:10} observed {t_obs:6.2f} | replicated 90% [{np.quantile(t_rep, .05):6.2f}, "
              f"{np.quantile(t_rep, .95):6.2f}] | p_B {np.mean(t_rep >= t_obs):.3f}")

print("Poisson:"); ppc(y_c, y_rep_p)
```

```output
Poisson:
  mean       observed   3.73 | replicated 90% [  3.24,   4.24] | p_B 0.497
  var/mean   observed   4.83 | replicated 90% [  0.75,   1.27] | p_B 0.000
  frac zeros observed   0.20 | replicated 90% [  0.00,   0.06] | p_B 0.000
  max        observed  22.00 | replicated 90% [  7.00,  11.00] | p_B 0.000
```

The mean passes with $p_B = 0.497$, which proves nothing, because the model was fitted to reproduce it.
The three assumption-driven statistics all fail at $p_B = 0.000$. The observed dispersion ratio of 4.83
is far outside the replicated range of 0.75 to 1.27. One day in five has zero failures, where the Poisson
expects at most 6%, and a maximum of 22 sits above anything the model replicates (90% band 7 to 11). Each
failure also points at the fix: the data need a variance larger than the mean.

### Posterior predictive p-values are conservative

This subsection shows that $p_B$ cannot be read like a classical p-value, because it rarely gets extreme
even when it should. The data are used twice: once to fit $\theta$, once to judge it. Unlike a classical
p-value, $p_B$ is therefore not uniform when the model is true. It is pulled toward 0.5, and the pull is
strongest for statistics close to what was fitted. The simulation below draws 400 datasets *from the
Poisson model itself* and records $p_B$ for three statistics. A calibrated p-value would put 10% of its
mass outside $[0.05, 0.95]$ and have sd $1/\sqrt{12} = 0.289$.

```python
R, S_small = 400, 500
pb = {name: np.empty(R) for name in ("mean", "var/mean", "max")}
for r in range(R):
    y_sim = rng.poisson(4.0, n_c)                                        # the model is TRUE here
    lam_r = rng.gamma(a0 + y_sim.sum(), 1 / (b0 + n_c), S_small)
    rep = rng.poisson(lam_r[:, None], (S_small, n_c))
    for name in pb:
        T = T_stats[name]
        t_rep, t_obs = T(rep), T(y_sim)
        pb[name][r] = np.mean(t_rep > t_obs) + 0.5 * np.mean(t_rep == t_obs)   # mid-p for ties
for name, v in pb.items():
    print(f"T = {name:9} sd(p_B) {v.std():.3f} (uniform 0.289) | "
          f"P(p_B < 0.05 or > 0.95) {np.mean((v < .05) | (v > .95)):.3f} (uniform 0.100)")
```

```output
T = mean      sd(p_B) 0.022 (uniform 0.289) | P(p_B < 0.05 or > 0.95) 0.000 (uniform 0.100)
T = var/mean  sd(p_B) 0.277 (uniform 0.289) | P(p_B < 0.05 or > 0.95) 0.095 (uniform 0.100)
T = max       sd(p_B) 0.263 (uniform 0.289) | P(p_B < 0.05 or > 0.95) 0.068 (uniform 0.100)
```

When the model is true, $p_B$ for the mean has sd 0.022 and never leaves $[0.05, 0.95]$, so it cannot
reject anything. The dispersion ratio is nearly calibrated (sd 0.277, 9.5% extreme), because the Poisson
fit does not tune it. The maximum is in between (6.8% extreme), slightly conservative and lumpy because
it is discrete. The practical rule: a $p_B$ near 0 or 1 is strong evidence of misfit, while a $p_B$ near
0.5 is weak evidence of fit unless $T$ is roughly ancillary. Treat PPCs as a search for *how* the model
is wrong, not as a test with a size.

## Fixing the likelihood: negative binomial

The fix is to let each day have its own hidden rate. If busy and quiet days have different $\lambda$,
then counts pooled over days vary more than any single Poisson would allow. The negative binomial is a
Gamma mixture of Poissons: $y \mid \mu, \phi \sim \mathrm{NB}(\mu, \phi)$ with $\mathbb{E}y = \mu$ and
$\mathrm{Var}\,y = \mu + \mu^2/\phi$. Small $\phi$ means strong overdispersion, and $\phi \to \infty$
recovers the Poisson. Two parameters fit comfortably on a grid (ch. 1), with priors
$\log\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\log 3, 1)$ and $\log\phi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1.5)$. You also keep the grid's
log-likelihood matrix, because exact leave-one-out on a grid is just a reweighting.

```python
def nb_logpmf(y, mu, phi):
    return stats.nbinom.logpmf(y, phi, phi / (phi + mu))

lmu_g, lphi_g = np.linspace(np.log(2), np.log(8), 150), np.linspace(np.log(0.2), np.log(20), 150)
LMU, LPHI = [a.ravel() for a in np.meshgrid(lmu_g, lphi_g, indexing="ij")]
ll_grid_nb = nb_logpmf(y_c[:, None], np.exp(LMU), np.exp(LPHI))          # (n, G)
log_prior_nb = stats.norm.logpdf(LMU, np.log(3), 1) + stats.norm.logpdf(LPHI, 0, 1.5)
lp_nb = ll_grid_nb.sum(0) + log_prior_nb
w_nb = np.exp(lp_nb - logsumexp(lp_nb))
cell = rng.choice(LMU.size, S, p=w_nb)
d_mu, d_phi = lmu_g[1] - lmu_g[0], lphi_g[1] - lphi_g[0]
mu_s = np.exp(LMU[cell] + rng.uniform(-d_mu / 2, d_mu / 2, S))          # jitter within the cell
phi_s = np.exp(LPHI[cell] + rng.uniform(-d_phi / 2, d_phi / 2, S))
print(f"mu  posterior mean {mu_s.mean():.2f}, 95% [{np.quantile(mu_s, .025):.2f}, {np.quantile(mu_s, .975):.2f}]")
print(f"phi posterior mean {phi_s.mean():.2f}, 95% [{np.quantile(phi_s, .025):.2f}, {np.quantile(phi_s, .975):.2f}]")
y_rep_nb = stats.nbinom.rvs(phi_s[:, None], (phi_s / (phi_s + mu_s))[:, None], size=(S, n_c), random_state=rng)
print("NegBin:"); ppc(y_c, y_rep_nb)
```

```output
mu  posterior mean 3.76, 95% [2.93, 4.80]
phi posterior mean 1.03, 95% [0.66, 1.53]
NegBin:
  mean       observed   3.73 | replicated 90% [  2.75,   4.97] | p_B 0.502
  var/mean   observed   4.83 | replicated 90% [  2.85,   7.47] | p_B 0.406
  frac zeros observed   0.20 | replicated 90% [  0.11,   0.33] | p_B 0.597
  max        observed  22.00 | replicated 90% [ 13.00,  33.00] | p_B 0.377
```

The dispersion $\phi$ has posterior mean 1.03, far from the Poisson limit $\phi \to \infty$, and it is
confidently identified (95% interval 0.66 to 1.53). Every statistic now lands mid-distribution, with
$p_B$ between 0.38 and 0.60. The mean's posterior interval also widened from $[3.32, 4.18]$ to
$[2.93, 4.80]$. The Poisson was not only wrong about the tails. It was also overconfident about the
quantity it fitted.

## LOO-PIT: a calibration check that does not double-dip

Ask, for each day: before seeing it, where did the model expect today's count to fall? If the model is
honest, the real counts should land at random percentiles of its forecasts: as often in the bottom 20% as
in the top 20%. The probability integral transform (PIT) turns that into a check, and it fixes the double
use of data by predicting each point from the others. If $F_{-i}$ is the leave-one-out predictive CDF,
$u_i = F_{-i}(y_i)$ is uniform when the model is calibrated. Counts need the randomised version,
$u_i = F_{-i}(y_i - 1) + v_i\,p_{-i}(y_i)$ with $v_i \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$ a random tie-breaker. A
U-shape means predictive intervals that are too narrow (underdispersion), and a hump means intervals that
are too wide.

Both models allow exact LOO here. For the Poisson, $\lambda \mid y_{-i}$ is again Gamma, so
$p(\tilde y \mid y_{-i})$ is negative binomial in closed form. On the NB grid, dividing the posterior
weights by $p(y_i\mid\theta)$ and renormalising *is* the posterior given $y_{-i}$.

```python
a_i, b_i = a0 + y_c.sum() - y_c, b0 + n_c - 1                           # Gamma posterior without y_i
cdf_p = lambda t: stats.nbinom.cdf(t, a_i, b_i / (b_i + 1))             # Poisson-Gamma predictive
w_loo = w_nb[None, :] * np.exp(-ll_grid_nb)                             # posterior given y_{-i}, on the grid
w_loo /= w_loo.sum(1, keepdims=True)
cdf_nb = lambda t: np.sum(w_loo * stats.nbinom.cdf(t[:, None], np.exp(LPHI), np.exp(LPHI) / (np.exp(LPHI) + np.exp(LMU))), 1)
v = rng.uniform(size=n_c)
for name, F in (("Poisson", cdf_p), ("NegBin", cdf_nb)):
    lo, hi = F(y_c - 1), F(y_c)
    u = lo + v * (hi - lo)
    hist = np.histogram(u, bins=5, range=(0, 1))[0]
    print(f"{name:8} LOO-PIT counts per fifth {hist} (expect 16 each) | outside [0.05, 0.95]: "
          f"{np.mean((u < .05) | (u > .95)):.3f} (expect 0.10) | KS p {stats.kstest(u, 'uniform').pvalue:.3f}")
```

```output
Poisson  LOO-PIT counts per fifth [35 11  9  7 18] (expect 16 each) | outside [0.05, 0.95]: 0.412 (expect 0.10) | KS p 0.000
NegBin   LOO-PIT counts per fifth [15 17 16 15 17] (expect 16 each) | outside [0.05, 0.95]: 0.113 (expect 0.10) | KS p 0.999
```

The Poisson's LOO-PIT is U-shaped. 35 of 80 days fall in the bottom fifth and 18 in the top, and 41% land
outside the central 90% band, where 10% is expected. Its predictive intervals are far too narrow at both
ends. The negative binomial is flat (15 to 17 per bin, 11.3% outside, KS $p$ 0.999). LOO-PIT checks the
whole predictive distribution at once, and the leave-one-out step avoids the conservativeness of $p_B$.

## Predictive accuracy as the target

Grade a forecaster by the probability she gave to what actually happened. If she said 30% and it rained,
she scores $\log 0.3$. Add those log scores up over many days and you have the yardstick used for the
rest of the chapter. To compare models you need a score like this, and the Bayesian default is the
**expected log pointwise predictive density** for new data $\tilde y_i$ from the true process $p_t$:

$$
\mathrm{elpd} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \int p_t(\tilde y_i)\,\log p(\tilde y_i \mid y)\,d\tilde y_i,
\qquad p(\tilde y_i \mid y) = \int p(\tilde y_i\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta .
$$

The log score is the only proper local scoring rule, and it rewards the whole predictive distribution,
not just a point forecast. You do not know $p_t$. The naive plug-in reuses the observed data, giving the
**log pointwise predictive density**, computed from $S$ draws as

$$
\mathrm{lppd} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} p(y_i\mid\theta^{(s)}) .
$$

The lppd is optimistic, because each $y_i$ helped choose the $\theta$ that is now scoring it. The bias
grows with the effective number of parameters. Every criterion below is an estimate of this optimism.

### The classical corrections, briefly

This shows the two older penalties, each a "fit minus complexity" estimate. AIC uses
$\log p(y\mid\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}) - k$, with $k$ the parameter count. DIC uses
$\log p(y\mid\bar\theta) - p_{\mathrm{DIC}}$, with posterior mean $\bar\theta$ and
$p_{\mathrm{DIC}} = 2\big(\log p(y\mid\bar\theta) - \mathbb{E}_{\text{post}}\log p(y\mid\theta)\big)$,
which shrinks when the prior regularises. Both plug in a single point, which fails when that point is a
poor summary (multimodality, constraints).

### WAIC derived from a cumulant expansion

This derivation shows that a point's optimism is, to second order, the posterior variance of its
log-likelihood. Write $L_i = \log p(y_i\mid\theta)$ as a random variable under the posterior, with mean
$\mu_i$ and variance $\sigma_i^2$. The cumulant generating function gives
$\log\mathbb{E}[e^{tL_i}] = t\mu_i + \tfrac{t^2}{2}\sigma_i^2 + O(t^3)$. At $t = 1$ this is the lppd
term. At $t = -1$ it is the *leave-one-out* term, because by Bayes' rule

$$
p(y_i\mid y_{-i}) = \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\mid y}\big[1/p(y_i\mid\theta)\big]}
\;\Rightarrow\; \log p(y_i\mid y_{-i}) = -\log\mathbb{E}[e^{-L_i}] \approx \mu_i - \tfrac12\sigma_i^2 .
$$

Subtracting, $\log \mathbb{E}[e^{L_i}] - \log p(y_i\mid y_{-i}) \approx \sigma_i^2$. So the optimism of
the lppd for point $i$ is approximately the posterior variance of its log-likelihood:

$$
\boxed{\;\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{WAIC}} = \mathrm{lppd} - p_{\mathrm{WAIC}},
\qquad p_{\mathrm{WAIC}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{Var}_{\theta\mid y}\big[\log p(y_i\mid\theta)\big]\;}
$$

Watanabe proved the asymptotic equivalence with LOO for singular models too. It is fully Bayesian (it
averages over the posterior and uses no point estimate) and pointwise. It fails when a single
$\sigma_i^2$ is large, which is exactly when the expansion is invalid.

## All the criteria against the truth

The fair test of a penalty is to generate data where you know the true out-of-sample score, then see
which estimate lands on it. A conjugate Normal-inverse-Gamma regression gives closed forms for
everything: posterior draws, the evidence, and the predictive distribution, which is Student-t. So you
can refit $n$ times for exact LOO and compute the true elpd on a large test set from the known generator.
The models use the first $k$ of 10 covariates, and only the first four have nonzero effects.

```python
def nig_fit(X, y, tau=1.0, a=2.0, b=2.0):
    """w | s2 ~ N(0, s2 tau^2 I), s2 ~ InvGamma(a, b). Returns posterior params and log evidence."""
    n, k = X.shape
    Vn = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X + np.eye(k) / tau**2)
    mn = Vn @ X.T @ y
    an, bn = a + n / 2, b + 0.5 * (y @ y - mn @ (X.T @ X + np.eye(k) / tau**2) @ mn)
    log_ev = (-n / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi) + 0.5 * np.linalg.slogdet(Vn)[1] - k * np.log(tau)
              + a * np.log(b) - an * np.log(bn) + gammaln(an) - gammaln(a))
    return dict(Vn=Vn, mn=mn, an=an, bn=bn, log_ev=log_ev)

def nig_pred_logpdf(f, Xnew, ynew):                       # Student-t posterior predictive
    scale = np.sqrt(f["bn"] / f["an"] * (1 + np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Xnew, f["Vn"], Xnew)))
    return stats.t.logpdf(ynew, 2 * f["an"], Xnew @ f["mn"], scale)

def nig_draws(f, S, rng):
    s2 = 1 / rng.gamma(f["an"], 1 / f["bn"], S)
    z = rng.standard_normal((S, len(f["mn"]))) @ np.linalg.cholesky(f["Vn"]).T
    return f["mn"] + np.sqrt(s2)[:, None] * z, s2

def exact_loo(X, y, **kw):
    idx = np.arange(len(y))
    return np.array([nig_pred_logpdf(nig_fit(X[idx != i], y[idx != i], **kw), X[i:i+1], y[i:i+1])[0] for i in idx])

n_r, w_true = 40, np.array([1.0, 0.6, 0.3, 0.15, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0])
X_te = rng.standard_normal((20_000, 10))
y_te = X_te @ w_true + rng.standard_normal(20_000)
ks, R_ds, S_r = (1, 2, 4, 7, 10), 40, 2000
crit = {c: np.zeros((R_ds, len(ks))) for c in ("lppd", "AIC", "DIC", "WAIC", "LOO", "true")}
for r in range(R_ds):                                       # 40 independent training sets
    X_r = rng.standard_normal((n_r, 10))
    y_r = X_r @ w_true + rng.standard_normal(n_r)
    for j, k in enumerate(ks):
        X = X_r[:, :k]
        f = nig_fit(X, y_r)
        W, s2 = nig_draws(f, S_r, rng)
        ll = stats.norm.logpdf(y_r, W @ X.T, np.sqrt(s2)[:, None])          # (S, n) pointwise log-lik
        lppd_i = logsumexp(ll, 0) - np.log(S_r)
        beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y_r, rcond=None)[0]
        crit["lppd"][r, j] = lppd_i.sum()
        crit["AIC"][r, j] = stats.norm.logpdf(y_r, X @ beta, np.sqrt(np.mean((y_r - X @ beta) ** 2))).sum() - (k + 1)
        ll_bar = stats.norm.logpdf(y_r, X @ W.mean(0), np.sqrt(s2.mean())).sum()
        crit["DIC"][r, j] = ll_bar - 2 * (ll_bar - ll.sum(1).mean())
        crit["WAIC"][r, j] = lppd_i.sum() - ll.var(0, ddof=1).sum()
        crit["LOO"][r, j] = exact_loo(X, y_r).sum()
        crit["true"][r, j] = n_r * nig_pred_logpdf(f, X_te[:, :k], y_te).mean()   # elpd on fresh data
print("averages over 40 training sets (elpd scale, higher is better)")
print(f"{'k':>2}" + "".join(f"{c:>8}" for c in crit) + f"{'sd(LOO-true)':>14}")
for j, k in enumerate(ks):
    print(f"{k:>2}" + "".join(f"{v[:, j].mean():8.2f}" for v in crit.values())
          + f"{(crit['LOO'][:, j] - crit['true'][:, j]).std():14.2f}")
print(f"LOO picks the true-elpd-best k in {np.mean(crit['LOO'].argmax(1) == crit['true'].argmax(1)):.2f} of datasets")
```

```output
averages over 40 training sets (elpd scale, higher is better)
 k    lppd     AIC     DIC    WAIC     LOO    true  sd(LOO-true)
 1  -63.44  -65.32  -65.21  -65.28  -65.30  -65.19          4.28
 2  -58.12  -60.89  -60.75  -60.85  -60.89  -60.10          3.45
 4  -55.51  -60.11  -59.90  -60.20  -60.35  -59.17          3.83
 7  -54.33  -61.59  -61.30  -62.01  -62.44  -60.72          4.68
10  -52.88  -62.72  -62.31  -63.54  -64.57  -62.76          6.10

```

Read across a row. The lppd is always too high, and the gap to the truth grows with $k$: 1.75 nats at
$k=1$ and 9.88 at $k=10$, roughly one nat per parameter plus one for the noise variance. AIC, DIC, WAIC
and exact LOO all remove most of that gap, and all of them pick $k=4$ on average, as does the truth. At
$k=10$ AIC and DIC are close to the truth on average, while WAIC and LOO are slightly pessimistic, by 0.8
and 1.8 nats. But look at the last column. On any *single* dataset the LOO estimate misses the true elpd
by 3.5 to 6 nats, which is more than the gaps between models. So LOO picks the truly best $k$ in only 35%
of datasets. Every estimate of elpd is noisy, and the noise has to go into the comparison (below). The
later sections reuse the last training set, `X_r, y_r`.

## Importance-sampling LOO and Pareto smoothing

Exact LOO means refitting $n$ times. The shortcut is to reuse the one posterior you have and reweight its
draws so that point $i$ counts as if it had never been seen. Draws that fit $y_i$ badly get *more*
weight, because without $y_i$ they would have been more plausible. Refitting $n$ times is cheap only for
conjugate models. The identity in the WAIC derivation gives LOO from one fit: the posterior is the
proposal and $p(\theta\mid y_{-i})$ the target, so the importance ratios are
$r_i^{(s)} \propto 1/p(y_i\mid\theta^{(s)})$ (ch. 3) and

$$
p(y_i\mid y_{-i}) \approx \frac{\sum_s r_i^{(s)}\,p(y_i\mid\theta^{(s)})}{\sum_s r_i^{(s)}}
= \frac{1}{\frac{1}{S}\sum_s 1/p(y_i\mid\theta^{(s)})} .
$$

That is a harmonic mean, and its variance can be infinite. When $y_i$ is influential the full posterior
is much narrower than the leave-$i$-out one, so the proposal has lighter tails than the target, which is
the failure mode you met in ch. 3. **PSIS** (Vehtari, Gelman and Gabry 2017; Vehtari et al. 2024) fits a
generalised Pareto distribution to the largest $M = \min(S/5,\, 3\sqrt{S})$ ratios. It replaces them with
quantiles of the fit, truncates at the raw maximum, and reports the shape $\hat k$ (the same diagnostic
as ch. 3). Here is the reference algorithm: the Zhang–Stephens profile estimator, with a weak prior that
shrinks $\hat k$ toward 0.5. It is checked on ch. 3's Gaussian test, where $k = 1 - \sigma_q^2$.

```python
def gpd_fit(x):
    """Zhang & Stephens (2009) GPD fit to sorted exceedances x, with PSIS's prior on k."""
    n = len(x)
    m = 30 + int(np.sqrt(n))
    b = 1 - np.sqrt(m / (np.arange(1, m + 1) - 0.5))
    b = b / (3 * x[int(n / 4 + 0.5) - 1]) + 1 / x[-1]
    k = np.log1p(-b[:, None] * x).mean(1)
    L = n * (np.log(-b / k) - k - 1)                       # profile log-likelihood for each b
    wts = 1 / np.exp(L[None, :] - L[:, None]).sum(1)
    b_post = np.sum(b * wts) / wts.sum()
    k_post = np.log1p(-b_post * x).mean()
    sigma = -k_post / b_post
    return (n * k_post + 10 * 0.5) / (n + 10), sigma        # prior: 10 pseudo-obs at k = 0.5

def psis(log_r):
    """Pareto-smoothed log weights (normalised) and khat for one observation."""
    S_ = len(log_r)
    lw = log_r - log_r.max()
    M = int(np.ceil(min(0.2 * S_, 3 * np.sqrt(S_))))
    order = np.argsort(lw)
    tail_idx, cutoff = order[-M:], lw[order[-M - 1]]
    k, sigma = gpd_fit(np.exp(lw[tail_idx]) - np.exp(cutoff))
    p = (np.arange(M) + 0.5) / M
    smoothed = np.log(np.exp(cutoff) + sigma / k * np.expm1(-k * np.log1p(-p)))
    lw[tail_idx] = np.minimum(smoothed, 0.0)                # never exceed the largest raw weight
    return lw - logsumexp(lw), k

for sig in (0.8, 0.7, 0.5):                                 # ch. 3's test: N(0,1) target, N(0, sig^2) proposal
    x = rng.normal(0, sig, S)
    log_r = stats.norm.logpdf(x) - stats.norm.logpdf(x, 0, sig)
    lw, k = psis(log_r)
    raw = np.exp(log_r - logsumexp(log_r))
    print(f"sigma_q {sig}: khat {k:.2f} (theory {1 - sig**2:.2f}) | E[x^2] raw IS {raw @ x**2:.3f}, "
          f"PSIS {np.exp(lw) @ x**2:.3f} (truth 1)")

def loo_estimates(ll):
    """ll: (S, n). Returns pointwise raw-IS LOO, PSIS-LOO and khat."""
    is_loo = -(logsumexp(-ll, 0) - np.log(ll.shape[0]))
    res = [psis(-ll[:, i]) for i in range(ll.shape[1])]
    psis_loo = np.array([logsumexp(lw + ll[:, i]) for i, (lw, _) in enumerate(res)])
    return is_loo, psis_loo, np.array([k for _, k in res])
```

sigma_q 0.7: khat 0.30 (theory 0.51) | E[x^2] raw IS 0.901, PSIS 0.899 (truth 1)

When the true $k$ is 0.36 or 0.51, the fit reads low (0.25, 0.30). That is the prior pulling toward 0.5

## Differences, standard errors, and ties

Two students sit the same exam. Do not compare their total scores with each one's own uncertainty.
Compare them question by question, because a hard question is hard for both. The elpd of a single model
has a standard error, but what matters for comparison is the *paired* difference. Both models score the
same $y_i$, so you take pointwise differences
$d_i = \widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{A,i} - \widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{B,i}$ and

$$
\widehat{\Delta} = \sum_i d_i, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\widehat\Delta) = \sqrt{n\,\mathrm{Var}(d_i)} .
$$

This SE is usually far smaller than either model's own SE, because the hard and easy points are shared.
Rules of thumb: $|\Delta| < 4$ is a tie whatever the SE, and the SE itself is only trustworthy when $n$
is not tiny and the models are not badly misspecified. Treat a difference within about two SEs as noise.

```python
def compare(name, a, b):
    d = a - b
    se = np.sqrt(len(d) * d.var(ddof=1))
    print(f"{name:32} diff {d.sum():+7.2f} | SE {se:5.2f} | diff/SE {d.sum() / se:+5.1f} | "
          f"SE(A) alone {np.sqrt(len(a) * a.var(ddof=1)):5.2f}")

loo_pois = stats.nbinom.logpmf(y_c, a_i, b_i / (b_i + 1))                    # exact, closed form

```

## Bayes factors versus LOO: prior sensitivity

Suppose you give a model a ridiculously vague prior on one coefficient, $\pm 1000$. Before seeing data,
it spreads its bets so thin that it predicted the actual data poorly. The Bayes factor punishes exactly
that. After seeing even a few points, the vague prior no longer matters, and LOO does not care. The Bayes
factor compares **prior** predictive performance: the evidence
$p(y\mid M) = \int p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta\mid M)\,d\theta$ scores how well the model predicted the data
before seeing any of it. LOO compares **posterior** predictive performance. The difference is practical.
For one extra coefficient with prior sd $\tau$, the evidence carries an Occam factor of roughly
$\sigma_{\text{post}}/\tau$, so every tenfold widening of a vague prior costs $\log 10 \approx 2.3$ nats.
That is Lindley's paradox (ch. 3). The LOO predictive conditions on $n-1$ points, and the prior washes
out.

```python
print(f"{'tau':>6} {'log BF(k=4 vs 3)':>17} {'LOO diff':>9}")
for tau in (0.5, 1, 10, 100, 1000):
    log_bf = nig_fit(X_r[:, :4], y_r, tau=tau)["log_ev"] - nig_fit(X_r[:, :3], y_r, tau=tau)["log_ev"]
    d = exact_loo(X_r[:, :4], y_r, tau=tau).sum() - exact_loo(X_r[:, :3], y_r, tau=tau).sum()
    print(f"{tau:>6g} {log_bf:17.2f} {d:9.2f}")
```

```output
   tau  log BF(k=4 vs 3)  LOO diff
   0.5             -1.26     -1.09
     1             -1.94     -1.22
    10             -4.24     -1.27
   100             -6.54     -1.27
  1000             -8.85     -1.27
```

From $\tau = 10$ on, the log Bayes factor falls by $\log 10 = 2.30$ per decade: $-4.24$, $-6.54$,
$-8.85$. With a vague enough prior you can make the evidence reject the $k = 4$ model as strongly as you
like, without touching the data. The LOO difference moves from $-1.09$ to $-1.27$ and then freezes. Bayes
factors are the right tool when the priors are genuine scientific statements about effect sizes. With
default "weakly informative" priors they measure the prior as much as the model. LOO measures predictive
performance and stays put.

## Stacking predictive distributions

If three forecasters each watch a different part of the sky, the best forecast mixes them, even if one of
them is individually best. Picking that one throws information away. When you cannot pick a winner, you
can average. **Bayesian model averaging** weights model $k$ by its posterior probability
$p(M_k\mid y) \propto p(y\mid M_k)\,p(M_k)$. That is the right answer only in the $\mathcal{M}$-closed
case, where one candidate is true. Then BMA weights converge to 1 on that model. When no candidate is
true ($\mathcal{M}$-open), BMA still converges to a single model, the one closest in KL divergence, even
when a mixture would predict better. *Pseudo-BMA* swaps the evidence for LOO,
$w_k \propto \exp(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo},k})$, and inherits the same winner-take-all
behaviour.

**Stacking** (Yao, Vehtari, Simpson and Gelman 2018) optimises the weights for prediction directly. It
maximises the LOO log score of the mixture over the simplex:

$$
\hat w = \arg\max_{w \in \Delta_K} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\, p(y_i\mid y_{-i}, M_k).
$$

The objective is concave in $w$. It is the log-likelihood of a mixture with *fixed* components, so the EM
update $w_k \leftarrow \frac1n\sum_i w_k p_{ik} / \sum_j w_j p_{ij}$ climbs to the global optimum. The
test below uses three covariates of equal strength, and each candidate model sees only one of them.

```python
n_s, rng_s = 300, np.random.default_rng(18)
X_s, X_st = rng_s.standard_normal((n_s, 3)), rng_s.standard_normal((20_000, 3))
y_s = X_s @ [0.8, 0.7, 0.6] + rng_s.standard_normal(n_s)
y_st = X_st @ [0.8, 0.7, 0.6] + rng_s.standard_normal(20_000)
P_loo = np.column_stack([np.exp(exact_loo(X_s[:, [k]], y_s)) for k in range(3)])      # (n, K)
P_test = np.column_stack([np.exp(nig_pred_logpdf(nig_fit(X_s[:, [k]], y_s), X_st[:, [k]], y_st)) for k in range(3)])
log_ev = np.array([nig_fit(X_s[:, [k]], y_s)["log_ev"] for k in range(3)])

w_stack = np.full(3, 1 / 3)
for _ in range(2000):                                       # EM for fixed-component mixture weights
    w_stack = (w_stack * P_loo / (P_loo @ w_stack)[:, None]).mean(0)
elpd_k = np.log(P_loo).sum(0)
weights = {"BMA (evidence)": np.exp(log_ev - logsumexp(log_ev)),
           "pseudo-BMA (LOO)": np.exp(elpd_k - logsumexp(elpd_k)),
           "stacking": w_stack,
           "best single": np.eye(3)[np.argmax(elpd_k)]}
print(f"elpd_loo per model {np.round(elpd_k, 2)} | log evidence {np.round(log_ev, 2)}")
for name, w in weights.items():
    print(f"{name:17} weights {np.round(w, 3)} | test log score per obs {np.log(P_test @ w).mean():.4f}")
```

```output
elpd_loo per model [-526.43 -541.76 -545.95] | log evidence [-530.02 -545.42 -549.7 ]
BMA (evidence)    weights [1. 0. 0.] | test log score per obs -1.7202
pseudo-BMA (LOO)  weights [1. 0. 0.] | test log score per obs -1.7202
stacking          weights [0.623 0.281 0.096] | test log score per obs -1.6957

```

Model 1 wins the elpd comparison by 15 nats, so BMA and pseudo-BMA both give it weight 1.000 and score
exactly like the best single model: $-1.7202$ per test point. Stacking keeps all three, with weights
0.623, 0.281 and 0.096, and scores $-1.6957$. That is 0.025 nats per observation better, or about 7 nats
over 300 points, and the gap grows with $n$ because BMA only concentrates harder. A mixture of three
one-covariate predictives is wider than any one of them, which suits data that none of them explains
alone. Stacking is not model selection. Its weights say which combination predicts best, not which model
is true.
