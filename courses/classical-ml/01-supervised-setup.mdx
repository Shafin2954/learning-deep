## What supervised learning is asking

Supervised learning means learning from worked answers. You get a pile of questions that
already have the right answer written next to them, and you want a rule that answers *new*
questions correctly.

You have $N$ training examples $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn i.i.d. from some unknown
joint distribution $P(X, Y)$. You want to find a function $f: X \to Y$ that predicts
well on **new** examples from the same distribution — not just the training set.

Here each $x_i$ is an **input** (the question: a house's size, a photo, a patient's lab
values) and each $y_i$ is its **label** (the answer: the price, "cat", "sick"). **i.i.d.**
means *independent and identically distributed* — every example is a fresh, separate draw
from the same source, like separate rolls of the same die.

> **Picture it:** a student preparing for an exam with a book of past papers and their
> answers. The goal is not to memorise the past papers. The goal is to pass *next year's*
> paper, which is written by the same examiner but has different questions.

This single sentence contains four hidden assumptions worth unpacking before touching data:
i.i.d., same distribution, predicts well (needs a loss), and new examples (generalization).
Classical ML is largely the study of when these assumptions hold and what breaks when they don't.

> **Watch out:** "same distribution" is the assumption real projects break most often. A
> model trained on last year's customers and used on this year's is quietly betting that
> nothing changed.

## Loss functions — the thing you actually minimize

A loss is a score for one mistake: zero when the guess is perfect, bigger when the guess is
worse. Training a model means turning its knobs until the total of these scores is small.

A **loss function** $\ell(y, \hat{y})$ measures the cost of predicting $\hat{y}$ when the
true label is $y$. Different tasks need different losses:

$$
\ell_{\text{MSE}}(y, \hat{y}) = (y - \hat{y})^2 \qquad \text{(regression)}
$$

> **In plain words:** **mean squared error (MSE)** — subtract the guess from the truth and
> square it. Off by 2 costs 4; off by 10 costs 100.

$$
\ell_{\text{log}}(y, \hat{y}) = -y \log \hat{y} - (1-y) \log(1-\hat{y}) \qquad \text{(binary classification)}
$$

> **In plain words:** **log loss** (also called **cross-entropy**) — $\hat{y}$ is the
> predicted probability of class 1. If the truth is 1, the cost is $-\log \hat{y}$: tiny when
> you said 0.99, huge when you said 0.01. Confident wrong answers are punished hardest.

$$
\ell_{0/1}(y, \hat{y}) = \mathbf{1}[y \neq \hat{y}] \qquad \text{(0–1 loss, non-differentiable)}
$$

> **In plain words:** **0–1 loss** — cost 1 if the predicted class is wrong, 0 if it is
> right. Counting these gives the error rate. The bold $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is an
> *indicator*: 1 when the statement inside is true, 0 otherwise.

The choice of loss encodes your beliefs about which errors matter and how much. MSE penalizes
large errors quadratically — outliers dominate. MAE ($|y - \hat{y}|$) is more robust.
Cross-entropy pushes the model to produce calibrated probabilities, not just rankings.

> **Picture it:** a loss is the fine schedule for a traffic court. MSE fines ten times the
> speed-over squared, so one wild driver can dominate the day's total. **MAE** (mean
> absolute error) fines in proportion, so one wild driver counts only as much as their
> actual overshoot.

## Risk — what we really want to minimize

**Risk** is the average loss. The risk you care about is the average over *all* future
examples; the risk you can compute is the average over the examples you have. Most of
machine learning lives in the gap between those two numbers.

The **expected risk** (or true risk) is the expected loss under the data distribution:

$$
R(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim P}[\ell(y, f(x))]
$$

> **In plain words:** if you could draw infinitely many fresh examples $(x, y)$ from the
> real world $P$, run your rule $f$ on each, and average the losses, you would get $R(f)$.
> The symbol $\mathbb{E}$ means "average over all possibilities".

We can't compute $R(f)$ because $P$ is unknown. Instead we minimize the **empirical risk** —
the average loss on the training set:

$$
\hat{R}(f) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ell(y_i, f(x_i))
$$

> **In plain words:** add up the loss on each of your $N$ training examples and divide by
> $N$. This is the number your training code actually prints.

**Empirical risk minimization (ERM)** is the foundation of most supervised learning. It says:
find $f$ that minimizes $\hat{R}(f)$. The central question is: does a small $\hat{R}$ imply
a small $R$? The answer is: sometimes, and the gap between them is what we call
**generalization error**.

The code below makes the gap visible. It draws 50 noisy points from a sine wave, fits
polynomials of degree 1, 5, and 15, and scores each on the 50 training points and on 500
fresh test points from the same source. The test score stands in for the true risk.

```python
import numpy as np

# Simulate the gap between training loss and true risk
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N_train = 50
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, N_train)
y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, N_train)

# Fit polynomial of degree d to x, y
for d in [1, 5, 15]:
    coeffs = np.polyfit(x, y, d)
    train_preds = np.polyval(coeffs, x)
    train_mse = np.mean((y - train_preds) ** 2)

    # Evaluate on fresh test points (the "true" distribution)
    x_test = rng.uniform(0, 1, 500)
    y_test = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x_test) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 500)
    test_preds = np.polyval(coeffs, x_test)
    test_mse = np.mean((y_test - test_preds) ** 2)

    print(f"degree {d:2d}: train MSE = {train_mse:.4f}  test MSE = {test_mse:.4f}")
```

```output
degree  1: train MSE = 0.3042  test MSE = 0.2895
degree  5: train MSE = 0.0881  test MSE = 0.1018
degree 15: train MSE = 0.0543  test MSE = 0.1396
```

Read the two columns side by side. Training MSE falls every time the degree rises: 0.3042,
then 0.0881, then 0.0543. Test MSE does not follow: it falls from 0.2895 to 0.1018, then
*rises* to 0.1396 at degree 15. The degree-15 curve has the lowest training loss but a
worse test loss than degree 5: it bent itself to fit the training noise. That's
**overfitting** — learning the accidents of your sample instead of the pattern.

> **Watch out:** the noise here has standard deviation 0.3, so the best possible MSE is
> $0.3^2 = 0.09$. Degree 15's training MSE of 0.0543 is *below* that floor — a sure sign it
> is fitting noise. A training score better than the noise level is a warning, not a win.

## Bias–variance decomposition

A model can be wrong in two different ways. It can be **stubbornly wrong** — the same
mistake every time, because it is too simple to bend to the truth. Or it can be **jumpy** —
right on average, but its answer changes a lot depending on which training set it happened
to see.

For squared loss, the expected test error of any estimator $\hat{f}$ decomposes as:

$$
\mathbb{E}[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2] = \underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f^*(x))^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+ \underbrace{\text{Var}[\hat{f}(x)]}_{\text{variance}}
+ \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}
$$

> **In plain words:** imagine retraining the same kind of model on many different training
> sets and asking each one for a prediction at the same point $x$.
> - $f^*(x)$ is the true answer at $x$.
> - **bias$^2$** is how far the *average* of those predictions sits from the truth, squared.
> - **variance** is how much the individual predictions scatter around their own average.
> - **irreducible noise** $\sigma^2$ is the randomness baked into $y$ itself.

- **Bias**: does the model class contain the truth? A linear model fitting a nonlinear function
  has high bias regardless of how much data you have.
- **Variance**: how much does $\hat{f}$ change across different training sets? A degree-15
  polynomial fitted to 50 points swings wildly; fitted to 5000 points it stabilises.
- **Irreducible noise** $\sigma^2$: the best possible error — no model can beat it.

> **Picture it:** archers at a target. Bias is how far the *centre* of an archer's arrows
> lands from the bullseye. Variance is how widely the arrows spread around that centre. A
> simple model is a steady hand aimed at the wrong spot; a flexible model aims at the
> bullseye with a shaky hand.

You can't measure bias and variance from one dataset, but a simulation can. The code fits a
straight line and a degree-9 polynomial to 200 different training sets of 30 points each,
and records each fit's prediction at $x = 0.25$, the top of the sine wave, where the true
value is 1.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_datasets = 200
N = 30

bias2_d1, var_d1 = [], []
bias2_d9, var_d9 = [], []

for _ in range(n_datasets):
    x = rng.uniform(0, 1, N)
    y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, N)
    x0 = 0.25  # evaluate at a fixed point (the peak of the sine)

    for d, b_list, v_list in [(1, bias2_d1, var_d1), (9, bias2_d9, var_d9)]:
        coeffs = np.polyfit(x, y, d)
        b_list.append(np.polyval(coeffs, x0))

truth = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.25)   # f*(0.25) = sin(pi/2) = 1
print(f"degree 1 : bias²={( np.mean(bias2_d1)-truth)**2:.4f}  var={np.var(bias2_d1):.4f}")
print(f"degree 9 : bias²={(np.mean(bias2_d9)-truth)**2:.4f}  var={np.var(bias2_d9):.4f}")
```

```output
degree 1 : bias²=0.2695  var=0.0180
degree 9 : bias²=0.0026  var=0.1941
```

The straight line has bias$^2$ = 0.2695: on average it predicts about 0.48 instead of 1,
because a line cannot reach up to the hump of a sine wave. Its variance is small (0.0180) —
every line looks much the same. Degree 9 is the mirror image: bias$^2$ is nearly gone
(0.0026), but variance is about ten times larger (0.1941), because each fit wiggles
differently. The sweet spot is somewhere between — and finding it is what regularization
and cross-validation are for.

## Capacity and the Occam's razor principle

**Capacity** is how many different shapes a model can bend into. More capacity lets you fit
the training data better, but it also gives the model more room to fit noise.

The **hypothesis class** $\mathcal{H}$ is the set of functions your model can express.
A linear model has low capacity; a degree-15 polynomial has high capacity. The more
expressive $\mathcal{H}$ is, the smaller the empirical risk can be made — but the larger
the generalization gap tends to become.

> **Picture it:** a hypothesis class is a box of rulers. A low-capacity box holds only
> straight rulers. A high-capacity box also holds bendy rulers that can trace any curve —
> including the random jitter between points.

**Occam's razor**: among models that fit the data equally well, prefer the simpler one.
In practice this is implemented via:
- Regularization (penalty on model complexity).
- Cross-validation (measuring test performance directly).
- Explicit capacity control (max depth in trees, architecture size in NNs).

You'll meet regularization in ch. 3, tree depth in ch. 10, and cross-validation is the
measuring stick used throughout the course.

## Recap in plain words

- Supervised learning learns a rule from labelled examples and is judged on *new* examples.
- A loss scores one prediction; MSE punishes big misses hardest, log loss punishes confident wrong probabilities.
- Empirical risk is the training average of the loss; true risk is the average over the whole world, which you can only estimate on held-out data.
- Training loss keeps falling as a model gets more flexible, but test loss eventually rises: that is overfitting.
- Test error splits into bias$^2$ (stubbornly wrong), variance (jumpy), and noise (unfixable).
- Simpler models win ties; regularization, cross-validation, and capacity limits are how you enforce that.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial import polynomial as P

# Simulate bias-variance at different training sizes
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
true_fn = lambda x: np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)

for N in [20, 100, 500]:
    preds = []
    for _ in range(100):
        x = rng.uniform(0, 1, N)
        y = true_fn(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.2, N)
        coeffs = np.polyfit(x, y, 5)
        preds.append(np.polyval(coeffs, 0.5))
    truth = true_fn(0.5)
    print(f"N={N:4d}: bias²={(np.mean(preds)-truth)**2:.5f}  var={np.var(preds):.5f}")
```

```output
N=  20: bias²=0.00024  var=0.00735
N= 100: bias²=0.00000  var=0.00135
N= 500: bias²=0.00000  var=0.00037
```

This fixes the model (a degree-5 polynomial) and grows the training set. Bias$^2$ is already
tiny at $N = 20$ (0.00024) and rounds to zero after that. Variance is what more data buys
you: it drops from 0.00735 at $N = 20$ to 0.00135 at $N = 100$ and 0.00037 at $N = 500$ —
roughly 20 times smaller for 25 times more data. More data tames variance; it does nothing
for bias.

Try changing the degree from 5 to 1. You should see bias$^2$ stay large at every $N$, because no
amount of data makes a straight line curve.

Argue in one paragraph (no code required): why does the 0–1 loss function ($\mathbf{1}[y
\neq \hat{y}]$) not break the ERM framework conceptually, yet cause practical problems when

> **Hint:** picture nudging a model's weights a tiny bit. How often does the number of
> misclassified points change? What does that mean for the slope that gradient descent relies on?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Conceptually, 0–1 loss is fine: $\hat{R}(f) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \mathbf{1}[y_i \neq \hat{y}_i]$
is a well-defined empirical risk. The practical problem is that it is **piecewise constant**:
its gradient with respect to the model's parameters is zero almost everywhere, and undefined
at the decision boundary. Gradient-based optimisation (gradient descent) needs a gradient
signal to update parameters. So in practice we minimise a **surrogate loss** that is convex
and differentiable: hinge loss (SVM), cross-entropy (logistic regression/softmax), or
exponential loss (AdaBoost). These upper-bound the 0–1 loss and have useful gradients.
Minimising the surrogate tends to minimise the 0–1 loss too, though the connection is
approximate.

The key insight: a small weight change almost never flips a prediction, so the error count
is a flat staircase. A surrogate loss is a smooth ramp laid over that staircase, and a ramp
always tells you which way is downhill. You'll meet each surrogate in its own chapter:
cross-entropy (ch. 4), hinge loss (ch. 9), exponential loss (ch. 12).
</details>
