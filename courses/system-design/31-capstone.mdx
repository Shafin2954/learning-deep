## The project

Build a working key-value service that contains, from scratch, the machinery this course spent
thirty chapters explaining: consistent-hashing sharding, replication with quorum reads and
writes, an LRU cache with stampede protection, a token-bucket rate limiter, a Bloom filter
guarding the store, and a client with retries, backoff, jitter, and a circuit breaker. Then load
test it, break it deliberately, and write the design document that explains every number you
chose. Finally, design one large system entirely on paper, at the same level of rigour, without
writing any of its code.

The service is the proof that you can build the primitives. The design document is the proof
that you know what they cost.

## Hard constraints (no vibe coding)

- **No AI assistance of any kind.** Not for the code, not for the design document, not for
  debugging. If you get stuck, re-read the chapter.
- You MAY NOT use a library that implements the primitive you are being asked to build. No
  `redis`, `memcached`, `hashring`, `pybloom`, `ratelimit`, `pybreaker`, `tenacity`, or
  `backoff` for the core implementations.
- You MAY use the standard library (`hashlib`, `bisect`, `threading`, `asyncio`, `socket`,
  `sqlite3`, `http.server`), plus a web framework (FastAPI/Flask) and a load-testing tool of
  your choice for the harness only.
- You MAY use `redis` or a real database **only** as a comparison baseline in the benchmark
  section, clearly labelled as such — never as part of the implementation being graded.
- The nodes must be real processes communicating over a real transport (HTTP, or TCP sockets).
  A single-process simulation with function calls does not count.
- Every number in the design document must come from a measurement or from an
  explicitly-stated back-of-envelope calculation. No adjectives without numbers.

## What it must contain (and the chapter each tests)

1. **A consistent-hash ring with virtual nodes** (ch. 4). Support `add_node`, `remove_node`,
   `get_node(key)`. Demonstrate that adding the $n$-th node moves approximately $1/n$ of keys and
   that load spread stays within 15% of even with 128+ virtual nodes per physical node.
2. **A replicated store with tunable quorums** (ch. 13, 16). Each key is stored on `N=3` nodes;
   `W` and `R` are per-request parameters. Show a stale read with `W=1, R=1` and its absence with
   `W=2, R=2`, and implement read repair.
3. **An LRU cache with TTL, jitter, single-flight, and stale-while-revalidate** (ch. 5, 6).
   Measure the hit rate on a Zipf workload and demonstrate that a hot-key expiry produces one
   origin fetch, not hundreds.
4. **A Bloom filter** (ch. 7) in front of the store, sized for a stated false-positive target.
   Report measured versus theoretical false-positive rate on at least 100,000 probes.
5. **A token-bucket rate limiter** (ch. 20), per API key, with correct `429` responses carrying
   `Retry-After` and `RateLimit-*` headers, and weighted costs for expensive endpoints.
6. **A resilient client** (ch. 21, 22): timeouts, retries only on retryable errors, exponential
   backoff with full jitter, a retry budget, a circuit breaker with half-open probes, and a
   propagated deadline.
7. **Bounded queues and load shedding** (ch. 22): a bounded request queue with priority shedding
   under overload, and a documented degradation order.
8. **Observability** (ch. 23): RED metrics per endpoint, a latency histogram (not pre-computed
   percentiles), structured logs with a request id, and one SLO with an error budget and a
   burn-rate alert rule.
9. **A load test and a failure test** (ch. 2, 19, 22). Measure throughput and p50/p99/p99.9 at
   several concurrency levels; then, while under load: kill one node, partition one node, make
   one node slow (add 2 s of latency), and fill the cache with a scan. Record what happened to
   availability, latency, and correctness in each case.
10. **A design document** covering: requirements as numbers, the API, the data model and key
    design, the sharding and replication scheme, the consistency model you chose and what it
    means for a client (ch. 16), the failure modes and degradation behaviour, capacity planning,
    and an explicit list of what you did **not** build and why.
11. **A paper design of one large system** (ch. 30) — pick one you have not built: a ride-hailing
    dispatcher, a flash-sale ticketing system, a multi-region collaborative editor, a metrics
    backend, or your own RAG or e-invoicing product. Same document structure as item 10, plus a
    section on the two hardest trade-offs and how you resolved them.

## Acceptance criteria

- **Sharding.** Adding a 5th node to a 4-node ring moves between 15% and 25% of 100,000 keys
  (ideal 20%), and the busiest node holds no more than 1.15x the mean.
- **Quorums.** With `N=3, W=2, R=2`, no stale read occurs in 10,000 read-after-write trials while
  one node is partitioned. With `W=1, R=1` you can demonstrate at least one stale read and
  explain the exact interleaving that produced it.
- **Cache.** Hit rate above 80% on a Zipf($\alpha \approx 1.0$) workload with a cache holding 5%
  of the keyspace. A synchronized expiry of one hot key under 500 concurrent readers results in
  exactly one origin fetch.
- **Bloom filter.** Measured false-positive rate within 20% (relative) of the theoretical value
  for your chosen $m$ and $k$; zero false negatives across all probes.
- **Rate limiter.** Sustained rate within 5% of the configured limit over a 60-second run; bursts
  up to the configured capacity are admitted; every rejection carries a `Retry-After` that, if
  honoured, results in a successful retry.
- **Resilience.** With one node made 2 s slow, p99 for requests that do not touch that node
  degrades by less than 20% (this is the bulkhead working), and the circuit breaker opens within
  10 seconds and recovers within 30 seconds of the node's recovery.
- **Load test.** A throughput/latency table at concurrency 1, 8, 64, and 256, with p50, p99, and
  p99.9 for each, plus the saturation point identified and explained.
- **Correctness under failure.** No acknowledged write is lost in any of the four failure tests.
  If your design permits loss, you must state exactly which writes and why, before running the
  test.
- **Design document.** Every architectural decision has a "because" clause citing a number.
  A reader who has not seen your code can predict its behaviour under a 10x traffic increase.
