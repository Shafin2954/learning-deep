## The unlabeled data problem

Labelling millions of photos by hand is slow and costly, but unlabelled photos are everywhere.
**Self-supervised learning (SSL)** invents its own practice questions from the images — "are
these two crops from the same photo?", "what was under this blacked-out patch?" — and learns
useful features by answering them.

Labels are expensive. Most images in the world have no annotation. Self-supervised learning extracts supervisory signal from the data itself — from structure that exists without human labels. A good self-supervised model learns representations that transfer to downstream tasks (classification, detection, segmentation) with far fewer labelled examples. (A **representation** or **embedding** is the vector of features a network produces for an image; a **downstream task** is the real job you care about afterwards.)

> **Picture it:** learning a language by doing fill-in-the-blank puzzles from books, with no
> teacher. Nobody tells you what words mean, but after thousands of puzzles you understand
> them well enough to learn a new task (say, translation) from a handful of examples.

Two dominant paradigms emerged:
1. **Contrastive learning** — push representations of related views together, unrelated apart.
2. **Masked autoencoding** — reconstruct masked parts of the input.

## SimCLR: contrastive with InfoNCE

SimCLR's practice question is a matching game: take one photo, make two different random edits
of it (ch. 7 augmentations), and train the network so the two edits land close together while
all other photos in the batch land far away.

SimCLR (Chen 2020) creates two augmented views of the same image and maximises agreement in embedding space:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{InfoNCE}(i, j) = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_k)/\tau)}
$$

where $\text{sim}$ is cosine similarity and $\tau$ is a temperature. The negative examples come from other images in the batch.

> **In plain words:**
> - $z_i$ and $z_j$ are the embeddings of the two views of one image (a **positive pair**).
> - $z_k$ runs over every other embedding in the batch except $z_i$ itself; all of them except
>   $z_j$ are **negatives**.
> - **Cosine similarity** is 1 for vectors pointing the same way and $-1$ for opposite ones.
> - The fraction is a softmax: "what share of $z_i$'s attention goes to its true partner?".
>   The loss is minus the log of that share, so it is small when the partner wins clearly.
> - The **temperature** $\tau$ sharpens ($\tau$ small) or softens ($\tau$ large) the competition.

In code this is a cross-entropy where, for row $i$, the "correct class" is the index of its
partner. Rows $0..B-1$ are view 1 and rows $B..2B-1$ are view 2, so row $i$'s partner is $i + B$.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def simclr_loss(z: torch.Tensor, temperature: float = 0.5) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    z: (2*B, D) — first B rows are view1, next B rows are view2
    """
    B = z.size(0) // 2
    z = F.normalize(z, dim=1)

    # Similarity matrix (2B, 2B)
    sim = torch.mm(z, z.T) / temperature

    # Mask self-similarities
    mask = torch.eye(2 * B, dtype=torch.bool, device=z.device)
    sim.masked_fill_(mask, float("-inf"))

    # Positive pairs: (i, i+B) and (i+B, i)
    labels = torch.cat([torch.arange(B, 2*B), torch.arange(B)]).to(z.device)
    return F.cross_entropy(sim, labels)

torch.manual_seed(0)
B, D = 32, 128
z = torch.randn(2 * B, D)
loss = simclr_loss(z)
print(f"SimCLR loss (random): {loss.item():.3f}")
print(f"Chance level (all sims equal): {-torch.tensor(1/(2*B-1)).log().item():.3f}")
```

```output
SimCLR loss (random): 4.163
Chance level (all sims equal): 4.143
```

With random embeddings the loss is 4.163, almost exactly the chance level 4.143. That second
number is $\ln(2B - 1) = \ln 63$: the loss you get when the partner is no more similar than any
of the 63 other candidates. A trained encoder pushes the loss well below it.

The loss is cross-entropy where the positive pair is the "correct class" among the $2B - 1$
candidates ($2B - 2$ negatives plus the one partner). Lower = more agreement.

### The collapse problem

**Collapse** is the cheating solution where the network maps every image to the same vector.
Then every pair looks "similar", and a loss that only rewarded agreement would be perfect.

For InfoNCE, collapse does not pay: if every embedding is the same, the partner is no more
similar than the negatives, and the loss sits at the chance level $\ln(2B-1)$ — the 4.143 above.
The negatives are what stop collapse, which is why SimCLR wants a large batch (4096+ images):
more negatives, harder and more informative comparisons. SimCLR also adds a projection head
(2-layer MLP) that is discarded after pretraining. The loss is computed on the head's output,
so the head can throw away information the loss does not need (such as colour, which the
augmentations randomise); the encoder's features before the head keep more of it and
transfer better.

> **Watch out:** the batch size is part of the loss. Halving the batch halves the negatives
> and changes the chance level, so loss values are not comparable across batch sizes.

## MoCo: momentum encoder

MoCo gets many negatives without a giant batch: it keeps a long queue of embeddings from
earlier batches. To keep those old embeddings comparable with new ones, they come from a second
encoder that changes very slowly.

Contrastive loss needs many negatives. SimCLR uses a large batch. **MoCo** maintains a queue of negative keys from past batches, using a slowly-updated **momentum encoder** (EMA of the main encoder) to keep key representations consistent:

$$
\theta_k \leftarrow m \cdot \theta_k + (1-m) \cdot \theta_q \quad (m \approx 0.999)
$$

> **In plain words:** $\theta_q$ are the weights of the main (query) encoder, trained by
> gradient descent; $\theta_k$ are the key encoder's weights. Each step, the key encoder moves
> only $1 - m = 0.1\%$ of the way toward the main encoder. This running blend is an **EMA**
> (exponential moving average).

> **Picture it:** a long-exposure photo of a moving car. The key encoder is a blurred,
> slow-changing average of the recent main encoders, so keys computed 50 batches ago still
> roughly match today's.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import copy

class MoCoEncoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base_encoder: nn.Module, dim=128, m=0.999, K=4096):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder_q = base_encoder
        self.encoder_k = copy.deepcopy(base_encoder)
        for p in self.encoder_k.parameters():
            p.requires_grad = False
        self.m = m
        self.K = K
        self.register_buffer("queue", F.normalize(torch.randn(dim, K), dim=0))
        self.register_buffer("queue_ptr", torch.zeros(1, dtype=torch.long))

    @torch.no_grad()
    def _momentum_update(self):
        for pq, pk in zip(self.encoder_q.parameters(),
                          self.encoder_k.parameters()):
            pk.data = pk.data * self.m + pq.data * (1 - self.m)

encoder = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 128))
moco    = MoCoEncoder(encoder)
print("MoCo keys queue:", moco.queue.shape)
moco._momentum_update()
print("Momentum update applied without crash.")
```

```output
MoCo keys queue: torch.Size([128, 4096])
Momentum update applied without crash.
```

The queue holds 4,096 keys of 128 numbers each, stored as columns and normalised to length 1.
It is a buffer, not a parameter: it is saved with the model but never trained. The momentum
update runs under `@torch.no_grad()`, since the key encoder gets no gradients.

## BYOL: no negative pairs

BYOL drops the negatives entirely: one network tries to predict what a slowly-moving copy of
itself outputs for another view of the same image. A few asymmetries keep it from collapsing.

**BYOL** (Bootstrap Your Own Latent, Grill 2020) showed that you do not need negatives at all. It uses two networks:
- **Online network**: predicts the target.
- **Target network**: EMA of online; not directly trained.

A stop-gradient on the target side prevents collapse. Without the stop-gradient, all outputs go to the same constant. (A **stop-gradient** treats a value as a fixed constant during backpropagation, like `.detach()` in PyTorch.)

$$
\mathcal{L} = 2 - 2 \cdot \frac{\langle q_\theta(z_\theta), z_\xi \rangle}{\|q_\theta(z_\theta)\| \cdot \|z_\xi\|}
$$

> **In plain words:** $q_\theta(z_\theta)$ is the online network's prediction; $z_\xi$ is the
> target network's projection of the other view. The fraction is their cosine similarity, so
> the loss is $2 - 2\cos$: 0 when they point the same way, 2 when they are at right angles,
> and 4 when they are opposite.

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F, copy

def byol_loss(online_pred: torch.Tensor, target_proj: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    online_pred   = F.normalize(online_pred, dim=-1)
    target_proj   = F.normalize(target_proj, dim=-1)
    return 2 - 2 * (online_pred * target_proj).sum(dim=-1).mean()

torch.manual_seed(0)
online = F.normalize(torch.randn(16, 128))
target = F.normalize(torch.randn(16, 128))
print(f"BYOL loss (random):  {byol_loss(online, target).item():.3f}")
print(f"BYOL loss (perfect): {byol_loss(online, online.detach()).item():.3f}")
```

```output
BYOL loss (random):  1.953
BYOL loss (perfect): 0.000
```

Two random 128-dimensional unit vectors are nearly at right angles, so the random loss is close
to 2 (here 1.953). Comparing a vector with itself gives cosine 1 and loss 0.000.

> **Watch out:** a BYOL loss near 0 early in training is a warning sign, not a success. It
> usually means the outputs have collapsed to a constant. Track the standard deviation of the
> embeddings across a batch; if it falls toward 0, the features are dead.

## MAE: Masked Autoencoder (He 2021)

MAE's practice question is a jigsaw with most pieces missing: hide three quarters of the image
patches and ask the network to paint them back in.

MAE takes a ViT and masks 75% of patches randomly. Only unmasked tokens pass through the encoder. A lightweight decoder reconstructs pixel values of the masked patches. The task: predict pixel values of hidden patches. (An **autoencoder** compresses an input and rebuilds it; "masked" means part of the input is hidden.)

Why 75% (not 50%): lower mask ratios are easy (the model cheats from neighboring patches). 75% forces true understanding.

> **Picture it:** guessing a painting when only one stamp-sized square in four is visible.
> With half the squares you could fill gaps by copying neighbours; with a quarter, you must
> know what dogs and faces look like.

The masking trick: draw a random number per patch, sort, and keep the patches with the
smallest numbers. Sorting random noise gives a fresh random subset for every image.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def mask_patches(tokens: torch.Tensor, mask_ratio: float = 0.75):
    """tokens: (B, N, D). Returns visible, masked indices."""
    B, N, D = tokens.shape
    n_mask   = int(N * mask_ratio)
    n_keep   = N - n_mask

    noise   = torch.rand(B, N)
    ids_shuf = torch.argsort(noise, dim=1)
    ids_keep = ids_shuf[:, :n_keep]           # indices to keep

    visible = torch.gather(tokens, 1,
        ids_keep.unsqueeze(-1).expand(-1, -1, D))
    return visible, ids_keep, ids_shuf[:, n_keep:]

tokens  = torch.randn(2, 196, 768)   # 196 patches, ViT-B dim
vis, keep_ids, mask_ids = mask_patches(tokens, 0.75)
print(f"Total patches: 196  |  Visible: {vis.shape[1]}  |  Masked: {mask_ids.shape[1]}")
```

```output
Total patches: 196  |  Visible: 49  |  Masked: 147
```

Of 196 patches, 49 stay visible and 147 are hidden: $196 \times 0.75 = 147$.

The encoder runs on only 49 tokens (4 times fewer tokens than the full ViT). The decoder is 4 to 8 times smaller than the encoder, and it sees masked tokens as learned mask embeddings. (Attention itself, which grows with the square of the token count, gets $4^2 = 16$ times cheaper.)

## DINO / DINOv2

DINO trains a student network to give the same answer as a teacher network, where the teacher
is a slow average of the student. Nobody provides labels; the two networks teach each other.

**DINO** (Caron 2021) uses self-distillation: student and teacher are both ViTs, teacher is EMA of student. No class labels — the student learns to match the teacher's output distribution on different crops. Emergent property: DINO's attention maps naturally segment objects without any segmentation training.

**DINOv2** scales this up with curated data and shows that foundation vision models can emerge from SSL alone, matching supervised models on most benchmarks. (A **foundation model** is one large pretrained model reused for many tasks.)

> **Picture it:** a student copying the class's best notes, where those best notes are a
> rolling average of the student's own past notes. The trick that makes it work is that the
> student sees small crops of the image while the teacher sees large ones, so the student must
> infer the whole scene from a part.

## Linear probe evaluation

To grade an SSL model, you freeze it and fit the simplest classifier on its features (ch. 6).
If a straight line separates the classes, the features did the hard work.

To measure representation quality without fine-tuning: freeze the backbone, train only a linear classifier on top.

`nn.Identity()` replaces the head with a layer that returns its input, so the backbone outputs
its 2,048 pooled features.

```python
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
import numpy as np
import torchvision.models as models
import torch

# Use a pretrained ResNet as a stand-in for a self-supervised model
backbone = models.resnet50(weights=models.ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V2)
backbone.fc = nn.Identity()   # remove classification head
backbone.eval()

# Random data as proxy for downstream task
torch.manual_seed(0); np.random.seed(0)
X_raw = torch.randn(200, 3, 112, 112)
y_raw = np.random.randint(0, 5, 200)

with torch.no_grad():
    feats = backbone(X_raw).numpy()

clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=200)
clf.fit(feats, y_raw)
print(f"Linear probe accuracy: {clf.score(feats, y_raw):.2%}")
print(f"Feature dim: {feats.shape[1]}")
```

```output
Linear probe accuracy: 79.50%
Feature dim: 2048
```

The probe scores 79.50% — but on random labels, measured on the same 200 images it was trained
on. That number is memorisation: 2,048 features are plenty to fit 200 arbitrary labels. The
honest probe accuracy here would be about 20% (chance for 5 classes) on held-out images.

> **Watch out:** a linear probe is only meaningful on a held-out split with real labels. Report
> test accuracy, as in the published numbers below.

Good SSL representations achieve linear probe accuracy within 5–10% of full fine-tuning on ImageNet (e.g., DINO ViT-B/8: about 80% linear probe vs about 84% fine-tuned).

## When self-supervised beats supervised

SSL pays off when you have lots of images but few labels, or images unlike ImageNet.

- **Large unlabelled corpus, small labelled set**: SSL learns general features from unlabelled, adapts with few labels.
- **Domain shift**: SSL on your domain (medical, satellite) beats ImageNet-supervised transfer. (**Domain shift** means your images look systematically different from the pretraining images.)
- **Few-shot**: linear probe or 1-NN from SSL features often outperforms supervised on rare classes. (**Few-shot** = only a handful of labelled examples per class; **1-NN** labels an image by its single nearest neighbour in feature space.)

## Recap in plain words

- Self-supervised learning creates its own training signal from unlabelled images.
- Contrastive methods (SimCLR, MoCo) pull two views of one image together and push other images away; negatives prevent collapse.
- MoCo keeps a queue of old negatives encoded by a slow EMA copy of the encoder.
- BYOL and DINO use no negatives; an EMA teacher plus a stop-gradient keeps them from collapsing.
- MAE hides 75% of patches and learns by reconstructing them, running the encoder on visible patches only.
- Linear probes measure feature quality, but only on held-out data with real labels.

## Try it Yourself

The SimCLR loss on two kinds of fake embeddings: pairs where view 2 is view 1 plus a little
noise, and fully random pairs.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def simclr_loss(z, temperature=0.5):
    B = z.size(0) // 2
    z = F.normalize(z, dim=1)
    sim = torch.mm(z, z.T) / temperature
    mask = torch.eye(2*B, dtype=torch.bool)
    sim.masked_fill_(mask, float("-inf"))
    labels = torch.cat([torch.arange(B, 2*B), torch.arange(B)])
    return F.cross_entropy(sim, labels)

# Simulate embeddings from similar vs random views
torch.manual_seed(0)
B, D = 16, 64
z1     = torch.randn(B, D)
z_good = torch.cat([z1, z1 + torch.randn(B, D) * 0.1])   # view2 = view1 + small noise
z_rand = torch.randn(2 * B, D)
print(f"SimCLR loss (similar views): {simclr_loss(z_good):.3f}")
print(f"SimCLR loss (random):        {simclr_loss(z_rand):.3f}")
```

```output
SimCLR loss (similar views): 1.627
SimCLR loss (random):        3.350
```

The similar-views loss (1.627) is about half the random one (3.350). Random pairs sit near the
chance level for $B = 16$, which is $\ln 31 \approx 3.43$; similar pairs are easy to pick out, so
the partner wins most of the softmax.

Try decreasing temperature from 0.5 to 0.1 and observe how sharply the loss distinguishes the two cases.

## Exercise

Implement the MAE reconstruction loss: given a model that produces pixel-level predictions for masked patches (`pred`, shape `(B, N_masked, P*P*C)`) and the true pixel values (`target`, same shape), compute the normalized per-patch MSE where each patch is normalized by its own mean and variance before computing loss. This is the exact MAE loss.

> **Hint:** take the mean and variance over the last dimension (the pixels of one patch) with
> `keepdim=True`, so they broadcast back over that patch. Add a small `eps` before the square root.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import torch

def mae_loss(pred: torch.Tensor, target: torch.Tensor, eps: float = 1e-6) -> torch.Tensor:
    """
    pred, target: (B, N_masked, P*P*C)
    Normalize target per patch, then MSE.
    """
    mean = target.mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    var  = target.var(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    target_norm = (target - mean) / (var + eps).sqrt()
    return ((pred - target_norm) ** 2).mean()

B, N_masked, patch_dim = 4, 147, 16 * 16 * 3   # 75% of 196, 16×16 RGB
torch.manual_seed(0)
pred   = torch.randn(B, N_masked, patch_dim)
target = torch.randn(B, N_masked, patch_dim)

loss = mae_loss(pred, target)
print(f"MAE reconstruction loss: {loss.item():.4f}")

# Perfect reconstruction
print(f"Perfect recon loss:       {mae_loss(target, target).item():.4f}")
```

Normalizing per patch removes absolute pixel intensity, making the model focus on relative texture and shape within each patch rather than global brightness — a key reason MAE representations generalize well.

Running it prints about 2.0 for random predictions: two independent standard-normal values
differ by a squared amount of $1 + 1 = 2$ on average. The "perfect" line prints 0.0019, not
exactly 0, because it compares the *raw* target with the *normalised* target. Since this
random target is already almost mean 0 and variance 1, the two nearly match. A model that
output the normalised target would score exactly 0.

## Under the Trench

Rare and advanced topics you should at least know the name of. You won't need them daily,
but you'll stop being surprised when you meet them.

- **Deformable & dilated convolutions** — flexible/large receptive fields; deformable conv learns offsets for the sampling grid, so the kernel can bend to fit an object's shape.
- **Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN)** — multi-scale feature maps for detecting objects of all sizes; the "FPN" in the ch. 9 detectors.
- **Keypoint & pose estimation** — heatmap regression over joint locations; used in HRNet and MediaPipe. The network paints one "probability blob" per joint (elbow, knee).
- **Optical flow & video models** — PWC-Net, RAFT for dense flow; 3D CNNs and video transformers for temporal modeling. Optical flow is the per-pixel motion between two frames.
- **NeRF & 3D / point clouds** — neural radiance fields for view synthesis; PointNet for unordered 3D point sets.
- **OCR pipelines** — CRNN (detection + CTC recognition); Tesseract and PaddleOCR as baselines. OCR (optical character recognition) reads text out of images.
- **Grad-CAM & saliency maps** — visualize which pixels drive a CNN's prediction; useful for debugging and trust.
- **Model compression for vision (MobileNet, depthwise-separable)** — edge inference with < 5 MB models; the ch. 5 blocks plus pruning and quantisation.
