## What you are actually predicting

A recommender's job is to pick a handful of things to show one person, in order, from a pile
far too big to show. The product question is "which ten things go on this screen, top to
bottom?", not "what score would this person give this item?".

A recommender does not predict ratings. It produces an **ordered short list** of items for a user,
under a budget — ten slots on a home page, three in a carousel, one in a notification. Everything
that follows in this course is a way of filling those slots, and most of the mistakes come from
optimising something else and hoping it transfers.

> **Picture it:** a shop window with ten hooks. The shop owns a thousand products. Nobody cares how
> well the owner could guess a customer's opinion of product 731; what matters is which ten go on
> the hooks, and which one goes at eye level.

Two words you will see on every page. A **user** is the person you recommend to; an **item** is
anything you could show them (a film, a song, a product). An **interaction** is one logged event
between them: a click, a play, a purchase. The **interaction matrix** is a giant table with one
row per user and one column per item, where a cell is filled if that user touched that item.

The block below builds a synthetic year of logs that behaves like real data. Each user has a
hidden **taste vector** (four numbers describing what they like), each item has a **style vector**,
and the chance of an interaction grows when the two point the same way. Some users are far more
active than others, popularity is **heavy-tailed** (a few items are enormously popular, most are
not), a quarter of items launch partway through the year, and tastes drift month to month.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import sparse

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_users, n_items = 3_000, 1_200

# latent taste: users and items each live in a small space
user_taste = rng.normal(0, 1, (n_users, 4))
item_style = rng.normal(0, 1, (n_items, 4))
activity = rng.lognormal(-0.5, 1.1, n_users)                  # some users do far more than others
exposure = rng.lognormal(-1.0, 1.8, n_items)                  # popularity is heavy-tailed
launch_day = rng.integers(0, 365, n_items) * (rng.random(n_items) < 0.25)   # 25% launch later

months = []
for month in range(12):                                       # a year, one month at a time
    day0 = month * 30
    user_taste = user_taste + rng.normal(0, 0.25, (n_users, 4))   # taste drifts
    live = launch_day <= day0
    logit = (user_taste @ item_style.T / 2 - 4.2
             + np.log(activity)[:, None] + np.log(exposure)[None, :])
    prob = np.where(live[None, :], 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit)), 0.0) / 12
    r, c = np.nonzero(rng.random((n_users, n_items)) < prob)
    months.append(pd.DataFrame({"user": r, "item": c,
                                "ts": day0 + rng.integers(0, 30, len(r))}))

events = pd.concat(months).sort_values("ts").reset_index(drop=True)
print(f"{n_users:,} users x {n_items:,} items = {n_users * n_items:,} cells")
print(f"observed interactions: {len(events):,} "
      f"({len(events.drop_duplicates(['user', 'item'])):,} distinct user-item pairs)")
print(f"density: {len(events) / (n_users * n_items):.4%}")
```

```output
3,000 users x 1,200 items = 3,600,000 cells
observed interactions: 84,392 (75,955 distinct user-item pairs)
density: 2.3442%
```

Read the three lines. There are 3,600,000 possible user-item cells, but only 84,392 logged
events, and only 75,955 distinct user-item pairs (some users came back to the same item). The
density on the last line is the share of cells with an event: 2.34%.

That density is the defining fact of the field. **Over 97% of the matrix is missing** — and real
catalogues are two orders of magnitude sparser still. The missing entries are not missing at
random — a user has not seen most items, and the ones they have
seen were shown to them by a previous recommender.

> **Watch out:** an empty cell does not mean "this user dislikes this item". It usually means
> "this user never saw this item". Treating the 97% of empty cells as zeros-that-mean-no is the
> first big mistake in recommender work, and the rest of this course keeps coming back to it.

## Explicit and implicit feedback are different problems

There are two kinds of signal you can learn from. Either the user tells you how much they liked
something, or you watch what they do and guess.

**Explicit feedback** is a direct statement of preference, like a 1-to-5 star rating. **Implicit
feedback** is behaviour you infer preference from: clicks, plays, purchases, time spent. The table
compares them.

| | Explicit (ratings) | Implicit (clicks, plays, purchases) |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | graded preference, 1–5 stars | binary presence, unbounded counts |
| Negatives | a low rating is a real negative | **absence is not a negative** |
| Volume | rare; users rate a fraction of what they consume | abundant; every interaction is logged |
| Bias | who chooses to rate, and when | what was shown, and how it was ranked |
| Objective | reconstruct the observed entries | rank all items, including unobserved ones |

> **Picture it:** explicit feedback is a restaurant review card the customer chose to fill in.
> Implicit feedback is the till receipt. You get a receipt from everyone, but it never tells you
> what they hated; you get a card from a few people, and those people are not typical customers.

The next block turns the first 20,000 events into star ratings. The rating is the user-item taste
match plus noise, centred around 3.4 and clipped to the 1–5 range.

```python
ratings = pd.DataFrame({
    "user": events["user"].values[:20_000],
    "item": events["item"].values[:20_000],
})
noise = rng.normal(0, 0.8, len(ratings))
latent = (user_taste[ratings["user"]] * item_style[ratings["item"]]).sum(axis=1) / 2
ratings["stars"] = np.clip(np.round(3.4 + latent + noise), 1, 5)
print(ratings["stars"].value_counts().sort_index().to_string())
print(f"\nmean rating {ratings['stars'].mean():.3f}")
print(f"share of the matrix with a rating: {len(ratings) / (n_users * n_items):.4%}")
```

```output
stars
1.0     651
2.0    1827
3.0    3996
4.0    5255
5.0    8271

mean rating 3.933
share of the matrix with a rating: 0.5556%
```

Look at the counts: 8,271 fives against 651 ones, a mean of 3.933. The ratings pile up at the top.
And the last line shows how rare ratings are: even if every one of the first 20,000 events had
been rated, only 0.56% of the matrix would carry a star.

Ratings skew high because people mostly rate things they chose to consume. A model that predicts
ratings well is answering "how much would this user like this item *if they watched it*" — which
is not the question the product asks.

> **Watch out:** a model with a low rating error can still make a bad recommendation list. Rating
> error only measures the cells users chose to fill, and those cells are mostly items they already
> expected to like.

## The interaction matrix, stored properly

Almost every cell is empty, so you should only store the filled ones. Writing down the full table
wastes memory on millions of zeros.

A **sparse matrix** stores only the non-zero cells. The **CSR** format ("compressed sparse row")
keeps three arrays: `data` (the values), `indices` (the column of each value) and `indptr` (where
each row starts and ends inside the other two). The number of stored values is called **nnz**
("number of non-zeros").

> **Picture it:** instead of a 3,000-page notebook with a 1,200-box grid on every page, you keep one
> short shopping list per user: "items 4, 81, 902". `indptr` is the table of contents telling you on
> which line each user's list starts.

```python
def to_csr(df, n_users, n_items, value=1.0):
    return sparse.csr_matrix((np.full(len(df), value), (df["user"], df["item"])),
                             shape=(n_users, n_items))

M = to_csr(events, n_users, n_items)
M.data[:] = 1.0
print(f"CSR matrix {M.shape}, {M.nnz:,} stored values")
print(f"dense would need {n_users * n_items * 8 / 1e6:.1f} MB; "
      f"CSR needs {(M.data.nbytes + M.indices.nbytes + M.indptr.nbytes) / 1e6:.1f} MB")
per_user = np.diff(M.indptr)
per_item = np.asarray(M.sum(axis=0)).ravel()
print(f"\ninteractions per user: median {np.median(per_user):.0f} | "
      f"p10 {np.percentile(per_user, 10):.0f} | p99 {np.percentile(per_user, 99):.0f}")
print(f"interactions per item: median {np.median(per_item):.0f} | "
      f"max {per_item.max():.0f} | items with < 5: {(per_item < 5).sum()}")
```

```output
CSR matrix (3000, 1200), 75,955 stored values
dense would need 28.8 MB; CSR needs 0.9 MB

interactions per user: median 19 | p10 5 | p99 110
interactions per item: median 24 | max 975 | items with < 5: 226
```

The first line says 75,955 stored values: duplicates of the same (user, item) pair were summed into
one cell, and the block then set every value to 1.0, so the matrix records "touched or not". The
second line is the memory payoff: 28.8 MB dense against 0.9 MB sparse, a 32-fold saving at only 2%
density. The last two lines show the skew: the median user has 19 interactions but the top 1% have
110 or more; the busiest item has 975 interactions while 226 items have fewer than 5.

Never materialise the dense matrix. At a realistic scale — ten million users, a million items —
the dense form is 80 terabytes and the sparse form is a few gigabytes.

> **Watch out:** many innocent-looking operations silently densify a sparse matrix, for example
> `M.toarray()`, `M + 1`, or subtracting a mean from every cell. On a toy dataset this works; on
> production data it crashes the machine.

## The long tail decides your architecture

A few items get most of the attention and most items get almost none. That shape, called the
**long tail**, decides what a model can and cannot learn.

The **head** is the small set of very popular items; the **tail** is the long list of rarely
touched ones. The block sorts items from most to least popular and asks what share of all
interactions the top slice carries.

> **Picture it:** a bookshop where the ten bestsellers on the front table sell as much as the
> entire back room. The back room is the tail: it is most of the shop, and the till barely hears
> from it.

```python
order = np.argsort(-per_item)
cum = np.cumsum(per_item[order]) / per_item.sum()
for frac in (0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50):
    k = int(frac * n_items)
    print(f"top {frac:>5.0%} of items ({k:>4}) carry {cum[k - 1]:>6.1%} of all interactions")
print(f"\nitems never interacted with: {(per_item == 0).sum()}")
print(f"users with fewer than 5 interactions: {(per_user < 5).sum()} "
      f"({(per_user < 5).mean():.1%})")
```

```output
top    1% of items (  12) carry  12.4% of all interactions
top    5% of items (  60) carry  34.6% of all interactions
top   10% of items ( 120) carry  51.0% of all interactions
top   25% of items ( 300) carry  76.6% of all interactions
top   50% of items ( 600) carry  93.3% of all interactions

items never interacted with: 72
users with fewer than 5 interactions: 257 (8.6%)
```

Read down the first block. The top 1% of items (12 of them) carry 12.4% of interactions; the top
10% carry 51.0%, over half; the top half carries 93.3%. So the bottom 600 items share under 7% of
the signal. Then 72 items were never touched at all, and 257 users (8.6%) have fewer than 5
interactions.

Two consequences you will meet in every later chapter. **Head items dominate the training signal**,
so a model that only learns popularity scores surprisingly well; and **tail items and new users
have almost no data**, which is the cold-start problem (ch. 9) rather than a modelling failure.

## The baseline that beats most first attempts

Before building anything clever, recommend the most popular items the user has not touched yet.
That baseline is embarrassingly hard to beat, so you always measure against it.

Three ideas appear in this block. A **temporal split** trains on everything before a cut-off day
and tests on what happens after, the way the model will be used in real life: you learn from the
past and predict the future. **Recall@k** asks "of the held-out items this user actually touched,
how many made it into my top $k$?"; the block divides by the smaller of $k$ and the number of
held-out items, so a perfect list always scores 1. The **popularity baseline** ranks items by their
training-period interaction count and skips anything the user has already seen.

```python
def temporal_split(events, holdout_days=60):
    cut = events["ts"].max() - holdout_days
    return events[events["ts"] <= cut], events[events["ts"] > cut]

train, test = temporal_split(events)
print(f"train {len(train):,} interactions (days <= {train['ts'].max()}) | "
      f"test {len(test):,} (days > {train['ts'].max()})")

train_pop = np.zeros(n_items)
np.add.at(train_pop, train["item"].values, 1.0)
top_items = np.argsort(-train_pop)

seen = {u: set(g) for u, g in train.groupby("user")["item"]}
truth = {u: set(g) for u, g in test.groupby("user")["item"]}

def recall_at_k(recommend, k=10, users=None):
    users = users if users is not None else list(truth)
    hits, total = 0, 0
    for u in users:
        if not truth[u]:
            continue
        rec = recommend(u, k)
        hits += len(set(rec) & truth[u])
        total += min(k, len(truth[u]))
    return hits / max(total, 1)

def popular_rec(u, k):
    out, i = [], 0
    while len(out) < k and i < len(top_items):
        if top_items[i] not in seen.get(u, ()):        # do not recommend what they already have
            out.append(top_items[i])
        i += 1
    return out

def random_rec(u, k, r=np.random.default_rng(0)):
    return r.choice(n_items, size=k, replace=False)

print(f"\nrecall@10, random     : {recall_at_k(random_rec):.4f}")
print(f"recall@10, popularity : {recall_at_k(popular_rec):.4f}")
```

```output
train 67,285 interactions (days <= 299) | test 17,107 (days > 299)

recall@10, random     : 0.0100
recall@10, popularity : 0.1055
```

The first line shows the split: 67,285 training interactions up to day 299 and 17,107 test
interactions in the last 60 days. Then the comparison. Random lists reach recall@10 of 0.0100 —
close to what chance predicts: 10 random items out of 1,200 catch about 0.8% of a user's held-out
items, and capping the denominator at 10 nudges heavy users slightly above that. Popularity reaches 0.1055, more than ten times better, with zero
personalisation.

Popularity is not a strawman. In many production systems it is within a few points of the deployed
model, and any personalised model that cannot beat it is not earning its serving cost. It is also
the baseline that makes evaluation honest: a metric that cannot separate your model from
popularity is not measuring personalisation.

> **Watch out:** always filter out items the user already interacted with before scoring. If you
> forget, any model that "recommends" the user's own history gets free hits on repeat purchases,
> and the metric rewards a list the user would find useless.

## Recap in plain words

- A recommender fills a few ordered slots; it is a ranking problem, not a rating-prediction problem.
- The user-item table is about 98% empty here, and empty means "never saw it", not "dislikes it".
- Ratings (explicit) are rare and skew positive; clicks and plays (implicit) are plentiful but have no real negatives.
- Store interactions as a sparse CSR matrix: 0.9 MB instead of 28.8 MB here, and the gap grows with scale.
- The top 10% of items carry half the interactions, so popularity alone is a strong model.
- Evaluate with a temporal split and always compare against the popularity baseline.

## Try it Yourself

`dataset_report` summarises any event log in four lines. It adds the **Gini coefficient** of item
popularity, a single number for how unequal the shares are: 0 means every item is equally popular,
1 means one item takes everything. The second call keeps only the events on the 120 most popular
items.

```python
def dataset_report(events, n_users, n_items):
    M = to_csr(events, n_users, n_items)
    per_user, per_item = np.diff(M.indptr), np.asarray(M.sum(axis=0)).ravel()
    gini_x = np.sort(per_item)
    gini = float((2 * np.arange(1, n_items + 1) - n_items - 1).dot(gini_x)
                 / (n_items * gini_x.sum()))
    print(f"users {n_users:,} | items {n_items:,} | interactions {M.nnz:,}")
    print(f"density {M.nnz / (n_users * n_items):.4%} | "
          f"item Gini {gini:.3f} (0 = uniform, 1 = one item takes everything)")
    print(f"cold users (<5): {(per_user < 5).mean():.1%} | "
          f"cold items (<5): {(per_item < 5).mean():.1%}")
    print(f"a random user's list would need {n_items / max(np.median(per_item), 1):.0f}x "
          f"the median item's data to be personalised at the tail")

dataset_report(events, n_users, n_items)
print()
head_only = events[events["item"].isin(np.argsort(-per_item)[:120])]
dataset_report(head_only, n_users, n_items)
```

```output
users 3,000 | items 1,200 | interactions 75,955
density 2.1099% | item Gini 0.693 (0 = uniform, 1 = one item takes everything)
cold users (<5): 8.6% | cold items (<5): 18.8%
a random user's list would need 50x the median item's data to be personalised at the tail

users 3,000 | items 1,200 | interactions 38,745
density 1.0762% | item Gini 0.930 (0 = uniform, 1 = one item takes everything)
cold users (<5): 17.3% | cold items (<5): 90.0%
a random user's list would need 1200x the median item's data to be personalised at the tail
```

The second call keeps the same 1,200-item catalogue but throws away every interaction outside the
120 most popular items — the log a "head-only" product produces. Watch the numbers move the wrong
way: the Gini rises from 0.693 to 0.930 and the cold-item share goes from 19% to 90%, because the
1,080 remaining items now have no data at all. That is the feedback loop such a product creates:
it cannot learn about the tail because it never shows the tail, and the logs then prove the tail is
unpopular. Run this on your own logs and the gap between catalogue size and covered items tells you
how much of that loop you are in.

Notice also that the head-only log still has 38,745 interactions, over half of the full 75,955,
from only 10% of the items. Try a variation: change the 120 to 600 (the top half) and check how
close the Gini and the cold-item share come back to the full-log values.

```python
class InteractionData:
    def __init__(self, events, n_users, n_items, holdout_days=60):
        cut = events["ts"].max() - holdout_days
        self.train = events[events["ts"] <= cut]
        self.test = events[events["ts"] > cut]
        self.n_users, self.n_items = n_users, n_items
        self.M = to_csr(self.train, n_users, n_items)
        self.M.data[:] = 1.0
        self.seen = {u: set(g) for u, g in self.train.groupby("user")["item"]}
        self.truth = {u: set(g) for u, g in self.test.groupby("user")["item"]}
        self.pop = np.asarray(self.M.sum(axis=0)).ravel()

    def eval_recommender(self, recommend, k=10):
        hits = total = 0
        recommended = set()
        users = [u for u in self.truth if self.truth[u]]
        for u in users:
            rec = list(recommend(u, k))
            recommended.update(rec)
            hits += len(set(rec) & self.truth[u])
            total += min(k, len(self.truth[u]))
        return {"recall@k": hits / max(total, 1),
                "coverage": len(recommended) / self.n_items,
                "users_scored": len(users)}

data = InteractionData(events, n_users, n_items)
order = np.argsort(-data.pop)

def pop_rec(u, k):
    out = []
    for i in order:
        if i not in data.seen.get(u, ()):
            out.append(i)
            if len(out) == k:
                break
    return out

def rand_rec(u, k, r=np.random.default_rng(1)):
    return r.choice(data.n_items, size=k, replace=False)

print("TEMPORAL split (train on the past, predict the future)")
for name, fn in (("random", rand_rec), ("popularity", pop_rec)):
    print(f"  {name:11} {data.eval_recommender(fn)}")

# the same evaluation with a random split, for comparison
shuffled = events.sample(frac=1.0, random_state=0).reset_index(drop=True)
n_hold = len(data.test)
rand_split = shuffled.copy()
rand_split["ts"] = 0
rand_split.loc[rand_split.index[:n_hold], "ts"] = 999
data_rand = InteractionData(rand_split, n_users, n_items, holdout_days=1)
order_r = np.argsort(-data_rand.pop)

def pop_rec_r(u, k):
    out = []
    for i in order_r:
        if i not in data_rand.seen.get(u, ()):
            out.append(i)
            if len(out) == k:
                break
    return out

print("\nRANDOM split (leaks the future into the past)")
print(f"  popularity  {data_rand.eval_recommender(pop_rec_r)}")
print(f"\ntest interactions: temporal {len(data.test):,} | random {len(data_rand.test):,}")

def leak_surface(d):
    """Share of held-out (user, item) pairs whose ITEM the user also touched in train."""
    hit = tot = 0
    for u, items in d.truth.items():
        tot += len(items)
        hit += len(items & d.seen.get(u, set()))
    return hit / max(tot, 1)

print(f"\nheld-out pairs the model has already seen for that user:")
print(f"  temporal split {leak_surface(data):.4f} | random split {leak_surface(data_rand):.4f}")
```

```output
  random      {'recall@k': 0.01096553688407861, 'coverage': 1.0, 'users_scored': 2716}
  popularity  {'recall@k': 0.10666476787240102, 'coverage': 0.0225, 'users_scored': 2716}

RANDOM split (leaks the future into the past)
  popularity  {'recall@k': 0.12671012105150062, 'coverage': 0.0225, 'users_scored': 2710}

  temporal split 0.1561 | random split 0.1515
```

Both splits are scored on the same 17,107 held-out interactions, and the random split reports
0.1267 against the temporal split's 0.1067 — **19% higher, from a recommender with no
personalisation at all.**

The mechanism is worth spelling out, because it is not the one people usually name. A random split
puts rows from the *test period* into the training data, so the popularity vector it computes
already reflects which items are popular then — including the items that launched in month 9. The
temporal split has to extrapolate popularity forward across a catalogue that changed, and gets
punished for the items it cannot know about. No user-level memorisation is involved.

The last line adds the mechanism that *does* involve users: about 15% of held-out pairs are items
the user already touched in training, under either split. Popularity cannot exploit that, but the
neighbourhood and matrix-factorisation models from chapter 3 onward absolutely can. Use the
temporal split from the beginning — the future is what you are ranking for — rather than switching
once the models are good enough to cheat.

Two design decisions in `InteractionData` are worth keeping. It stores `seen` per user and every
recommender filters against it, because recommending an item the user has already interacted with
is free recall in an offline metric and a bug in a product. And `eval_recommender` returns
**coverage** alongside recall, because a recommender that shows the same ten items to everyone can
score respectably on recall while being useless — that is the accuracy-diversity tension chapter 2
puts numbers on.

Look at the coverage numbers to see that tension already: random lists cover 100% of the catalogue
and popularity covers 2.25%, about 27 items for 2,716 users.

One caveat this data layer cannot fix: the held-out interactions are the items the *previous*
system showed the user, so an offline metric rewards you for reproducing that system's choices.
An item the user would have loved but never saw counts as a miss. That is exposure bias, it is the
reason offline gains often fail to reproduce online, and the only real answers are randomised
exposure data or the bandit machinery in chapter 11.
