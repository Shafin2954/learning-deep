## Why cross-entropy is the right loss

A good model is one that would have bet heavily on what actually happened. Cross-entropy is
the score that rewards exactly that, and this chapter shows why it is not an arbitrary choice.

You've been minimizing cross-entropy. This chapter explains *why* that's correct and connects it
to the two numbers you'll stare at all month: **training loss** and **perplexity**. The punchline:
minimizing cross-entropy equals **maximizing the likelihood of the data**, and an LLM is just this
applied to "predict the next token," over and over.

The **training loss** is the average cross-entropy on the training data, printed at every step.
**Perplexity** is the same number converted into "how many options the model is torn between",
which is easier to read.

> **Picture it:** a weather forecaster who gives a probability for rain every day. At the end of
> the year you multiply together the probabilities she gave to what actually happened. A
> forecaster who said 90% on rainy days and 10% on dry days gets a large product; one who always
> says 50% gets a small one. Picking the best forecaster this way is maximum likelihood.

### From likelihood to cross-entropy

Start from "make the observed data as probable as possible" and two small algebra moves turn it
into the cross-entropy loss.

A loss function scores how wrong the model is. For predicting a category, the principled choice
comes from **maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)**: pick parameters that make the observed data
most probable. The probability the model assigns to all the true labels is

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \prod_i p_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)
$$

> **In plain words:**
> - $\theta$ ("theta") is every adjustable number in the model.
> - $p_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)$ is the probability the model gives to the true label $y_i$ when it
>   sees input $x_i$.
> - $\prod_i$ multiplies these together over all examples, treating them as independent.
> - $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$, the **likelihood**, is "how probable the whole dataset looks to this
>   model". MLE picks the $\theta$ that makes it largest.

Products of many small probabilities underflow and are awkward to differentiate, so take the log
(monotonic — same maximizer) and negate (so we *minimize*):

$$
-\log \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_i \log p_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)
$$

> **In plain words:** the log turns a product into a sum, and each true label contributes
> $-\log$ of its probability. Whatever $\theta$ maximized the product also minimizes this sum,
> because $\log$ never reverses an ordering.

**Underflow** is the opposite of overflow: a number so close to zero that the computer stores
it as exactly 0. One million probabilities of 0.5 multiply to $0.5^{10^6}$, which is 0 in
floating point, while their logs add to a perfectly ordinary $-693{,}147$.

That sum, averaged, *is* cross-entropy. So "minimize cross-entropy" and "maximize likelihood" are
the same instruction — and why F1's gradient $\mathrm{softmax} - \mathrm{onehot}$ keeps appearing:
it's the gradient of negative log-likelihood.

## A language model is next-token classification

An LLM plays one game, again and again: read the text so far, then guess the next word piece.
Each guess is an ordinary classification with a very large number of classes.

The whole idea of an LLM in one line: at each position, predict the next token as a classification
over the vocabulary. The classes are the ~30k–250k tokens; the label is the actual next token. The
loss is cross-entropy averaged over every position:

$$
L = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\big(\text{token}_t \mid \text{token}_1 \ldots \text{token}_{t-1}\big)
$$

> **In plain words:**
> - $T$ is the number of positions in the text.
> - $\text{token}_t$ is the true token at position $t$; the tokens before it are the context.
> - Each term is $-\log$ of the probability the model gave to the real next token.
> - $L$ is the average of those penalties over all positions.

A **token** is the unit the model reads and writes: a word, a piece of a word, or a punctuation
mark, depending on the **tokenizer** (the program that cuts text into tokens).

Pretraining minimizes exactly this over a huge corpus; fine-tuning (Bengali ch. 8–9) minimizes the
same loss on your task. Nothing exotic — F1's cross-entropy with the vocabulary as the class set
and a causal mask so position $t$ can't peek at the future (you build that mask in F10/F12).

A **causal mask** blocks each position from seeing later positions, so the model cannot cheat by
reading the answer it is meant to predict.

> **Picture it:** a fill-in-the-next-word quiz where the page below the current line is covered
> by a sheet of paper. Every line of a book is a separate quiz question, so one book gives you
> millions of free labelled examples.

## Perplexity is exp(loss)

Loss values like 1.54 are hard to feel. Raising $e$ to the loss turns it into a count: "the model
is as unsure as if it were choosing uniformly among this many tokens".

$$
\mathrm{perplexity} = e^{\,L}
$$

> **In plain words:** undo the logarithm. If the average penalty is $\ln 5$, perplexity is 5.

Interpretation: the model's **effective branching factor** — on average, how many tokens it's
choosing between. A model uniformly unsure among 5 tokens has cross-entropy $\ln 5$ and perplexity
5. A perfect model has perplexity 1. Lower is better.

> **Picture it:** a guessing game at a fork in a path. Perplexity 1 means there is only one road.
> Perplexity 5 means the model hesitates as much as someone facing five equally likely roads.

A caveat that bites in Bengali: perplexity depends on the **tokenizer** — different tokenizations
of the same text give different perplexities, so never compare perplexities across models with
different vocabularies. This ties straight to fertility (Bengali ch. 3).

> **Watch out:** a tokenizer that cuts a sentence into many short tokens makes each guess easier
> (lower per-token perplexity) while the model is no better at the sentence as a whole. Compare
> perplexities only between models that share a tokenizer, or convert to a per-character or
> per-word figure first. **Fertility** is the average number of tokens per word.

### Computing loss and perplexity in code

The block scores a 4-token sequence with random logits over a 6-token vocabulary, then checks
that a model with no opinion has perplexity equal to the vocabulary size.

```python
import numpy as np
def softmax(z):
    z = z - z.max(-1, keepdims=True); e = np.exp(z); return e / e.sum(-1, keepdims=True)

# A tiny "language model" scoring a 4-token sequence over a vocab of 6.
np.random.seed(0); vocab = 6
logits  = np.random.randn(4, vocab)        # one logit row per position
targets = np.array([2, 5, 0, 3])           # the actual next tokens

p   = softmax(logits)
nll = -np.log(p[np.arange(4), targets])    # per-position negative log-likelihood
loss = nll.mean(); ppl = np.exp(loss)
print("per-token NLL :", nll.round(3))
print("cross-entropy :", round(loss, 4))
print("perplexity    :", round(ppl, 3))

# Sanity: a perfectly uniform model has perplexity == vocab size.
uniform = np.zeros((4, vocab))
print("uniform ppl   :",
      round(np.exp(-np.log(softmax(uniform)[np.arange(4), targets]).mean()), 3),
      "(should equal vocab = 6)")
```

```output
per-token NLL : [2.289 0.97  1.681 1.24 ]
cross-entropy : 1.5448
perplexity    : 4.687
uniform ppl   : 6.0 (should equal vocab = 6)
```

Read it row by row. The per-token penalties are 2.289, 0.97, 1.681 and 1.24; the worst one,
position 0, is where the true token got the least probability (about $e^{-2.289} \approx 0.10$).
Their mean is the cross-entropy, 1.5448, and $e^{1.5448} = 4.687$ is the perplexity: these
random logits are as unsure as a choice among about 4.7 tokens. The uniform model gives exactly
6.0, matching the vocabulary size.

> **Watch out:** perplexity is $e$ to the *mean* loss, not the mean of per-token perplexities.
> Here $e$ to each token's loss would give values from about 2.6 to 9.9, whose plain average is
> not 4.687.

## Why this matters for the competition

The number your training script prints is this one loss, whatever the task looks like on the
surface. Knowing that tells you what training can and cannot fix.

Whether your task is classification, QA, or generation, the model under the hood is minimizing
token-level cross-entropy. Understanding that (1) demystifies the training curves, (2) tells you
"good loss" and "good task metric" can diverge (Bengali ch. 14 — you optimize loss but are *scored*
on F1/BLEU/EM), and (3) explains why label quality matters so much: MLE faithfully fits whatever
labels you give it, errors included.

**F1**, **BLEU** and **EM** (exact match) are task scores that compare the generated answer with
a reference. They are not differentiable, so you cannot train on them directly; you train on
cross-entropy and hope the two improve together.

> **Watch out:** a falling validation loss with a flat or falling task metric is a real
> situation, not a bug. The model may be getting better at the easy tokens that dominate the
> average while the few tokens that decide the answer do not improve.

## Recap in plain words

- Maximum likelihood means choosing the model that makes the observed data most probable.
- Taking the log and flipping the sign turns maximum likelihood into minimizing cross-entropy.
- An LLM is a next-token classifier, trained with cross-entropy averaged over every position.
- Perplexity is $e$ to the average loss: the effective number of tokens the model is torn
  between.
- A uniform model's perplexity equals the vocabulary size; a perfect model's is 1.
- Perplexities are only comparable between models with the same tokenizer.
- Training always lowers token-level cross-entropy, which can disagree with the task metric
  you are scored on.

## Try it Yourself

This loop makes the true token steadily more likely by raising its logit, and prints how the
cross-entropy and perplexity respond.

```python
import numpy as np
def softmax(z):
    z = z - z.max(-1, keepdims=True); e = np.exp(z); return e / e.sum(-1, keepdims=True)

# Watch perplexity drop as the model gets more confident in the RIGHT token.
vocab = 10; target = 3
for boost in [0, 1, 2, 4, 8]:
    logits = np.zeros(vocab); logits[target] = boost     # make the true token more likely
    p = softmax(logits); ce = -np.log(p[target])
    print(f"boost={boost}: p(true)={p[target]:.3f}  CE={ce:.3f}  ppl={np.exp(ce):.3f}")
```

```output
boost=0: p(true)=0.100  CE=2.303  ppl=10.000
boost=1: p(true)=0.232  CE=1.461  ppl=4.311
boost=2: p(true)=0.451  CE=0.797  ppl=2.218
boost=4: p(true)=0.858  CE=0.153  ppl=1.165
boost=8: p(true)=0.997  CE=0.003  ppl=1.003
```

At boost 0 the model is uniform (ppl 10); as confidence in the true token rises, perplexity falls
toward 1.

Follow the columns. At boost 0, $p(\text{true}) = 0.100$, the CE is $\ln 10 = 2.303$ and the
perplexity is 10.000, the vocabulary size. At boost 4 the true token has 0.858 and perplexity is
1.165. At boost 8 it has 0.997 and perplexity is 1.003, almost perfect. Notice the diminishing
returns: going from boost 4 to 8 lowers perplexity by only 0.162.

Try a variation: use negative boosts such as `-2` and `-8`. The true token becomes *less* likely
than the others, and perplexity climbs above 10, far beyond "uniformly unsure".

## Exercise

Given a model's predicted probability for the correct token at each of $T$ positions (a 1-D array
`p_true`), write `perplexity(p_true)` directly — no logits needed. Then explain in one line why you
must average the *log* probabilities, not the probabilities themselves.

> **Hint:** perplexity is $e$ to the average of $-\log p$. Try one very small probability, such
> as 0.001, in your test array and compare both averaging methods.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
def perplexity(p_true):
    return np.exp(-np.log(p_true).mean())

print(perplexity(np.array([0.5, 0.25, 0.1, 0.9])))
```

Perplexity is the **geometric** mean of the per-token branching factor. Averaging in log-space
gives the geometric mean of probabilities; one near-zero probability (a token the model thought
impossible but happened) correctly blows perplexity up — averaging raw probabilities would hide it.

The test prints about 3.071. If you instead took 1 over the plain average probability, you would
get $1/0.4375 \approx 2.286$, an over-optimistic score, because the plain average lets the
confident 0.9 cover up the poor 0.1. The **geometric mean** multiplies the values and takes the
$n$-th root, so a single tiny factor drags the whole result down.
</details>
