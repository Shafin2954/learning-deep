## Why the optimizer matters

Knowing which way is downhill is not the same as knowing how far to step. The optimizer and the
schedule are the rules for turning "which way" into an actual move, and they decide whether
training is fast, slow, or explodes.

Backprop (F2) gives you gradients. The *optimizer* decides how to turn gradients
into weight updates — and the **learning-rate schedule** decides how big those steps
are over time. When you fine-tune a Bengali LLM (Bengali ch. 9), almost every knob
you touch lives here: learning rate, warmup, the schedule, weight decay. Getting
these wrong is the #1 reason a fine-tune diverges or learns nothing. So understand
them, don't copy them blindly.

The **learning rate** is the step size: how far the weights move per update. A training run
**diverges** when the loss grows instead of shrinking, usually ending in `inf` or `nan`.

> **Picture it:** driving down a winding mountain road at night. The gradient is the direction
> your headlights show. The optimizer is how you steer (smoothly, or jerking the wheel at every
> bend). The schedule is your speed over the trip: slow while your eyes adjust, fastest on the
> open stretch, slow again as you pull into the parking spot.

## SGD and the ill-conditioning problem

The simplest rule takes a fixed-size step straight downhill. It breaks down when the valley is
steep in one direction and nearly flat in another, because one step size cannot suit both.

Plain SGD takes a step opposite the gradient:

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta \cdot g
$$

> **In plain words:** new weight = old weight minus (learning rate $\eta$) times (gradient $g$).
> **SGD** (stochastic gradient descent) is this rule applied with gradients computed on small
> random batches.

The catch: the largest safe $\eta$ is bounded by the *steepest* direction of the loss.
But deep networks are wildly **ill-conditioned** — some directions are steep, others
nearly flat. A single $\eta$ that's safe for the steep directions is painfully slow in
the flat ones. (Verified below: on a problem with curvatures 1 and 200, SGD crawls
while adaptive methods finish.)

**Curvature** is how fast the slope itself changes along a direction: a narrow canyon wall has
high curvature, a broad plain has low curvature. The **condition number** is the ratio of the
steepest to the flattest curvature, 200 in the example below. On a bowl with curvature $c$, a
step is only stable while $\eta < 2/c$, so curvature 200 caps $\eta$ below 0.01 for *every*
direction.

> **Picture it:** a long, narrow canyon. A big step across the narrow way slams you into the
> opposite wall. A step small enough to avoid that moves you along the canyon floor at a crawl.

## Momentum: accumulate velocity

Momentum lets the weights pick up speed in directions where the gradient keeps agreeing with
itself, like a ball rolling downhill.

Momentum keeps a velocity vector $v$ and updates:

$$
v \leftarrow \beta v - \eta g, \qquad w \leftarrow w + v \qquad (\beta \approx 0.9)
$$

> **In plain words:** the new velocity keeps 90% of the old velocity ($\beta = 0.9$) and adds
> the new downhill push. Then the weights move by the velocity. A steady push in one direction
> keeps adding up, to about $1/(1 - 0.9) = 10$ times a single step.

Momentum averages recent gradients, so directions where the gradient consistently
points the same way build up speed, while oscillating directions cancel out. It's a
big, cheap win over SGD on ill-conditioned losses — exactly the regime nets live in.

> **Picture it:** a heavy ball rolling along the canyon. Pushes from the left and right walls
> alternate and mostly cancel, while the gentle slope along the floor pushes the same way every
> time, so the ball keeps accelerating down the canyon.

## Adam: a per-parameter learning rate

Adam gives every weight its own step size. Weights whose gradients have been big get smaller
steps, and weights whose gradients have been tiny get bigger ones, so every direction moves at a
similar pace.

Adam keeps a running mean of gradients ($m$, like momentum) *and* a running mean of
squared gradients ($v$), then scales each parameter's step by $1/\sqrt{v}$. Every
parameter gets its own effective learning rate — large where gradients have been
small, small where they've been large.

A **running mean** (here an **exponential moving average**) is an average that gives the most
weight to recent values and lets old values fade away by a constant factor at each step.

### Adam update rule

Four lines, applied element by element to every weight.

$$
m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1 - \beta_1) g \qquad (\beta_1 \approx 0.9)
$$

> **In plain words:** $m$ is the running average of the gradient, its typical direction. It
> keeps 90% of its old value and mixes in 10% of the new gradient.

$$
v \leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1 - \beta_2) g^2 \qquad (\beta_2 \approx 0.999)
$$

> **In plain words:** $v$ is the running average of the *squared* gradient, its typical size.
> It forgets much more slowly: 99.9% old, 0.1% new.

$$
\hat{m} = \frac{m}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \qquad \hat{v} = \frac{v}{1 - \beta_2^t} \qquad \text{(bias correction)}
$$

> **In plain words:** both averages start at 0, so early on they are too small. Dividing by
> $1 - \beta^t$ fixes that. At step $t = 1$ with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $m$ is divided by $0.1$, which
> recovers the gradient itself. As $t$ grows, $\beta^t \to 0$ and the correction fades away.

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta \cdot \frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \varepsilon}
$$

> **In plain words:** step by the learning rate times "typical direction divided by typical
> size". The ratio is about $\pm 1$ when gradients are steady, whatever their raw scale, so
> each weight moves roughly $\eta$ per step. The tiny $\varepsilon$ only prevents division by 0.

This adaptivity is why Adam (and its variant **AdamW**, which decouples weight decay)
is the **default optimizer for transformers**: it's robust to the per-parameter scale
differences that are everywhere in attention blocks, and it needs far less
learning-rate babysitting than SGD. Use **AdamW** in practice. For LLM fine-tuning,
typical $\eta$ is small: `1e-4` to `2e-5` for full/LoRA fine-tuning (Bengali ch. 9
gives concrete numbers).

**Weight decay** shrinks every weight a little at each step, pulling the model toward smaller
weights (F6). **LoRA** is a fine-tuning method that trains small add-on matrices instead of the
full weights (Bengali ch. 9).

The block below races the three optimizers on the canyon from the previous section: curvature 1
in one direction, 200 in the other, starting from $(5, 5)$, for 300 steps each.

```python
import numpy as np

# Ill-conditioned quadratic: curvatures 1 (flat) and 200 (steep). Condition # = 200.
a, b = 1.0, 200.0
def grad(w): return np.array([a*w[0], b*w[1]])
def loss(w): return 0.5*(a*w[0]**2 + b*w[1]**2)

def optimize(kind, steps=300, lr=0.009):
    w = np.array([5.0, 5.0]); v = np.zeros(2); m = np.zeros(2); s = np.zeros(2)
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        g = grad(w)
        if kind == "sgd":
            w = w - lr * g
        elif kind == "momentum":
            v = 0.9 * v - lr * g; w = w + v
        elif kind == "adam":
            m = 0.9*m + 0.1*g; s = 0.999*s + 0.001*g*g
            mh = m/(1 - 0.9**t); sh = s/(1 - 0.999**t)
            w = w - 0.1 * mh / (np.sqrt(sh) + 1e-8)
    return loss(w)

for k in ["sgd", "momentum", "adam"]:
    print(f"{k:9s} final loss: {optimize(k):.3e}")
# sgd ~5.5e-2 (stuck, lr capped by the steep direction);
# momentum ~5.6e-11, adam ~3.4e-10  -> both crush SGD.
```

```output
sgd       final loss: 5.509e-02
momentum  final loss: 5.604e-11
adam      final loss: 3.370e-10
```

SGD stays stuck at $5 \times 10^{-2}$; both momentum and Adam reach near-zero. On a
real transformer, where condition numbers are far worse, this gap is what separates
"trains" from "crawls."

Look at the exponents. SGD ends at $5.5 \times 10^{-2}$, momentum at $5.6 \times 10^{-11}$, and
Adam at $3.4 \times 10^{-10}$: both are at least a hundred million times lower than SGD. SGD's
learning rate of 0.009 sits just under the stability limit $2/200 = 0.01$ for the steep
direction, so in the flat direction each step only shrinks $w_0$ by a factor of $0.991$. After
300 steps $w_0$ is still about $5 \times 0.991^{300} \approx 0.33$, which is exactly the leftover
loss $0.5 \times 0.33^2 \approx 0.055$.

> **Watch out:** this race is not perfectly fair. SGD and momentum use $\eta = 0.009$, while
> Adam uses a step of 0.1 (hard-coded in the `adam` branch). That is normal: Adam's step is
> measured in "units per step" rather than "units per gradient", so its good learning rates
> live on a different scale. Never copy a learning rate from SGD to Adam or back.

## Warmup: don't take big steps before the optimizer knows the landscape

At the very start of training, Adam's averages hold almost no history, so its steps are based
on guesswork. Warmup starts with tiny steps and grows them, so no early guess can do much damage.

At step 0, Adam's running averages ($m$, $v$) are basically empty, so its step
directions are unreliable — and transformer activations are fragile early in
training. Taking a full-size step now can spike the loss to NaN. **Warmup** ramps
$\eta$ linearly from 0 to its target over the first few hundred/thousand steps. For
transformers this is not optional folklore; skipping it is a classic way to make
fine-tuning diverge.

**Activations** are the intermediate values computed inside the network during the forward
pass. **NaN** means "not a number", the value that appears after an overflow and then spreads
to everything it touches.

> **Picture it:** stepping onto a frozen lake. You test the ice with small steps first and only
> stride out once you know it holds. The optimizer's statistics are your knowledge of the ice.

## Linear warmup → cosine decay schedule

The full recipe is a ramp up, then a smooth slide down to almost zero by the end of training.

After warmup, you *decay* the learning rate so the model can settle into a good
minimum instead of bouncing around it. The standard recipe is **linear warmup →
cosine decay**: ramp up, then ease down following a half-cosine to $\approx 0$.

$$
\eta(t) = \begin{cases}
\eta_{\max} \cdot \dfrac{t}{T_{\text{warmup}}} & t < T_{\text{warmup}} \\[6pt]
\dfrac{\eta_{\max}}{2}\!\left(1 + \cos\!\left(\pi \cdot \dfrac{t - T_{\text{warmup}}}{T_{\text{total}} - T_{\text{warmup}}}\right)\right) & t \ge T_{\text{warmup}}
\end{cases}
$$

> **In plain words:**
> - $t$ is the current step, $T_{\text{warmup}}$ the length of the ramp, and $T_{\text{total}}$
>   the total number of steps.
> - During warmup the rate grows in a straight line from 0 to $\eta_{\max}$.
> - After warmup, the fraction inside the cosine goes from 0 to 1, so the cosine goes from 1
>   to $-1$, and the rate slides smoothly from $\eta_{\max}$ down to 0.

```python
import numpy as np
# Linear warmup -> cosine decay schedule (what you'll actually use).
def lr_at(step, base_lr=2e-4, warmup=100, total=1000):
    if step < warmup:
        return base_lr * step / warmup                     # ramp up
    p = (step - warmup) / (total - warmup)                 # 0 -> 1
    return 0.5 * base_lr * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * p))         # cosine to ~0

for s in [0, 50, 100, 300, 600, 1000]:
    print(f"step {s:4d}: lr = {lr_at(s):.2e}")
```

```output
step    0: lr = 0.00e+00
step   50: lr = 1.00e-04
step  100: lr = 2.00e-04
step  300: lr = 1.77e-04
step  600: lr = 8.26e-05
step 1000: lr = 0.00e+00
```

Check the landmarks. Step 0 gives 0, step 50 (halfway through warmup) gives exactly half the
peak, $1.00 \times 10^{-4}$, and step 100 hits the peak $2.00 \times 10^{-4}$. Then the decay is
gentle at first (still $1.77 \times 10^{-4}$ at step 300), fastest in the middle ($8.26 \times
10^{-5}$ at step 600), and gentle again as it reaches 0 at step 1000.

**Mental model:** optimizer = *how* to step (direction/scale per parameter);
schedule = *how big* to step over time (warmup up, then decay down). Both matter;
neither rescues a broken loss or bad data.

> **Watch out:** the schedule's `total` must match the real number of training steps. If you
> set `total=1000` but train for 3,000 steps, the formula's cosine starts rising again after
> step 1000 and the learning rate climbs back up. Libraries usually clamp or warn, but check.

## Recap in plain words

- The optimizer turns gradients into weight updates; the schedule sets the step size over time.
- Plain SGD uses one step size for every direction, so a steep direction forces slow progress in
  every flat one.
- Momentum builds up speed in directions where the gradient keeps agreeing.
- Adam divides each weight's step by the typical size of its gradient, giving every weight its
  own learning rate.
- AdamW is Adam with weight decay applied directly to the weights; it is the default for
  transformers.
- Warmup ramps the learning rate up from 0 so early, poorly informed steps stay small.
- Cosine decay then eases the rate down to 0 so the model settles into a minimum.

## Try it Yourself

This draws the warmup-plus-cosine schedule as a text bar chart, one row every 100 steps.

```python
import numpy as np
def lr_at(step, base_lr=2e-4, warmup=100, total=1000):
    if step < warmup: return base_lr * step / warmup
    p = (step - warmup) / (total - warmup)
    return 0.5 * base_lr * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * p))

# Plot the schedule as text. Notice: 0 -> peak at warmup -> smooth down to ~0.
for s in range(0, 1001, 100):
    bar = "█" * int(lr_at(s) / 2e-4 * 30)
    print(f"{s:4d} |{bar}")
# Change warmup to 0 and imagine taking a full step at t=1 with empty Adam stats.
# That's how fine-tunes go to NaN.
```

The bars grow from nothing at step 0 to a full 30 blocks at step 100, then shrink: 26 blocks at

## Exercise

Implement an `AdamW` step in NumPy: standard Adam, but apply weight decay *directly
to the weights* (`w ← w − lr·wd·w`) rather than adding it to the gradient. Use it to
fit the logistic-regression weights for a small dataset. (Decoupled weight decay is
the "W" in AdamW and the correct way to regularize transformers.)

> **Hint:** start from the four Adam lines above. The only change is one extra term in the
> final update, `self.wd * w`, which uses the weights, not the gradient.

<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
class AdamW:
    def __init__(self, shape, lr=1e-3, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8, wd=0.01):
        self.lr, self.b1, self.b2, self.eps, self.wd = lr, b1, b2, eps, wd
        self.m = np.zeros(shape); self.v = np.zeros(shape); self.t = 0
    def step(self, w, g):
        self.t += 1
        self.m = self.b1*self.m + (1-self.b1)*g
        self.v = self.b2*self.v + (1-self.b2)*g*g
        mh = self.m/(1-self.b1**self.t); vh = self.v/(1-self.b2**self.t)
        w = w - self.lr*(mh/(np.sqrt(vh)+self.eps) + self.wd*w)   # decoupled decay
        return w
```

Key line: weight decay multiplies `w`, not `g`. Coupling it into the gradient (plain
"L2") interacts badly with Adam's per-parameter scaling — decoupling fixes it.

The problem with the coupled version: an L2 term added to the gradient is then divided by
$\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ like everything else, so weights with large gradients get almost no decay and
weights with small gradients get a lot. Decoupling gives every weight the same shrink rate.

Using it on a logistic regression with 200 points:

```python
np.random.seed(0)
X = np.random.randn(200, 2)
y = (X @ np.array([2.0, -3.0]) + 0.5 * np.random.randn(200) > 0).astype(float)
w = np.zeros(2); opt = AdamW(2, lr=0.05, wd=0.01)
for _ in range(500):
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w)))
    w = opt.step(w, X.T @ (p - y) / len(y))      # gradient of mean log-loss
print("weights:", w.round(2), " accuracy:", ((X @ w > 0) == y).mean())
```

This prints weights of about $(4.6, -6.12)$ and a training accuracy of 0.955. The weights are
larger than the $(2, -3)$ used to make the labels because logistic regression keeps sharpening
its probabilities on nearly separable data; what matters is the direction, and the ratio
$-6.12 / 4.6 \approx -1.33$ is close to the true $-1.5$.
</details>
