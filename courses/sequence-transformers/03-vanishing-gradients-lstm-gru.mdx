## The vanishing gradient problem

To learn from something that happened 100 steps ago, the error signal has to travel back
through 100 steps. In a plain RNN, each step multiplies the signal by a number-like factor.
Multiply by 0.9 a hundred times and almost nothing is left.

In a vanilla RNN, the gradient of the loss with respect to $h_0$ passes through $T$ Jacobians:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_0} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_T} \cdot \prod_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}
$$

> **In plain words:** how much the loss cares about the very first memory = how much it cares
> about the last memory, times a chain of $T$ "step-to-step sensitivity" matrices, one per
> step. The big $\prod$ means "multiply all of these together".

Each factor is $W_{hh}^T \cdot \text{diag}(1 - h_t^2)$. When the singular values of $W_{hh}$ are less than 1, this product shrinks exponentially — gradients reaching early timesteps are effectively zero. The model cannot learn long-range dependencies.

A **singular value** measures how much a matrix can stretch a vector in some direction. All
below 1 means every multiply shrinks the signal. A **vanilla RNN** is the plain RNN of ch. 2,
with no gates. The tanh factor $1 - h_t^2$ is at most 1, so it can only make things smaller.

> **Picture it:** a game of telephone through 100 people, where each person whispers a bit
> more quietly than they heard. By person 100 the message is silence. That silence is the
> vanishing gradient: the first player never learns what went wrong.

**Exploding gradients** are the opposite: singular values greater than 1 cause exponential growth. The fix is gradient clipping: $g \leftarrow g \cdot \min(1, \text{threshold} / \|g\|)$. This is cheap and effective.

> **In plain words:** if the gradient's length $\|g\|$ is above the threshold, scale it down
> so its length equals the threshold; otherwise leave it alone. The direction never changes.

> **Watch out:** clipping fixes explosion, not vanishing. You cannot scale a gradient of
> $10^{-30}$ back up into something useful — the information is gone. Vanishing needs a change
> of architecture, which is what LSTM and GRU are.

## Demonstrating gradient decay

You can watch the signal fade by pushing a vector backward through the same random matrix
and tanh factor many times, and measuring its length after each step.

```python
import numpy as np

def grad_norm_through_tanh(T, W_scale=0.9, H=8, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    Whh = rng.standard_normal((H, H)) * W_scale / np.sqrt(H)
    h = np.ones(H)  # arbitrary starting gradient
    norms = [np.linalg.norm(h)]
    for _ in range(T):
        dtanh = 1 - np.tanh(h)**2  # ≤ 1 element-wise
        h = Whh.T @ (dtanh * h)
        norms.append(np.linalg.norm(h))
    return norms

norms_20  = grad_norm_through_tanh(T=20,  W_scale=0.9)
norms_100 = grad_norm_through_tanh(T=100, W_scale=0.9)

print(f"Gradient norm after  20 steps: {norms_20[-1]:.2e}")
print(f"Gradient norm after 100 steps: {norms_100[-1]:.2e}")
print(f"Gradient norm after   1 steps: {norms_20[1]:.4f}")
```

```output
Gradient norm after  20 steps: 2.95e-02
Gradient norm after 100 steps: 1.11e-05
Gradient norm after   1 steps: 0.8509
```

The starting vector of eight 1s has length $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$. After 1 step it is
0.8509; after 20 steps 0.0295; after 100 steps 0.0000111 — about 250,000 times smaller than
at the start. Each extra step multiplies the signal by roughly the same factor below 1, which
is what "exponential decay" means.

After 100 steps the gradient is effectively zero — the model is blind to events 100+ tokens ago.

## LSTM: the fix

The LSTM adds a second memory lane — the **cell state** — where information is carried
forward by adding and scaling, not by passing through a full matrix multiply every step.
Small learned "valves" called gates decide what to keep, what to write, and what to show.

The Long Short-Term Memory (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) introduces a **cell state** $c_t$ that flows through time with only elementwise operations (addition, multiplication). This creates a gradient highway that doesn't suffer the multiplicative decay.

A **gate** is a vector of numbers between 0 and 1 produced by a sigmoid $\sigma$; multiplying
by it lets through a fraction of each entry (0 = block, 1 = pass). **Elementwise** ($\odot$)
means entry 1 times entry 1, entry 2 times entry 2, and so on.

The gate equations, with $[h_{t-1}, x_t]$ denoting concatenation:

$$
f_t = \sigma(W_f [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f) \quad \text{(forget gate)}
$$
$$
i_t = \sigma(W_i [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i) \quad \text{(input gate)}
$$
$$
\tilde{c}_t = \tanh(W_c [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c) \quad \text{(candidate)}
$$
$$
c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t \quad \text{(cell update)}
$$
$$
o_t = \sigma(W_o [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o) \quad \text{(output gate)}
$$
$$
h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t)
$$

Read them top to bottom:

- $f_t$ — **forget gate**: how much of each old cell entry to keep.
- $i_t$ — **input gate**: how much of the new candidate to write.
- $\tilde{c}_t$ — **candidate**: the new information on offer, in $(-1, 1)$.
- $c_t$ — the new cell state: kept old part plus written new part.
- $o_t$ — **output gate**: how much of the (squashed) cell to reveal as $h_t$.
- $h_t$ — the hidden state the rest of the network sees.

> **Picture it:** the cell state is a conveyor belt running through a factory. At each
> station a worker can remove some items (forget gate), add new ones (input gate), and show a
> photo of the belt to the next department (output gate). Items nobody touches ride along
> untouched for as long as the belt runs.

The cell update $c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t$ is the key: gradients flow through addition (not matrix multiply) when the forget gate $f_t \approx 1$.

### Why the addition helps

Along the cell lane, $\partial c_t / \partial c_{t-1} = \text{diag}(f_t)$ (ignoring the
indirect path through the gates). Over $T$ steps the gradient is multiplied by the forget
gates only, entry by entry — no $W_{hh}$ and no tanh factor. With $f_t = 1$ it passes
through unchanged; with $f_t = 0.88$ it decays, but much more slowly, and the network can
learn to push $f_t$ toward 1 for things worth remembering.

## Implementing an LSTM cell from scratch

All four gate blocks can be computed in one matrix multiply: stack the four weight matrices
side by side, multiply once, then slice the result into four parts.

```python
import numpy as np

class LSTMCell:
    def __init__(self, input_size, hidden_size, seed=0):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        # Combined weight for [h, x] -> [f, i, g, o]
        d = input_size + hidden_size
        self.W = rng.standard_normal((d, 4 * hidden_size)) * 0.01
        self.b = np.zeros(4 * hidden_size)
        self.H = hidden_size

    def forward(self, x, h_prev, c_prev):
        combined = np.concatenate([h_prev, x])       # (d,)
        gates = combined @ self.W + self.b            # (4H,)
        H = self.H
        f = self._sigmoid(gates[:H])                 # forget
        i = self._sigmoid(gates[H:2*H])              # input
        g = np.tanh(gates[2*H:3*H])                  # candidate
        o = self._sigmoid(gates[3*H:])               # output
        c = f * c_prev + i * g
        h = o * np.tanh(c)
        return h, c

    @staticmethod
    def _sigmoid(x):
        return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(x, -30, 30)))

cell = LSTMCell(input_size=4, hidden_size=8)
x   = np.random.default_rng(1).standard_normal(4)
h   = np.zeros(8)
c   = np.zeros(8)

for _ in range(10):
    h, c = cell.forward(x, h, c)

print("h shape:", h.shape, "  c shape:", c.shape)
print("h norm:", np.linalg.norm(h).round(5))
print("c norm:", np.linalg.norm(c).round(5))
```

```output
h shape: (8,)   c shape: (8,)
h norm: 0.01726
c norm: 0.03458
```

Both state vectors are 8 numbers long, as asked. The cell norm (0.03458) is about twice the
hidden norm (0.01726): $h$ is the cell squashed by tanh and then scaled by an output gate near
$\sigma(0) = 0.5$, because with weights of scale 0.01 every gate starts out close to 0.5.

> **Watch out:** the slicing order `[f, i, g, o]` is a convention of this code. PyTorch's
> `nn.LSTM` stores gates as `[i, f, g, o]`. Mixing them up when loading weights or setting
> the forget-gate bias gives a silently broken model.

## GRU: simpler alternative

The GRU folds the LSTM's two memory lanes back into one, and uses two gates instead of
three. It does nearly the same job with less machinery.

The Gated Recurrent Unit (Cho et al., 2014) merges forget and input into one **update gate** and uses a **reset gate** to expose the previous state selectively. Two gates instead of three (plus a candidate in each), fewer parameters.

$$
r_t = \sigma(W_r [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_r) \quad \text{(reset gate)}
$$
$$
z_t = \sigma(W_z [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_z) \quad \text{(update gate)}
$$
$$
\tilde{h}_t = \tanh(W_h [r_t \odot h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_h) \quad \text{(candidate)}
$$
$$
h_t = (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t
$$

- $r_t$ — **reset gate**: how much of the old state to use when proposing the candidate.
- $z_t$ — **update gate**: how far to move from the old state toward the candidate.
- $\tilde{h}_t$ — the candidate new state.
- $h_t$ — a blend: $(1 - z_t)$ parts old, $z_t$ parts new.

> **In plain words:** the last line is a slider. $z_t = 0$ keeps the old state exactly;
> $z_t = 1$ replaces it with the candidate; $z_t = 0.3$ moves 30% of the way.

When $z_t \approx 0$ the state is unchanged (like a large forget gate). When $z_t \approx 1$ the new candidate replaces it. The reset gate lets the GRU ignore the past when computing the candidate.

```python
class GRUCell:
    def __init__(self, input_size, hidden_size, seed=1):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        d = input_size + hidden_size
        self.Wr = rng.standard_normal((d, hidden_size)) * 0.01
        self.Wz = rng.standard_normal((d, hidden_size)) * 0.01
        self.Wh = rng.standard_normal((d, hidden_size)) * 0.01
        self.br = np.zeros(hidden_size)
        self.bz = np.zeros(hidden_size)
        self.bh = np.zeros(hidden_size)

    @staticmethod
    def _sigmoid(x):
        return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(x, -30, 30)))

    def forward(self, x, h_prev):
        combined = np.concatenate([h_prev, x])
        r = self._sigmoid(combined @ self.Wr + self.br)
        z = self._sigmoid(combined @ self.Wz + self.bz)
        combined_r = np.concatenate([r * h_prev, x])
        h_hat = np.tanh(combined_r @ self.Wh + self.bh)
        h = (1 - z) * h_prev + z * h_hat
        return h

gru = GRUCell(input_size=4, hidden_size=8)
h   = np.zeros(8)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for _ in range(10):
    h = gru.forward(rng.standard_normal(4), h)
print("GRU h norm after 10 steps:", np.linalg.norm(h).round(5))
```

```output
GRU h norm after 10 steps: 0.02528
```

After 10 random inputs, the state has norm 0.02528 — small, as with the LSTM, because tiny
weights give tiny candidates and an update gate near 0.5. This cell reuses the `np` import
from the LSTM block above, since all blocks on the page share one kernel.

## LSTM vs GRU: when to choose

Pick by size and data: the GRU is smaller and quicker, the LSTM has an extra memory lane that
sometimes helps on very long sequences. On most tasks the difference is small.

**GRU** has $\frac{3}{4}$ the parameters of LSTM, trains faster, and often matches LSTM on short-to-medium sequences. On tasks where capturing very long-range dependencies is critical (e.g., music generation over thousands of steps), LSTM has a slight edge in practice.

The $\frac{3}{4}$ comes from counting weight blocks: the LSTM has four (three gates and a
candidate), the GRU three (two gates and a candidate). For input size 4 and hidden size 8,
PyTorch's `nn.LSTM` has 448 parameters and `nn.GRU` has 336.

Both are largely superseded by attention-based models for tasks where parallelism matters — but they remain competitive on small datasets and streaming scenarios.

**Streaming** means processing data as it arrives, one step at a time — exactly what an RNN's
step-by-step update is built for.

## Recap in plain words

- Plain RNN gradients are multiplied by a factor below 1 at every step, so they fade
  exponentially with distance.
- Exploding gradients are fixed by clipping; vanishing gradients need a new architecture.
- The LSTM adds a cell state updated by addition, guarded by forget, input, and output gates.
- With the forget gate near 1, gradients travel along the cell state with little decay.
- The GRU merges this into one state with an update and a reset gate, using 3/4 of the
  parameters.
- Both remain useful for small data and streaming, but attention replaced them at scale.

## Try it Yourself

```python
import numpy as np

class LSTMCell:
    def __init__(self, input_size, hidden_size, seed=0):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        d = input_size + hidden_size
        self.W = rng.standard_normal((d, 4 * hidden_size)) * 0.01
        self.b = np.zeros(4 * hidden_size)
        self.H = hidden_size
    @staticmethod
    def _sigmoid(x):
        return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(x, -30, 30)))
    def forward(self, x, h_prev, c_prev):
        combined = np.concatenate([h_prev, x])
        gates = combined @ self.W + self.b
        H = self.H
        f = self._sigmoid(gates[:H])
        i = self._sigmoid(gates[H:2*H])
        g = np.tanh(gates[2*H:3*H])
        o = self._sigmoid(gates[3*H:])
        c = f * c_prev + i * g
        h = o * np.tanh(c)
        return h, c

# Compare gradient norms: vanilla RNN vs LSTM cell state
T = 50
# Vanilla: repeated Whh multiplication
Whh = np.random.default_rng(2).standard_normal((8, 8)) * 0.1
g = np.ones(8)
for _ in range(T):
    g = Whh.T @ ((1 - np.tanh(g)**2) * g)
print(f"Vanilla RNN gradient norm after {T} steps: {np.linalg.norm(g):.2e}")

# LSTM cell state gradient (forget gate near 1.0 → additive flow)
cell = LSTMCell(4, 8, seed=0)
# Initialize forget gate bias to +2 → forget gate ≈ sigmoid(2) ≈ 0.88
cell.b[:8] += 2.0
h, c = np.zeros(8), np.zeros(8)
c_grad = np.ones(8)
for _ in range(T):
    h, c = cell.forward(np.zeros(4), h, c)
# Approximate: gradient through c via forget gates
f_approx = cell._sigmoid(cell.b[:8])
c_grad_after = c_grad * f_approx ** T
print(f"LSTM cell gradient approx after {T} steps (f≈{f_approx.mean():.2f}): {np.linalg.norm(c_grad_after):.4f}")
```

```output
Vanilla RNN gradient norm after 50 steps: 6.89e-32
LSTM cell gradient approx after 50 steps (f≈0.88): 0.0050
```

Compare the two numbers: the plain RNN's gradient has shrunk to $6.89 \times 10^{-32}$,
while the LSTM cell path keeps 0.0050 — about $10^{29}$ times more signal. The LSTM figure is
the vector of eight 1s times $0.88^{50} \approx 0.0017$ per entry, so its norm is about
$0.0017 \times \sqrt{8}$. Try `cell.b[:8] += 5.0` instead: $\sigma(5) \approx 0.993$, and
$0.993^{50} \approx 0.7$, so most of the gradient survives 50 steps. That is why practitioners
initialise the forget-gate bias to a positive value.

import numpy as np

class GRUCell:
    def __init__(self, input_size, hidden_size, seed=1):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        d = input_size + hidden_size
        self.Wr = rng.standard_normal((d, hidden_size)) * 0.01
        self.Wz = rng.standard_normal((d, hidden_size)) * 0.01
        self.Wh = rng.standard_normal((d, hidden_size)) * 0.01
        self.br = np.zeros(hidden_size)
        self.bz = np.full(hidden_size, -2.0)  # forget bias
        self.bh = np.zeros(hidden_size)

    @staticmethod
    def _sigmoid(x):
        return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(x, -30, 30)))

    def forward(self, x, h_prev):
        combined = np.concatenate([h_prev, x])
        r = self._sigmoid(combined @ self.Wr + self.br)
        z = self._sigmoid(combined @ self.Wz + self.bz)
        combined_r = np.concatenate([r * h_prev, x])
        h_hat = np.tanh(combined_r @ self.Wh + self.bh)
        h = (1 - z) * h_prev + z * h_hat
        return h, z.mean()

gru = GRUCell(4, 8)
h = np.ones(8) * 0.5
x = np.ones(4)
for step in [0, 10, 25, 49]:
    for _ in range(1 if step == 0 else (step - prev_step if step > 0 else 0)):
        h, z_mean = gru.forward(x, h)
    prev_step = step
    print(f"Step {step:2d}: h_norm={np.linalg.norm(h):.4f}, z_mean={z_mean:.4f}")

With $b_z = -2$, the update gate $z \approx 0.12$, so $h$ barely moves each step — it "remembers" the initial state.

Running it gives `z_mean` of about 0.119 at every checkpoint, as planned. But look at the norms:
1.2416 after the first step, 0.3294 at step 10, 0.0503 at step 25, 0.0484 at step 49. Each step

initial state is gone and $h$ settles near the small candidate value. "Changes slowly" is true
per step, not over 50 steps. To truly hold a memory for 50 steps you need $z$ much closer to 0,
for example $b_z = -5$ ($z \approx 0.007$, and $0.993^{50} \approx 0.7$).
